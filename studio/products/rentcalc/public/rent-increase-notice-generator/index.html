How much notice a rent increase needs

A landlord cannot change the rent of a month-to-month tenancy overnight. In most states the increase is treated as a change of terms that takes the same notice as ending the tenancy, and a handful of states have written a specific rent increase rule on top. California requires 30 days, or 90 days when the increase, together with any others in the previous twelve months, is more than 10 percent. New York requires 30, 60 or 90 days by how long the tenant has been there whenever the increase is 5 percent or more. Washington requires 90 days under its 2025 law, which also caps most annual increases. Maryland requires written notice by mail with a certificate of mailing or by email, 60 days ahead for a tenancy of a month. Colorado requires 60 days where there is no written agreement. Virginia makes the new rent effective on the next rent due date coming 30 days after the notice. New Jersey requires a valid notice to quit with notice of the increase, one month for a monthly tenancy, and the increase must not be unconscionable. Where the state has no specific rule, this tool uses the notice the landlord would need to end the tenancy, which is the safe reading and what most leases say.

Effective date = the first rent due date on or after (notice date + the required notice)

Why the effective date is a rent day

Rent for a monthly tenancy is paid by the period, so an increase takes effect from the start of a period: the first rent due date that falls on or after the day the notice runs out. Giving notice on the 27th with rent due on the 1st and a 30-day rule therefore produces an effective date of the 1st of the month after next, not the 27th of next month. The letter the tool writes states that date, the old and new rent, the percentage, and the section of the statute the notice period comes from.

What the letter does not do

It does not override a lease. A fixed-term lease fixes the rent until it ends unless it says otherwise, and a month-to-month agreement may require longer notice than the statute, in which case the lease controls. It does not apply rent control or stabilization, local ordinances such as Seattle’s 180-day notice or Chicago’s, or the limits on subsidized housing, and it does not advise on whether an increase is reasonable; New Jersey courts can refuse an unconscionable one and Washington caps most increases. For a tenant who objects, the usual answer is that the tenancy may be ended on the ordinary notice instead.

Delivering it

Hand delivery with a signed acknowledgment or certified mail is enough everywhere in the list; Maryland specifies first-class mail with a certificate of mailing or email where the tenant has opted in, and California adds extra days for service by mail. Date the notice on the day it is actually delivered or posted, sign it, and keep a copy with the proof of delivery, because a short or unprovable notice simply pushes the increase to the next period.

Questions people ask

Can I raise the rent mid-lease?

Not during a fixed term unless the lease allows it. The notice periods here apply to month-to-month tenancies and to the start of a new term after a fixed term ends.

How often can I raise the rent?

Most states set no limit on frequency for an unregulated tenancy, only notice. Washington caps the annual amount under its 2025 law, California counts all increases in a twelve-month window toward the 10 percent trigger, and rent-controlled cities set their own rules.

The tenant refuses to pay the new rent. What then?

If the notice was valid and ran its full period, the new rent is the rent, and non-payment is treated like any other non-payment. If the notice was short, serve it again. In New Jersey the increase must also be reasonable, and the tenant can contest it in the possession case.

Do I need a reason to raise the rent?

No state in the list requires a reason for an increase, though New Jersey requires it to be not unconscionable and Washington caps the amount. A landlord may not raise the rent in retaliation for a complaint or as discrimination, which every state forbids separately.
