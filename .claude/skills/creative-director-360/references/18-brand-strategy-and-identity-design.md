## 1. Brand strategy foundations

Strategy is the part of the work the client cannot see and the part that decides whether the visible work survives contact with a board, a competitor and a decade. Every identity decision in sections 3 to 8 traces back to a line written here.

### 1.1 Positioning

Positioning: the place a brand occupies in the mind of a buyer relative to its alternatives. Al Ries and Jack Trout coined the use in a 1972 Advertising Age series and fixed it in "Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind" (1981). Their claims that still hold: the mind rejects what it cannot file, the first brand into a category owns it (the "law of leadership"), and a brand cannot own two words. Their claim that has aged badly: that positioning is mostly a communication act. In 2026 the position is proved by product, price, place and people before any copy is written.

The positioning statement, the format the house uses because every element is testable:

> For [the buyer, specific], [brand] is the [frame of reference: the category the buyer actually shops] that [point of difference, one] because [reason to believe, provable].

Geoffrey Moore's variant from "Crossing the Chasm" (1991) adds "Unlike [the nearest alternative], our product [the difference]". Use Moore's form when the client has a named competitor they lose deals to; it forces the comparison into the open.

The onlyness statement, Marty Neumeier, "Zag" (2006): "Our brand is the only [category] that [benefit]." Neumeier's full form adds who, where, why and when. The test is the word "only". If a competitor could sign the statement, there is no position yet. For a Jaipur jewellery house: "the only fine jeweller that still cuts in the city's own ateliers" survives the test; "the only jeweller with timeless design" fails it, because every jeweller claims it.

Frame of reference is the decision most teams get wrong. A Dubai members' club is shopped against other clubs, against the Four Seasons bar, against staying home. Pick the frame the buyer uses on the day they decide, because the point of difference only means something inside a frame.

### 1.2 Brand platform and brand house

Brand platform: the written set of foundational statements a brand operates from. The usual elements, with the question each answers:

| Element | Question it answers | Length | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why the organisation exists beyond money | One sentence | Grandiosity: "to make the world better" |
| Vision | What the world looks like if the purpose succeeds | One sentence | A sales target dressed as a vision |
| Mission | What the organisation does every day towards the vision | One sentence | A product description |
| Values | How people behave when nobody is watching | 3 to 5 words with a line each | "Integrity, innovation, excellence": the same list as everyone |
| Personality | The human character the brand would have | 3 adjectives plus their opposites | Adjectives nobody could disagree with |
| Promise | What the buyer can expect every time | One sentence | A tagline |
| Proposition | The reason to choose it over the alternatives | One sentence | Restating the promise |
| Pillars | The 3 to 4 proof areas that support the proposition | Nouns with evidence | Pillars that are features |
| Essence | The brand in two or three words | 2 to 3 words | Abstract nouns: "effortless elegance" |

The brand house (also drawn as a pyramid, a key or an onion: Unilever's Brand Key from the 1990s is the best known) arranges these in a diagram with essence at the roof and pillars holding it up.

Critique of the template-driven version. The format is fine; the failure is filling every box because the box exists. Three defects recur: the elements contradict each other because they were written by different people in different workshops; the words are category-generic because they were chosen by consensus; the document is never opened again because nothing in it constrains a decision. The house test is to read each line and ask what it forbids. "Warm" forbids nothing. "Speaks like a host, never like a hotel" forbids a great deal. A platform that forbids nothing is decoration. Write fewer elements with more teeth: Mask360's working minimum is positioning, three pillars with proof, personality with opposites, and one line of essence, on a single page.

### 1.3 Brand archetypes and why they are overused

Archetypes: recurring character types drawn from Carl Jung's work and applied to brands by Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson in "The Hero and the Outlaw" (2001). The twelve: Innocent, Explorer, Sage, Hero, Outlaw, Magician, Everyman (Regular Guy or Gal in the original), Lover, Jester, Caregiver, Creator, Ruler. Each carries a desire, a fear and a strategy: the Ruler desires control and fears chaos; the Lover desires intimacy and fears being unwanted.

Why they are overused. Twelve bins cannot hold a market of millions of brands; almost every luxury brand files itself under Ruler, Lover or Creator, which gives the archetype zero discriminating power inside the category. They describe personality, which is the easiest part of a platform, and say nothing about position, which is the hardest. They are also a workshop convenience: a deck of cards makes a three-hour meeting feel productive. Use them as a vocabulary check after the position is set (does the tone we have written sound like one coherent character?), never as the strategy. Mark Ritson's criticism, repeated across his Marketing Week columns, is the house view: archetypes are a tool for writing, and a poor one for choosing.

### 1.4 Tone of voice frameworks

Tone of voice: the consistent manner in which a brand's words sound, separate from what they say. Three working frameworks:

- Nielsen Norman Group's four dimensions (Kate Moran, 2016): funny to serious, formal to casual, respectful to irreverent, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. Place the brand on each scale with a number out of 10 and an example sentence. Its value is that it is measurable in a copy review.
- The "we are, we are never" pair list: three traits, each paired with the failure mode it must avoid ("confident, never boastful"). Its value is that it forbids something. Its risk is that every brand ends up "confident, never boastful".
- A public content style guide in the Mailchimp manner (styleguide.mailchimp.com, published openly since 2016): voice principles, then rules for grammar, numbers, inclusive language, and every content type from push notifications to legal copy. Its value is that writers can use it without a brand team present.

House rule: the tone is written as six example sentences in six situations (welcome, apology, price, sold out, legal, celebration) before any adjectives are chosen. The sentences are the standard; the adjectives are the index. Full verbal identity method lives in 10-copy-and-tone-of-voice.md.

### 1.5 Brand architecture

Brand architecture: the system that organises how a company's brands relate to each other and to the parent. David Aaker and Erich Joachimsthaler set the vocabulary in "The Brand Relationship Spectrum" (California Management Review, 2000).

| Model | Definition | Example | When it is right | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branded house | One master brand across everything; descriptors differ | Aman: Amanpuri (1988), Amangiri, Aman New York; the place name is the descriptor | One promise, one audience, trust transfers | One scandal hits every door |
| Sub-brands | Master brand plus a named offer with its own personality | Janu by Aman (first opened Tokyo, 2024); Tanishq's Rivaah and Mia | Reach a new audience without losing the halo | Sub-brand can dilute the master's codes |
| Endorsed | Independent brand carries a visible parent signature | Courtyard by Marriott; Taj (IHCL) with the Tata endorsement on corporate layers | Parent lends trust, child keeps its world | Two logos on every surface |
| House of brands | Independent brands; parent invisible to buyers | LVMH (about 75 maisons as of 2026: Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany); Unilever historically (Dove, Hellmann's) | Brands compete in different codes or price tiers | Every brand pays its own marketing bill |
| Hybrid | Deliberate mix by business unit | Tata: Tata Motors and Tata Steel (branded house), Titan and Taj (endorsed or standalone), Tata Neu (sub-brand app, 2022); Marriott: about 30 brands from endorsed (JW Marriott) to standalone (The Ritz-Carlton, St. Regis, W) under the Marriott Bonvoy loyalty umbrella (2019) | Most large groups end here | Needs a written decision rule or it drifts |

Unilever is the case for the drift from house of brands towards endorsement: the Wolff Olins "U" mark (2004) now appears on packs, because the corporate reputation became an asset worth lending.

Decision rule the house applies: a new offer gets its own brand only when its buyer, price tier or codes differ from the master's enough that the master's codes would hurt it. Everything else is a descriptor. Every extra brand is a permanent cost line.

### 1.6 Portfolio strategy and naming hierarchies

Portfolio strategy: deciding which brands to hold, which to fold and where each sits on price and audience. Naming hierarchy: the grammar for how names stack (master, range, product, variant). Keep four levels at most. An example for a hospitality group: group (corporate), hotel brand (consumer), property (place name), outlet (restaurant or spa name). The property and outlet names are where character lives; the group name is where trust lives. Write the hierarchy as a rule, with an example at each level, in the guidelines. Zomato's 2025 restructure is the current Indian reference: the listed parent was renamed Eternal Ltd in February 2025 so that Zomato, Blinkit, District and Hyperpure sit as consumer brands under a corporate name no buyer needs to know.

### 1.7 Brand audits

A brand audit is an inventory with judgement. Four lenses, run before any strategy is written, two to three weeks, output as one document with photographs of every touchpoint:

- Visual: collect every live application (site, app, social, packaging, signage, print, uniforms, vehicles, email). Count the logo variants in circulation (a typical unaudited mid-size brand has 6 to 12). Score consistency of colour, type and layout.
- Verbal: pull 50 to 100 pieces of live copy. Score against the NN/g dimensions. List the words used more than five times.
- Experiential: walk the first impression chain in section 10.2 as a buyer. Time each step. Photograph each.
- Competitive: the same visual and verbal audit on 5 to 8 competitors, laid out side by side on one board with the logos covered. This board is where category codes (section 1.8) become visible.

### 1.8 Semiotics for brands

Semiotics: the study of signs and how meaning is made from them. Roland Barthes ("Mythologies", 1957; "Elements of Semiology", 1964) gave brands the tools: denotation (what a sign literally shows: a horse and rider), connotation (what it suggests: aristocracy, sport) and myth (the connotation presented as natural fact: that this brand simply is aristocratic). A monogram on canvas denotes initials and connotes ownership, lineage and price.

Residual, dominant and emergent codes: the three-tier reading borrowed from Raymond Williams ("Marxism and Literature", 1977) and applied to commercial semiotics by Virginia Valentine and Monty Alexander at Semiotic Solutions in the 1990s. Residual codes are fading (gold foil, "since 1899" as the whole idea, the crest on everything). Dominant codes are the category's current normal (for luxury in 2026: serif wordmarks, beige, slow film, a Maison line). Emergent codes are rising at the edge (tactile imperfection, utilitarian signage type, a brand that posts like a person; see design-taste, references/currency-2026.md). The strategic use: locate the client's category on the three tiers, then decide whether to lead the emergent or own the dominant better than anyone.

Category codes: the set of signs a category shares, which let a buyer recognise the category in under a second. Breaking one is how a brand becomes visible. Method: list 10 to 15 codes from the competitive board; keep enough that the buyer still knows the category (usually 60 to 70% of them); break one loudly and one quietly. Liquid Death breaking water's codes with beer and metal codes is the textbook case; Raw Mango breaking Indian bridal's gold-and-red codes with plain handloom and a near-empty frame is the Indian one.

### 1.9 Cultural strategy

Douglas Holt, "How Brands Become Icons" (2004) and, with Douglas Cameron, "Cultural Strategy" (2010). The argument: iconic brands do not win by functional benefit or by personality; they win by performing a myth that resolves a tension in the culture of their moment. Holt's method: find the social disruption, identify the ideological opportunity it opens (an anxiety people want resolved), locate the source material (a subculture or a tradition that already lives the answer), and have the brand perform it consistently. Holt's examples (Nike performing the ethos of the lone combatant through inner-city athletes, Jack Daniel's performing frontier masculinity) are American; the mechanism travels. In India, Tanishq's wedding films performing a modern marriage against conservative expectation, and Cred performing the idea that paying bills on time is a form of status, are cultural plays in Holt's sense. The house uses Holt as the test for a campaign idea and Sharp (1.11) as the test for a media plan; they answer different questions.

### 1.10 The luxury strategy

Jean-Noël Kapferer and Vincent Bastien, "The Luxury Strategy" (2009; second edition 2012). Their central claim: luxury is a business model with its own rules that invert mass marketing, and a premium brand that borrows luxury's look without its economics ("premium" is comparative, "luxury" is incomparable) ends up neither. The second edition lists 24 anti-laws of marketing. The ones that bear directly on identity and communication:

1. Forget about positioning; luxury is superlative, never comparative.
2. Does your product have enough flaws? (The flaw is the trace of the hand.)
3. Do not pander to your customers' wishes.
4. Keep non-enthusiasts out.
5. Do not respond to rising demand.
6. Dominate the client.
7. Make it difficult for clients to buy.
9. The role of advertising is to recreate the dream; selling is the boutique's job.
10. Communicate to those you are not targeting.
11. The presumed price should always seem higher than the actual price.
12. Luxury sets the price; price does not set luxury.
16. Keep stars out of your advertising.
17. Cultivate closeness to the arts.
20. Do not test (consumer research cannot judge what has not been seen).
21. Do not look for consensus.
24. Do not sell openly on the internet (written in 2009; by 2026 the maisons sell online and the law is read as "never let the web set the terms").

What this means for identity: a luxury mark works as a seal and the selling happens elsewhere; the identity should age slowly (Hermès has kept the 1945 carriage), raise the perceived price (space, silence, restraint, see design-taste section 2), and address the many while serving the few (the campaign film is watched by millions who will never buy). For Mask360's premium clients (a Goa boutique hotel, a Mumbai beauty brand) the honest call is usually "premium" and the mistake is to dress premium in luxury's refusal to sell.

### 1.11 Distinctiveness versus differentiation, and availability

Byron Sharp, "How Brands Grow" (2010), with Jenni Romaniuk, "How Brands Grow Part 2" (2016), and Romaniuk, "Building Distinctive Brand Assets" (2018), from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. The claims the evidence supports across categories: brands grow by acquiring many light buyers; buyers see brands as more alike than marketers believe; loyalty follows penetration (the double jeopardy law); differentiation as perceived by buyers is weak and short-lived.

Distinctiveness: being recognisable as yourself quickly. Differentiation: being meaningfully different in what you offer. Sharp's position is that distinctiveness is the reliable lever and differentiation is overrated. Mark Ritson's position is that both matter and that Sharp's evidence comes from repertoire categories (soft drinks, banking) where it holds best. The house follows Ritson's synthesis with Sharp's discipline: differentiate in the product and the position; be ruthlessly distinctive in the identity.

Distinctive brand assets (DBAs): the non-name elements (colour, shape, typeface, character, sound, pattern, tagline) that trigger the brand name in memory. Romaniuk measures each on two axes, fame (the percentage of category buyers who link the asset to the brand) and uniqueness (the percentage of those links that go only to this brand), and plots them on a grid: use or lose (high, high), investment potential (low fame, high uniqueness), avoid solo use (high fame, low uniqueness: the asset is also another brand's), ignore or test (low, low). For a mature brand, the audit in 1.7 should end with this grid.

Mental availability: the propensity for the brand to be noticed or thought of in buying situations; built by linking the brand to many category entry points (CEPs: the cues a buyer starts from, such as "anniversary dinner", "gift for mother-in-law", "weekend in the hills"). Physical availability: how easy the brand is to find and buy where and when the buyer looks. For a premium brand with a single boutique, physical availability is the site, the Instagram DM and the WhatsApp line; the identity must be instantly recognisable in a 1080 by 1350 feed post because that is where the brand lives.

## 2. Naming

A name is the only identity element that appears in speech, search, law and the URL at once. It is also the element clients change most reluctantly, so the process has to be rigorous enough that the final name survives the founder's spouse, the lawyer and the Hindi-speaking driver.

### 2.1 Types of name

| Type | Definition | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Says what the thing is | Bank of Baroda, Dubai Mall, Blue Tokai Coffee Roasters (the descriptor half) | Instant comprehension | Weak or unregistrable as a trademark (India Trade Marks Act 1999, section 9(1)(b)); no room to grow |
| Suggestive | Hints at a benefit or quality | Amazon (scale), Nykaa (from nayika, heroine), Ounass (Arabic for "people") | Meaning plus protectability | Hint can date or translate badly |
| Abstract or coined | Invented word | Kodak, Zoya, Mokobara, NEOM (Greek neos plus the M of Arabic mustaqbal, future) | Strongest trademark; empty vessel for meaning | Costs money to fill with meaning |
| Founder | A person's name | Sabyasachi, Hermès, Tata, Chanel | Provenance, authorship | The person's reputation is the brand's; hard to sell the company |
| Acronym or initialism | Letters | HDFC, LVMH, IHCL | Short | Meaningless until famous; many clash |
| Compound | Two words fused | Facebook, Snapdeal, PhonePe | Memorable, available | Can feel start-up generic |
| Place or heritage | A place or tradition | Jaipur Rugs, Amangiri (Sanskrit: aman, peace; giri, mountain), Raw Mango | Rootedness | Geographic names are hard to register (section 9(1)(b) again) |
| Metaphor | An object or idea standing in | Apple, Jaguar, Oberoi's Trident, Tiger (Sabyasachi's crest) | Story and image come free | Crowded: there are many Tigers and Lotuses on the register |

### 2.2 The process

Eight steps, four to six weeks, run in parallel with strategy because the name must express the position:

1. Brief (2 days): position, audience, languages the name will be spoken in, markets and Nice classes it must be registrable in, hard constraints (must work as a handle, must survive a 16px favicon as an initial), names the client already loves and hates, and why.
2. Territories (3 days): five to seven conceptual directions ("the craftsman's tool", "a Rajasthani word for light", "an invented word with a hard consonant"), each with a rationale tied to the position.
3. Generation (1 week): 500 or more candidates across territories. Below 300 the shortlist is the designer's taste; above 500 the strong ones surface by comparison. Sources: dictionaries in every relevant language, etymologies, place names, trade vocabulary of the craft, mythologies, coinage by morpheme recombination, and generative models used as a thesaurus (they produce the obvious first; push them to the fourth and fifth round).
4. First screen (2 days): to 40 to 60. Kill criteria: more than four syllables, ambiguous spelling when heard, an existing famous brand in any category, an unintended meaning in Hindi, Urdu, Arabic, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil, Bengali, Punjabi, Malayalam or Kannada (the languages Mask360's audiences speak; section 2.3).
5. Linguistic and cultural check (1 week): native speakers, written report per language.
6. Availability (3 days): domain, handles, trademark pre-screen by class (section 2.4).
7. Shortlist of 8 to 12 with written rationale, pronunciation guide, a mock headline, a mock URL and a mock Instagram handle for each. Presented as section 2.5 describes.
8. Legal clearance (2 to 4 weeks with counsel) on the final three, in the order the client prefers. Never one name alone; one in three fails full clearance.

### 2.3 Linguistic checks across Hindi, Arabic, Urdu and regional languages

The check is for four things in each language: an unintended meaning, a rude homophone, a pronunciation that breaks the name, and a script problem when transliterated.

- Hindi and Urdu share phonology and differ in script and register; check both, because a Mumbai audience hears Hindi and a Dubai South Asian audience reads Urdu-register words. Aspirated and retroflex consonants (the difference between d and dh, t and ṭ) change meanings; a Latin spelling cannot encode them, so the Devanagari and Nastaliq spellings must be fixed and written into the guidelines.
- Arabic: no p, v or hard g in Modern Standard Arabic (p becomes b, v becomes f, g becomes gh, j or k depending on dialect); a name with p or v will be respelt by every Gulf speaker. Short vowels are unwritten, so an unfamiliar name read in Arabic script can be vocalised several ways. Check Gulf, Levantine and Egyptian pronunciations separately: a Lebanese creative director and an Emirati buyer will say the name differently. Fix the Arabic spelling in the guidelines as a drawn wordmark (section 3.4), because a name left to be typed will be typed four ways.
- Regional Indian languages: at minimum Marathi and Gujarati for Mumbai, Rajasthani and Hindi for Jaipur, Konkani for Goa, Punjabi for Delhi; Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam when the brand sells nationally. Each check costs a few thousand rupees with a native-speaking copywriter; skipping it has cost brands relaunches.
- Transliteration tests: write the name in Devanagari and Arabic script and read it back cold with three speakers each. If two of three say it differently, the Latin spelling needs to change or the spoken form needs to be fixed by a tagline that contains it.

### 2.4 Availability and trademark pre-screening

Order of checks, cheapest first:

1. Domain: the exact .com, .in and .ae; then .co and .ae alternatives. A premium exact .com on the aftermarket runs from about USD 2,000 to USD 50,000 in 2026 depending on the word; budget for it or name around it.
2. Handles: Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn, X, TikTok (TikTok is unavailable in India since June 2020 and active in the UAE). Instagram handles are limited to 30 characters; aim for the exact name under 15.
3. Trademark pre-screen by class. Trademarks are registered per class under the Nice Classification (WIPO; 45 classes: 1 to 34 goods, 35 to 45 services; the 12th edition came into force on 1 January 2023 and is updated in a new version each January). A hospitality brand needs at least class 43 (hotel, restaurant services), 35 (retail, advertising) and often 3 (cosmetics, for amenities), 25 (clothing, for merchandise) and 41 (events).
   - India: the public search at the IP India portal (tmrsearch.ipindia.gov.in) offers wordmark search (starts with, contains, match with), phonetic search (the most important for Indian names, because Indian examiners object on phonetic similarity) and Vienna code search for device marks. Search every relevant class with the name, its phonetic variants and its common misspellings.
   - UAE: the Ministry of Economy and Tourism (the trademark office; formerly the Ministry of Economy) runs the online search within its trademark e-services.
   - WIPO Global Brand Database (branddb.wipo.int): international registrations under the Madrid system plus many national collections; also an image similarity search for device marks.
4. Common-law use: a Google search in quotes, plus the app stores and Instagram, for an unregistered business using the name in the same category. In India, prior use defeats a later registration (section 34 of the Trade Marks Act 1999), so an unregistered Jaipur boutique with the name since 2015 is a real obstacle.

A pre-screen filters the list; clearance is counsel's work (section 2.6).

### 2.5 Presenting names

Names die in the room for bad reasons: read in a list, in the wrong typeface, by a client who hears them for the first time with no context. Rules:

- Never a list. Each name on its own page: the name set in a neutral face at large size, the pronunciation, the territory it came from, two sentences of rationale, a mock headline, the URL and the handle, and the pre-screen result.
- Show it in use before showing it alone: on a shopfront, on a box, in a search result, in a WhatsApp message.
- Say each name aloud three times before discussing it. Most objections are to the first hearing.
- Present 8 to 12; recommend three; say which one the studio would choose and why.
- Forbid the "ask the team" email. Names polled by a staff of forty return the safest one.
- Record the client's objections verbatim; "it sounds like a pharma brand" is data about the category codes, and a cue for the next round.

### 2.6 The law of naming in India and the UAE

India (as of October 2026; verify fees on the IP India site before quoting):

- Governing law: Trade Marks Act 1999 (in force 15 September 2003) and Trade Marks Rules 2017. Absolute grounds for refusal (section 9): marks devoid of distinctive character, descriptive marks, customary terms, marks that deceive, that hurt religious susceptibilities, that are scandalous or obscene, or prohibited under the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act 1950 (which bars names and emblems such as the national flag, Ashoka Chakra, Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, the UN and WHO, and the Prime Minister's name). Relative grounds (section 11): similarity to an earlier mark causing likelihood of confusion; well-known marks are protected across classes.
- Fees (First Schedule, Trade Marks Rules 2017): Form TM-A application per class, e-filing, Rs 4,500 for an individual, startup or small enterprise and Rs 9,000 for all others; physical filing Rs 5,000 and Rs 10,000. Fees are per class per mark.
- Timeline: examination report typically in 1 to 3 months; publication in the Trade Marks Journal; opposition window 4 months from publication (Rule 42); registration in roughly 12 to 24 months if unopposed. Protection 10 years from the application date, renewable every 10 years (section 25). The symbol ™ can be used from filing; ® only after registration (misuse is an offence under section 107).
- Company name: reserved separately through the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (SPICe+ Part A) under the Companies Act 2013 and Rule 8 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules 2014 (undesirable names, names implying government patronage). A reserved company name gives no trademark right and vice versa.
- Madrid: India joined the Madrid Protocol on 8 July 2013; an Indian applicant can extend to the UAE through WIPO from an Indian base application.

UAE (as of October 2026; fee figures from law-firm summaries, verify with the Ministry before quoting):

- Governing law: Federal Decree-Law No. 36 of 2021 on Trademarks, in force 2 January 2022, replacing Federal Law No. 37 of 1992. It introduced multi-class applications (the UAE and the GCC were single-class jurisdictions before), recognised non-traditional marks (3D, sound, smell, hologram) and a grievance committee route before court.
- Prohibited: marks offending public morals or religion, religious symbols, names and flags of states and international organisations, Red Crescent and Red Cross emblems, geographic names that would confuse as to origin, and marks identical or similar to well-known marks.
- Fees: reported as about AED 750 at filing, AED 750 for publication and AED 5,000 at registration, totalling about AED 6,500 per class (Cabinet Decision No. 20 of 2020 as amended in 2025 per law-firm commentary). Opposition period 30 days from publication. Protection 10 years, renewable.
- Madrid: the UAE acceded on 28 September 2021 with effect from 28 December 2021 (Federal Law No. 67 of 2021).
- Trade name versus trademark: a trade name is licensed by the emirate's economic department (Dubai's DET) or the free zone and must avoid religious terms, names of rulers, and foreign names without justification; it confers no trademark right. Arabic trade names are mandatory on signage with the Latin version, which affects lockup design (section 3.4).

House rule across both countries: file the wordmark in black and white first (which, in Indian and UAE practice, covers all colours; a colour claim limits protection to those colours), file the device mark separately, and file in every class the business will plausibly enter within five years. Filing early is cheaper than renaming.

## 3. Logo and mark design

The logo is the smallest part of an identity and the one the client will judge the whole project by. It must work at 16 pixels and 16 metres, in one colour, embroidered, debossed, and read correctly by a stranger in a second. Taste rules for marks are in design-taste; this section is construction.

### 3.1 Typology

| Type | What it is | Examples | Use when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wordmark (logotype) | The name set in custom or chosen letterforms | Google, Coca-Cola, Sabyasachi, Saint Laurent | The name is short and distinctive | Needs a separate device for small spaces |
| Lettermark | Initials as letterforms | HBO, IBM, HDFC | Long name, famous initials | Reads as finance or government |
| Monogram | Initials interlaced into one form | LV (1896 canvas), CC Chanel, GG Gucci, YSL (Cassandre, 1961), Loewe Anagram (Vicente Vela, 1970) | Luxury and fashion, where the monogram becomes a pattern | Crowded territory; the interlock is hard to draw well |
| Pictorial | A recognisable object | Apple, Shell, the Air India jharokha window (2023) | The object carries the story | Literal objects date; an object in the name is redundant |
| Abstract | A non-representational form | Nike swoosh, Pepsi globe, Mastercard circles | Global brands that must avoid any one language | Needs decades of spend to mean anything |
| Mascot | A character | Michelin Man (1898), Amul girl (1966), Air India Maharajah (1946), Reddit's Snoo | Warmth, story, social content | Needs a character bible and a 3D model in 2026 |
| Emblem | Type inside a shape, crest-like | Porsche crest, BMW roundel, Royal Enfield, Harley-Davidson, Burberry Equestrian Knight (1901) | Heritage, engineering, clubs | Illegible under 20mm unless a simplified version exists |
| Combination | Mark plus wordmark in a fixed lockup | Burger King, Tanishq | Most brands | Treating the lockup as the only form |
| Dynamic or generative | A system of rules that produces many versions | MTV (1981), AOL (Wolff Olins, 2009), MIT Media Lab (TheGreenEyl, 2011), OCAD University (Bruce Mau Design, 2011), City of Melbourne (Landor, 2009) | Institutions with many voices | Costs more to govern; a bad instance is still the logo |
| Responsive suite | One mark drawn at 4 to 6 levels of detail for size | Joe Harrison's responsivelogos.co.uk demonstrations (2014) showed the method with Disney, Coca-Cola and others | Every brand in 2026; the favicon, app icon and billboard need different drawings | Teams deploy the wrong level |

### 3.2 Construction: grids, geometry and optical correction

A construction grid: the geometric scaffold a mark is drawn on. Use it to make decisions repeatable, never as proof. The "golden ratio circles" overlay on the Twitter bird (2012) and the Apple mark started a decade of after-the-fact grids pasted on finished logos; a grid presented in a case study with 40 circles is a tell that the grid came last.

Geometry versus optics: a mark that is mathematically regular looks wrong, because the eye is not a ruler. Optical correction is the set of adjustments that make the form look regular:

| Correction | Rule of thumb | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Overshoot | Round and pointed forms extend past the baseline and cap line by about 1 to 3% of cap height (type designers use 2 to 3% at text sizes) | A circle that touches the line looks smaller than a square that touches it |
| Circle versus square | A circle of the same width as a square looks about 2 to 3% smaller; draw it that much larger | Area read by the eye is lower for the circle |
| Horizontal strokes | Draw horizontals about 5 to 10% thinner than verticals | Equal horizontals read heavier |
| Joins | Thin the strokes where two meet (the junction of a V or the crotch of an A) or cut an ink trap | Ink and pixels both gain weight at joins; the join reads as a blot |
| Optical centre | Place a centred element about 2 to 5% above geometric centre | The eye weights the upper half |
| Diagonals | A diagonal of equal stroke width reads heavier than a vertical; thin it slightly | Same reason as horizontals |
| Visual weight balance | Balance areas, never dimensions: a black circle next to a black square of equal width must be enlarged; a thin wordmark next to a solid mark must be set heavier or the mark lightened | Mass is what the eye weighs |
| Triangle and point | A point needs overshoot and a flat cap is cut off beyond the line | Acute forms vanish at the apex |

Work at 1,000 units cap height in Illustrator or Glyphs, correct at that size, then check at 24px and 8mm; corrections that are invisible at large size are what save the small one.

### 3.3 Clear space and minimum sizes

Clear space (exclusion zone): the margin around the mark that nothing else may enter. Define it from an element of the mark itself so it scales: the x-height of the wordmark, the cap height, or the height of the mark's main form. The common rule is clear space equal to the cap height on all sides, or the x-height on tight digital layouts; emblems need more (1.5 times the height) because their edges are busy.

Minimum sizes, with the physical reason:

| Context | Minimum | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Print, mark alone | 8 to 10mm wide | Offset printing loses strokes under about 0.25pt (0.09mm); at 8mm a hairline in the mark is already at that limit |
| Print, wordmark | 20 to 25mm wide for a 6 to 12 letter name | Letters need counters open at 300dpi |
| Print, emblem with text | 15 to 20mm, with a simplified version below that | Text inside a crest drops out first |
| Screen, mark alone | 24 to 32px | The thinnest stroke needs at least 1 device pixel at 1x; at 24px a mark with a stroke at 1/24 of its height is 1px |
| Screen, wordmark | 80 to 120px wide | Same reason, across more letters |
| Favicon | 16px, drawn separately | At 16px only a solid initial or a silhouette survives; Google asks for a square icon larger than 48px for search results |
| Embroidery | 25mm minimum, no stroke under 1mm, no detail under 2mm | Thread width is about 0.4mm; satin stitch needs at least 1mm |
| Engraving, debossing | No stroke under 0.3mm, no counter under 0.5mm | Tool radius |
| Signage | Legible at 10m per 25mm of cap height for a wordmark (a common signage rule) | Reading distance scales with cap height |

The responsive suite (3.1) exists because of this table: the full mark, a simplified mark, a glyph, and a favicon drawing.

### 3.4 Lockups, including bilingual lockups

Lockup: the fixed relationship between mark and wordmark (and strapline, if any). Supply horizontal, stacked and mark-only versions; define the gap as a multiple of a mark unit; forbid new lockups in the guidelines.

Bilingual lockups for Mask360's markets:

- Latin and Arabic. Arabic reads right to left, so in a horizontal bilingual lockup the Arabic sits on the right and the Latin on the left, each reading inwards from its own edge, with the mark in the middle or at the far left. In a stacked lockup the Arabic usually sits above (the Gulf convention on signage, and the UAE trade name rule puts Arabic on signage with equal or greater prominence). Align the Arabic baseline (its letters sit on and hang from a baseline like Latin) with the Latin baseline. Arabic has a smaller visual x-height ("tooth height") and taller ascenders (alef, lam) and deeper descenders; to match visual weight, set the Arabic at roughly 10 to 20% larger point size than the Latin and match stroke weight by eye, never by point size. Choose an Arabic face designed as a companion (29LT, Boutros, TPTQ Arabic, Google's Noto Naskh and Noto Kufi for free), or commission a drawn Arabic wordmark; a Latin wordmark with a default Arabic font under it reads as an afterthought and the Gulf buyer sees it instantly. Kufi (geometric, rectilinear) pairs with grotesks; Naskh (calligraphic, cursive) pairs with serifs; Thuluth and Diwani are display scripts for a crest or a seal. Type detail in 03-typography.md.
- Latin and Devanagari. Devanagari hangs from the shirorekha (the headline bar); align the shirorekha with the Latin cap height or slightly below it, and the Devanagari baseline with the Latin baseline, so the two share a band. Devanagari at the same point size looks larger than Latin because its full forms sit between the headline and the descender zone; reduce it about 5 to 10% to match. Use companion families (ITF's Kohinoor, Ek Type's Mukta and Baloo, Google's Noto Devanagari) and check conjuncts in the name itself; a wordmark with a broken conjunct is a wordmark with a spelling mistake.
- Three scripts (Latin, Devanagari, Arabic) occur on Indian hospitality brands in the Gulf; stack them in a fixed order (Latin, then the market script) and never show all three at once on a consumer surface.

### 3.5 Colour versions

Supply, in this order and with the rule for each: full colour (primary); mono black (print on one colour, fax-era but still used for forms, engraving and newspaper); reversed (white on the brand's dark colour and on black); one-colour brand colour; and, if the full-colour mark uses tints, a solid version for embroidery and vinyl. Specify Pantone solid coated and uncoated, CMYK, RGB, HEX and, for signage and paint, RAL and NCS. Colour system detail in 04-colour.md.

### 3.7 The monogram tradition in luxury

Luxury's monograms are property marks that became patterns: Louis Vuitton's Monogram canvas (Georges Vuitton, 1896, drawn to defeat counterfeiters), Goyard's chevron (1892), Gucci's GG (1960s, from Guccio Gucci's initials), Fendi's FF Zucca (Karl Lagerfeld, 1965), Dior Oblique (Marc Bohan, 1967), Celine's Triomphe (1972, from the chain around the Arc de Triomphe), Burberry's TB (Peter Saville, 2018, since retired in favour of the knight). The lesson for a new premium brand: a monogram works when it is a repeat pattern as well as a mark, when it interlocks with two or three letters at most, and when the brand has the patience to put it on product for a decade before expecting it to mean anything. A monogram launched with the brand and used only on the website is a lettermark with ambition. Indian houses with a working monogram: Sabyasachi's Bengal tiger functions as a crest rather than a monogram; Zoya and Tanishq use wordmarks with a secondary device. The Gulf's working monograms live in hospitality (the Jumeirah and Emaar property marks) and in Arabic calligraphic seals.

### 3.8 Wordmark craft

A custom wordmark is the cheapest route to distinctiveness, because nobody else can own the letterforms. Craft rules:

- Start from a typeface with the right skeleton, then redraw: adjust widths, cut custom terminals, lower or raise the crossbars so the name's own letters harmonise (the R and the A in a name often need different angles than the font supplies).
- Kern by hand, at three sizes (16px, 48px, 300mm). Kerning is the adjustment of space between specific letter pairs; tracking is uniform spacing. A wordmark is kerned, never tracked. Watch the pairs that always fail: AV, AT, LT, Ta, Yo, rn (which reads as m at small size), and any pair with a round next to a straight.
- Spacing is checked by the squint test (blur the mark; the grey should be even) and by inverting (white on black exaggerates uneven gaps).
- The serif return: between about 2012 and 2019 Saint Laurent (2012), Balenciaga (2017), Burberry (2018), Celine (2018), Berluti and Balmain moved to near-identical geometric or grotesk sans wordmarks, a period Thierry Brunfaut of Base Design and others named blanding (section 7.2). Burberry's 2023 serif and knight, and Ferragamo's 2022 wordmark by Peter Saville (which dropped "Salvatore"), marked the reversal. In 2026 a serif wordmark is the dominant luxury code and a sans is the emergent one again; the honest answer is that the face follows the brand's own history, and that a brand without a history should pick the skeleton that its product's forms already have.
- Draw the Arabic and Devanagari wordmarks with the same care (3.4), with a calligrapher or a script type designer; the Latin designer's instinct for stroke contrast does not transfer across scripts.

### 3.9 Trademark drawing

The trademark drawing is the version of the mark filed with the registry. Rules: a clean vector in black on white (covers all colours in India and the UAE; a colour filing limits protection), no clear-space marks, no gradient (gradients are described in words if claimed), the wordmark and the device filed as separate applications so each is protected alone, and the exact lockup filed if the lockup is the famous form. Figurative elements are classified under the Vienna Classification (WIPO; ninth edition in force since 1 January 2023); the examiner uses these codes to search for similar devices, which is why the pre-screen in 2.4 includes a Vienna code search. Provide the filing files as PDF and JPG at 300dpi, 8 by 8cm, which both registries accept.

## 4. Identity system design

A logo is a signature; a system is the handwriting. The system is what lets a junior designer at the client produce a correct Instagram story at 11pm without calling the studio. Every element below is specified in its own corpus file; this section sets what the identity project must decide about each and how they lock together.

### 4.1 Colour system

Decide in the identity project: the owned colour (one; the Hermès orange, Tiffany blue, Bottega green model), the two to three working neutrals, the accent (one), the functional colours for digital (success, warning, error, which must never collide with the brand colours), and the dark-mode mapping. Specify every colour in Pantone, CMYK, RGB, HEX, and as design tokens. Full method, contrast and token structure in 04-colour.md; taste caps (two plus one accent) in design-taste.

### 4.2 Typography system

Decide: the primary face and its licence (desktop, web, app, and the number of page views or users the licence covers; web licences for a mid-size brand run from about USD 300 to 3,000 per year in 2026, and a custom typeface from a foundry from about USD 20,000 upward), the secondary face if any, the Arabic and Devanagari companions, the type scale, and the fallback stack for email and documents. The identity project also delivers the wordmark's relationship to the typeface (same family or deliberately different). Full method in 03-typography.md; pairing taste in design-taste, references/type.md.

### 4.3 Layout and grid system

Decide: the base unit, the margin ratio (luxury margins run at 2 to 3 times what feels safe; see design-taste section 2), the logo position rule (one corner, always), the grid for the three master formats (A4 portrait, 1080 by 1350 feed, 1920 by 1080 screen), and the alignment rule for bilingual pages (mirrored grids for Arabic, where the margin logic flips). Full method in 05-layout-and-grids.md.

### 4.4 Imagery and photography direction

Decide: the light (natural, one direction), the lens (the 35 to 50mm look for interiors and people, macro for product), the colour treatment (one grade, written as a LUT and a Lightroom preset), the subjects (what is never shown), the ratio of people to place to product, and the retouching rule (skin texture kept, no symmetry correction). Deliver a shot list with 20 to 40 reference frames and the grade. The art-direction skill (anthropic-skills:art-direction) carries the method for briefing a shoot; the identity project's job is the rule set the shoot is briefed from.

### 4.5 Illustration style

Decide whether the brand illustrates at all. If yes: the one style (line weight in px at a reference size, colour fill rule, the degree of abstraction), the subjects it is used for and never used for (never for product in luxury; product is photographed), and one illustrator or a documented method so the style survives a change of hands. Corporate flat illustration ("corporate Memphis") is on the dead list in design-taste; a hand-drawn, imperfect line is the current counter-signal.

### 4.6 Iconography

Decide: the grid (24px is the convention, with a 20px live area and 2px padding; Material Symbols use 24 with a 20 safe area), the stroke weight (1.5 or 2px at 24px, consistent), corner radius, terminal style (round or square), and the optical rule (circles and diagonals drawn slightly larger to match squares, as in 3.2). Deliver as an SVG set with a naming convention and a Figma component library. Icons must share DNA with the mark (terminal style, stroke contrast) or they look bought. Decide also whether the brand uses an existing open set (Phosphor, Lucide, Material Symbols) with a customised weight, which is the right answer for most clients because a bespoke set of 200 icons costs more than the logo.

### 4.7 Pattern and graphic devices

A graphic device: a recurring shape, line or pattern derived from the mark that carries the identity when the logo is absent. Air India's jharokha window (used since 1971 on aircraft, made the central device of the 2023 system), Burberry's check (1920s lining, registered as a trademark), Etihad's facet pattern (the 2014 identity by Landor, drawn from Abu Dhabi's desert and architecture), Reddit's speech-bubble counter. Rules: derive it from the mark or the name so it is defensible; specify it as a vector with a scale rule; give it a minimum and maximum size relative to the format; and ration it (a device on every surface becomes wallpaper in a year).

### 4.8 Motion identity

Decide: how the mark animates (one reveal, under 1.5 seconds, with the easing specified), the house easing curve, the transition vocabulary (two or three moves), the type behaviour (static, kinetic, or performative as in Studio Dumbar's work), and the sound pairing. Deliver the logo animation as Lottie and ProRes with alpha. Motion rules in 12-motion-design.md.

### 4.9 Sound identity

Decide whether the brand has a sonic logo (a 1 to 3 second audio mark), a music palette and a voice (for IVR, video, apps). A sonic logo in 2026 costs from about USD 15,000 with a specialist studio and is justified for hospitality groups and consumer apps, rarely for a single boutique. Method in 15-sound-and-music.md.

### 4.10 Verbal identity

Decide: the tone (1.4), the naming hierarchy (1.6), the house vocabulary (words used, words banned), the bilingual rule (which language leads on which surface; whether Arabic is a translation or a parallel composition), and the strapline if there is one. Method in 10-copy-and-tone-of-voice.md; house copy rules in the mask360-copywriting skill.

### 4.11 Packaging and print

Decide: the substrate family (paper weights, finishes, the one special process such as blind deboss or edge paint), the box architecture (rigid, folding, drawer), the ribbon or closure, the unboxing sequence, and the print colour management. Luxury's box (the Hermès orange box, adopted during the Second World War when the usual cream card ran out; the Tiffany blue box; Cartier's red) is a distinctive asset with a higher fame score than most logos. Method, dielines and press specs in 20-print-and-packaging.md.

### 4.12 Digital and product

Decide: the design tokens (colour, type, space, radius, elevation, motion) exported from the identity so the product team builds from the same values; the component priorities (buttons, forms, cards, navigation); the accessibility floor (WCAG 2.2 AA contrast of 4.5:1 for body text, which constrains the pale luxury palette on screens); the dark mode; and the email system (the most-seen brand surface for hospitality, and the least designed). Method in 06-ui-design.md and 07-ux-and-product.md.

### 4.13 Environmental and retail

Decide: the signage family (fascia, totem, directional, room numbers, statutory) with materials, the lighting temperature (2700 to 3000K for hospitality and jewellery, 3500 to 4000K for galleries and offices), the material palette (which should share the brand's colour logic), the wayfinding type size by viewing distance (3.3), the bilingual signage rule (UAE: Arabic with the Latin, equal or larger), and the scent (section 10.4). The architect and the identity studio must share one material board; the usual failure is a brown-and-brass interior under a monochrome identity.

### 4.14 Uniforms and merchandise

Decide: who wears the mark and where (chest, cuff, collar; embroidered at 25mm minimum, 3.3), the uniform colour drawn from the palette's neutrals and never the accent (the accent is for moments), and the merchandise rule (what the brand will put its name on; a luxury brand puts its name on fewer things). Merchandise is a distinctiveness test: if the tote works with the logo covered, the system works.

### 4.15 The brand world beyond the logo

Brian Collins's studio (Collins, New York and San Francisco) and Koto (founded London 2015, now also New York, Berlin, Los Angeles and Sydney) work from the same premise: the identity is a world with its own physics (what moves, what sounds, what the light is like, what the brand would never do), and the logo is one object in it. Collins's work for Spotify (2015, with Spotify's in-house team; the duotone image treatment), Dropbox (2017), Mailchimp (2018; the Cooper Light wordmark and Freddie's wink), Twitch (2019) and the San Francisco Symphony (2018) each ships a vocabulary of moves before a logo rule. Koto's work for Discord (2021), Zoopla (2021) and Amazon Music (2020) delivers the same way: a brand idea in one line, a visual world built to express it, and the logo as the last decision. The method, which the house follows:

1. Write the brand idea as one sentence of feeling (design-taste section 4 calls this the single idea).
2. Build the world's physics: light, material, motion, sound, humour, the forbidden list.
3. Design the five surfaces that matter most for this brand (for a hotel: arrival, the key, the room, the breakfast menu, the Instagram grid) before touching the logo.
4. Draw the mark to fit the world.
5. Write the rules from what was made, so the guidelines describe a working system instead of a theory.

## 5. Process for an identity project

### 5.1 Phases, durations and deliverables

A full identity for a premium brand runs 12 to 16 weeks; a refresh or a small brand runs 8 to 10. The durations below assume a studio team of a creative director, a strategist, two designers and a project lead at about 60% allocation, and a client who returns feedback within three working days.

| Phase | Duration | Activities | Deliverables | Sign-off gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Discovery and immersion | 2 to 3 weeks | 8 to 15 stakeholder interviews (founder, heads of sales, operations, the best front-line staff, 3 to 5 customers, 1 to 2 lapsed customers), the four audits (1.7), a competitive board, a half-day workshop on ambition and the forbidden list, site visits | Discovery report (20 to 40 pages), audit boards, interview synthesis, a one-page problem statement | Client agrees the problem statement |
| 2. Strategy | 2 to 3 weeks | Positioning options (2 to 3), platform, architecture decision, naming if needed (section 2), tone | Strategy document: positioning statement, onlyness statement, platform on one page, architecture diagram, verbal identity principles | Client signs the positioning; nothing visual starts before this |
| 3. Creative territories | 2 weeks | Three directions, each one idea, each a moodboard of 15 to 25 references with the borrowed thing named, a sketch of the mark, one applied surface, a line of copy | Territory presentation (3 directions) | Client picks one territory, with reasons recorded |
| 4. Concept development | 2 to 3 weeks | The chosen territory developed: 2 to 3 mark routes, type, colour, imagery direction, 6 to 10 applications each | Concept presentation | Client picks one route; up to 2 rounds of amends |
| 5. Refinement | 1 to 2 weeks | The mark drawn to final (3.2, 3.8), optical tests at all sizes, colour versions, bilingual lockups, legal pre-screen on the device (2.4) | Final mark approval sheet (the mark at 10mm, 24px, 1m; all versions) | Mark sign-off; after this the mark does not change |
| 6. System build | 2 to 3 weeks | Colour, type, grid, imagery, icons, devices, motion, tokens (section 4) | System document, Figma library, token file | System review |
| 7. Applications | 2 weeks | The 15 to 30 surfaces the brand lives on, designed for real: site key screens, app icon, social grid for 12 posts, stationery, packaging, signage, uniforms, email, deck, menu or lookbook | Application set, print-ready and dev-ready | Application review |
| 8. Guidelines | 1 to 2 weeks | Section 6 | Guidelines (PDF and web), asset pack, file suite (3.6 and 12.2) | Guidelines accepted |
| 9. Rollout and launch | 2 to 8 weeks, client-paced | Launch plan, internal launch (staff see it first), phased replacement (digital day one, print as stock runs out, signage last), launch content | Rollout plan, launch assets, press kit | Launch date |
| 10. Handover | 1 week | Training for the client's team (2 sessions), a 90-day support window, a quarterly review | Training deck, handover register, a named contact | Project close |

Rounds: two rounds of amendments per gated presentation, written into the contract, with a day rate for a third. Sign-off is in writing from one named decision maker; a committee cannot sign off a logo. Decision makers who join at phase 4 restart the clock to phase 2.

### 5.3 What goes wrong

- Visual work starts before positioning is signed; the client judges logos against an unwritten brief and every route loses.
- Three territories presented with one favourite; the client senses it and picks another to assert control. Present three the studio would be proud to build.
- Feedback by committee email. Insist on a live review with the decision maker.
- The mark drawn at 1,000px and never at 16. The favicon exposes it at launch.
- Arabic or Devanagari added in week 14. Scripts are designed in phase 5 with the Latin.
- The guidelines written as a theory. Write them from the applications made in phase 7.
