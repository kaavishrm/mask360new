## 1. Brand strategy foundations

Strategy is the part of the work the client cannot see and the part that decides whether the visible work survives contact with a board, a competitor and a decade. Every identity decision in sections 3 to 8 traces back to a line written here.

### 1.1 Positioning

Positioning: the place a brand occupies in the mind of a buyer relative to its alternatives. Al Ries and Jack Trout coined the use in a 1972 Advertising Age series and fixed it in "Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind" (1981). Their claims that still hold: the mind rejects what it cannot file, the first brand into a category owns it (the "law of leadership"), and a brand cannot own two words. Their claim that has aged badly: that positioning is mostly a communication act. In 2026 the position is proved by product, price, place and people before any copy is written.

The positioning statement, the format the house uses because every element is testable:

> For [the buyer, specific], [brand] is the [frame of reference: the category the buyer actually shops] that [point of difference, one] because [reason to believe, provable].

Point of difference: the one attribute the brand claims that the alternatives inside the frame cannot. Reason to believe: the evidence that makes the claim credible to a sceptical buyer. Geoffrey Moore's variant from "Crossing the Chasm" (1991) adds "Unlike [the nearest alternative], our product [the difference]". Use Moore's form when the client has a named competitor they lose deals to; it forces the comparison into the open.

The onlyness statement, Marty Neumeier, "Zag" (2006): "Our brand is the only [category] that [benefit]." Neumeier's full form adds who, where, why and when. The test is the word "only". If a competitor could sign the statement, there is no position yet. For a Jaipur jewellery house: "the only fine jeweller that still cuts in the city's own ateliers" survives the test; "the only jeweller with timeless design" fails it, because every jeweller claims it.

Frame of reference is the decision most teams get wrong. A Dubai members' club is shopped against other clubs, against the Four Seasons bar, against staying home. Pick the frame the buyer uses on the day they decide, because the point of difference only means something inside a frame.

April Dunford, "Obviously Awesome" (2019), is the method most technology and B2B practitioners use in 2026. It builds the position from five components in a fixed order: competitive alternatives (what the buyer would do without you, including nothing), unique attributes, the value those attributes deliver with proof, the customers who care most about that value, and the market category that makes the value obvious. "Sales Pitch" (2023) turns the result into a sales story. Its strength is the order: the category is chosen last, from the value, where most teams choose it first. Category design, from "Play Bigger" (Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead and Kevin Maney, 2016), is the variant for a brand creating its own frame of reference: name the problem, name the category, and be seen as its leader. It suits a genuinely new offer and is overused by brands with a better version of an existing one.

Perceptual mapping (brands plotted on a 2 by 2 grid of two attributes) is the most common positioning slide and the most often misused. The team picks the axes it prefers, and the empty quadrant it then finds is often empty because no buyer wants it. House rule: the axes are the buyer's choice criteria taken from the phase 1 interviews (5.1), in the buyer's words, and an empty quadrant is tested for demand before anyone calls it an opportunity.

### 1.2 Brand platform and brand house

Brand platform: the written set of foundational statements a brand operates from. The usual elements, with the question each answers:

| Element | Question it answers | Length | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why the organisation exists beyond money | One sentence | Grandiosity: "to make the world better" |
| Vision | What the world looks like if the purpose succeeds | One sentence | A sales target dressed as a vision |
| Mission | What the organisation does every day towards the vision | One sentence | A product description |
| Values | How people behave when nobody is watching | 3 to 5 words with a line each | "Integrity, innovation, excellence": the same list as everyone |
| Personality | The human character the brand would have | 3 adjectives plus their opposites | Adjectives nobody could disagree with |
| Promise | What the buyer can expect every time | One sentence | A tagline |
| Proposition | The reason to choose it over the alternatives | One sentence | Restating the promise |
| Pillars | The 3 to 4 proof areas that support the proposition | Nouns with evidence | Pillars that are features |
| Essence | The brand in two or three words | 2 to 3 words | Abstract nouns: "effortless elegance" |

The brand house arranges these in a diagram with essence at the roof and pillars holding it up. It is also drawn as a pyramid, an onion or a key: Unilever's Brand Key from the 1990s, the best known, runs on one page from the competitive environment and the target through insight, benefits, values and reasons to believe to a discriminator and an essence.

Critique of the fill-every-box version. The common failure is filling every box because the box exists. Three defects recur: the elements contradict each other because they were written by different people in different workshops; the words are category-generic because they were chosen by consensus; the document is never opened again because nothing in it constrains a decision. The house test is to read each line and ask what it forbids. "Warm" forbids nothing. "We speak like a host greeting a guest by name" forbids the form-letter voice of a hotel chain. A platform that forbids nothing is decoration. Write fewer elements with more teeth: Mask360's working minimum is positioning, three pillars with proof, personality with opposites, and one line of essence, on a single page.

Legal exposure. Purpose and pillar lines become advertising claims. India's Central Consumer Protection Authority issued its Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Greenwashing or Misleading Environmental Claims in October 2024, and the ASCI Code (Advertising Standards Council of India) applies to every advertisement; under both, a sustainability claim in a purpose, pillar or tagline must be specific and substantiated, with its qualifiers stated, and a bare "eco-friendly" is actionable. Every pillar's proof column carries the evidence a regulator would ask for.

### 1.3 Brand archetypes and why they are overused

Archetypes: recurring character types drawn from Carl Jung's work and applied to brands by Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson in "The Hero and the Outlaw" (2001). The twelve: Innocent, Explorer, Sage, Hero, Outlaw, Magician, Everyman (Regular Guy or Gal in the original), Lover, Jester, Caregiver, Creator, Ruler. Each carries a desire, a fear and a strategy: the Ruler desires control and fears chaos; the Lover desires intimacy and fears being unwanted.

Why they are overused. Twelve bins cannot hold a market of millions of brands; almost every luxury brand files itself under Ruler, Lover or Creator, which gives the archetype zero discriminating power inside the category. They describe personality, the easiest part of a platform, and say nothing about position, the hardest. They are also a workshop convenience: a deck of cards makes a three-hour meeting feel productive. Use them as a vocabulary check after the position is set (does the tone we have written sound like one coherent character?); they are too coarse to be the strategy. Mark Ritson's criticism, repeated across his Marketing Week columns, is the house view: archetypes are a tool for writing, and a poor one for choosing.

### 1.4 Tone of voice frameworks

Tone of voice: the consistent manner in which a brand's words sound, separate from what they say. Three working frameworks:

- Nielsen Norman Group's four dimensions (Kate Moran, 2016): funny to serious, formal to casual, respectful to irreverent, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. Place the brand on each scale with a number out of 10 and an example sentence. Its value is that it is measurable in a copy review.
- The "we are, we are never" pair list: three traits, each paired with the failure mode it must avoid (we are confident; we are never boastful). Its value is that it forbids something. Its risk is that every brand ends up confident and never boastful.
- A public content style guide in the Mailchimp manner (styleguide.mailchimp.com, published openly since 2016): voice principles, then rules for grammar, numbers, inclusive language, and every content type from push notifications to legal copy. Its value is that writers can use it without a brand team present.

House rule: the tone is written as six example sentences in six situations (welcome, apology, price, sold out, legal, celebration) before any adjectives are chosen. The sentences are the standard; the adjectives are the index. Full verbal identity method lives in 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md.

### 1.5 Brand architecture

Brand architecture: the system that organises how a company's brands relate to each other and to the parent. David Aaker and Erich Joachimsthaler set the vocabulary in "The Brand Relationship Spectrum" (California Management Review, 2000).

| Model | Definition | Example | When it is right | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branded house | One master brand across everything; descriptors differ | Aman: Amanpuri (1988), Amangiri, Aman New York; the place name is the descriptor | One promise, one audience, trust transfers | One scandal hits every door |
| Sub-brands | Master brand plus a named offer with its own personality | Janu by Aman (first opened Tokyo, 2024); Tanishq's Rivaah and Mia | Reach a new audience without losing the halo | Sub-brand can dilute the master's codes |
| Endorsed | Independent brand carries a visible parent signature | Courtyard by Marriott; Taj (IHCL) with the Tata endorsement on corporate layers | Parent lends trust, child keeps its world | Two logos on every surface |
| House of brands | Independent brands; parent invisible to buyers | LVMH (about 75 maisons as of 2026: Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany); Unilever historically (Dove, Hellmann's) | Brands compete in different codes or price tiers | Every brand pays its own marketing bill |
| Hybrid | Deliberate mix by business unit | Tata: Tata Motors and Tata Steel (branded house), Titan and Taj (endorsed or standalone), Tata Neu (sub-brand app, 2022); Marriott: about 30 brands from endorsed (JW Marriott) to standalone (The Ritz-Carlton, St. Regis, W) under the Marriott Bonvoy loyalty umbrella (2019) | Most large groups end here | Needs a written decision rule or it drifts |

Unilever is the case for the drift from house of brands towards endorsement: the Wolff Olins "U" mark (2004) now appears on packs, because the corporate reputation became an asset worth lending.

The spectrum has finer steps than the five rows, and the step chosen sets the size and position of the parent's mark on every surface. Inside house of brands sits the shadow endorser (the parent is known to buyers and absent from the product: Toyota behind Lexus). Inside endorsed brands sit strong endorsement (Courtyard by Marriott), the linked name (a shared root, as McDonald's McCafé) and token endorsement (a small parent signature on the back of a pack). Inside sub-brands, the master brand is either the driver or a co-driver that shares the purchase decision (Aaker's example: Gillette Sensor).

Decision rule the house applies: a new offer gets its own brand only when its buyer, price tier or codes differ from the master's enough that the master's codes would hurt it. Everything else is a descriptor. Every extra brand is a permanent cost line.

Branded residences are endorsement by licence, and the dominant luxury real estate model in Dubai, growing in Mumbai and Gurugram. A hospitality or fashion house (Armani, Bulgari, Aman, Dorchester Collection; Bugatti Residences by Binghatti in Dubai) licenses its name and design standards to a developer for a fee and keeps approval over every touchpoint. Savills' World Branded Residences reports put the average sales premium at about 30% over comparable unbranded stock and rank Dubai first by scheme pipeline (verify the current edition before quoting a figure). The identity consequences: a co-brand lockup with the developer (3.4); the licensor's guideline overrides the developer's on every shared surface; and the sales gallery is designed as the brand's boutique.

### 1.6 Portfolio strategy and naming hierarchies

Portfolio strategy: deciding which brands to hold, which to fold and where each sits on price and audience. David Aaker's "Brand Portfolio Strategy" (2004) names the roles a brand can play: strategic brand (the future of the business), linchpin brand (it carries a major segment or the loyalty programme), silver bullet (it changes the image of another brand, as a flagship hotel does for its group), cash cow (it funds the rest with little investment), and flanker or fighter brand (it defends the price flank against a cheaper competitor). Give every brand one role in writing; a brand with no role is a candidate to fold. IHCL is the Indian hospitality case: Taj, SeleQtions, Vivanta, Gateway, Ginger, ama Stays & Trails and Tree of Life each sit at a different endorsement distance from Taj and from Tata (verify the current brandscape on ihcltata.com before citing it).

Naming hierarchy: the grammar for how names stack (master, range, product, variant). Keep four levels at most. For a hospitality group: group (corporate), hotel brand (consumer), property (place name), outlet (restaurant or spa name). The property and outlet names are where character lives; the group name is where trust lives. Write the hierarchy as a rule, with an example at each level, in the guidelines.

Real estate runs a hierarchy of its own: developer (Emaar, Lodha, ROSHN) for trust, then master community (Dubai Hills Estate), then project, then tower or phase. The project name is where character lives, and it must clear RERA registration (4.16) and the trademark pre-screen (2.4) before the hoardings print, because it appears on every legal document of every sale.

Zomato's 2025 restructure is the current Indian reference: the listed parent's board approved renaming it Eternal Ltd in February 2025, and shareholders approved the change in March 2025, so that Zomato, Blinkit, District and Hyperpure sit as consumer brands under a corporate name no buyer needs to know (7.3).

### 1.7 Brand audits

A brand audit is an inventory with judgement. Four lenses, run before any strategy is written, two to three weeks, output as one document with photographs of every touchpoint (any point where a buyer meets the brand; mapped in 10.1):

- Visual: collect every live application (site, app, social, packaging, signage, print, uniforms, vehicles, email). Count the logo variants in circulation (a typical unaudited mid-size brand has 6 to 12). Score consistency of colour, type and layout.
- Verbal: pull 50 to 100 pieces of live copy. Score against the NN/g dimensions. List the words used more than five times.
- Experiential: walk the first impression chain (the sequence of surfaces a buyer meets first, set out in 10.2) as a buyer. Time each step. Photograph each.
- Competitive: the same visual and verbal audit on 5 to 8 competitors, laid out side by side on one board with the logos covered. This board is where category codes (section 1.8) become visible.

### 1.8 Semiotics for brands

Semiotics: the study of signs and how meaning is made from them. Two founding models sit underneath brand work. Ferdinand de Saussure ("Course in General Linguistics", 1916, compiled from his students' notes) split the sign into signifier (the form) and signified (the concept) and showed that the link between them is learned. Charles Sanders Peirce's three sign types map onto the mark typology in 3.1: an icon resembles its object (a pictorial mark: Apple, the Air India window); an index points to its object by a physical trace (a hand-stitched edge, a blind deboss, a calligrapher's stroke); a symbol means by convention alone (an abstract mark: the Nike swoosh, Mastercard's circles), which is why a symbol has to be bought with years of spend.

Roland Barthes ("Mythologies", 1957; "Elements of Semiology", 1964) gave brands the working tools: denotation (what a sign literally shows: a horse and rider), connotation (what it suggests: aristocracy, sport) and myth (the connotation presented as natural fact: that this brand simply is aristocratic). A monogram on canvas denotes initials and connotes ownership, lineage and price.

Residual, dominant and emergent codes: the three-tier reading borrowed from Raymond Williams ("Marxism and Literature", 1977) and applied to commercial semiotics by Virginia Valentine and Monty Alexander at Semiotic Solutions in the 1990s; Laura R. Oswald's "Marketing Semiotics" (Oxford University Press, 2012) and Rachel Lawes's "Using Semiotics in Marketing" (Kogan Page, 2020) set out the commercial method. Residual codes are fading (gold foil, "since 1899" as the whole idea, the crest on everything). Dominant codes are the category's current normal (for luxury in 2026: serif wordmarks, beige, slow film, a Maison line). Emergent codes are rising at the edge (tactile imperfection, utilitarian signage type, a brand that posts like a person; see design-taste, references/currency-2026.md). The strategic use: locate the client's category on the three tiers, then decide whether to lead the emergent or own the dominant better than anyone.

Category codes: the set of signs a category shares, which let a buyer recognise the category in under a second. Breaking one is how a brand becomes visible. Method: list 10 to 15 codes from the competitive board; keep enough that the buyer still knows the category (usually 60 to 70% of them); break one loudly and one quietly. Liquid Death breaking water's codes with beer and metal codes is the textbook case; Raw Mango breaking Indian bridal's gold-and-red codes with plain handloom and a near-empty frame is the Indian one.

### 1.9 Cultural strategy

Douglas Holt, "How Brands Become Icons" (2004) and, with Douglas Cameron, "Cultural Strategy" (2010). The argument: iconic brands win by performing a myth that resolves a tension in the culture of their moment. Holt's method: find the social disruption, identify the ideological opportunity it opens (an anxiety people want resolved), locate the source material (a subculture or a tradition that already lives the answer), and have the brand perform it consistently. Holt's examples (Nike performing the ethos of the lone combatant through inner-city athletes, Jack Daniel's performing frontier masculinity) are American; the mechanism travels. In India, Tanishq's wedding films performing a modern marriage against conservative expectation, and Cred performing the idea that paying bills on time is a form of status, are cultural plays in Holt's sense. The house uses Holt as the test for a campaign idea and Sharp (1.11) as the test for a media plan; they answer different questions.

### 1.10 The luxury strategy

Jean-Noël Kapferer and Vincent Bastien, "The Luxury Strategy" (2009; second edition 2012). Their central claim: luxury is a business model with its own rules that invert mass marketing, and a premium brand that borrows luxury's look without its economics ("premium" is comparative, "luxury" is incomparable) ends up neither. The second edition lists 24 anti-laws of marketing. The ones that bear directly on identity and communication, with Kapferer and Bastien's numbers:

- Anti-law 1: "Forget about positioning, luxury is not comparative."
- Anti-law 2: "Does your product have enough flaws?" The flaw is the trace of the hand.
- Anti-law 3: "Don't pander to your customers' wishes."
- Anti-law 4: "Keep non-enthusiasts out."
- Anti-law 5: "Don't respond to rising demand."
- Anti-law 6: "Dominate the client."
- Anti-law 7: "Make it difficult for clients to buy."
- Anti-law 9: "The role of advertising is not to sell." Its job is to recreate the dream; the selling happens in the boutique.
- Anti-law 10: "Communicate to those whom you are not targeting."
- Anti-law 11: "The presumed price should always seem higher than the actual price."
- Anti-law 12: "Luxury sets the price, price does not set luxury."
- Anti-law 16: "Keep stars out of your advertising."
- Anti-law 17: "Cultivate closeness to the arts for initiates."
- Anti-law 20: "Don't test." Consumer research cannot judge what it has not yet seen (positions on testing in 5.5).
- Anti-law 21: "Do not look for consensus."
- Anti-law 24: "Do not sell on the Internet." Written in 2009; by 2026 the maisons sell online and the law is read as "never let the web set the terms".

What this means for identity: a luxury mark works as a seal and the selling happens elsewhere; the identity should age slowly (Hermès has kept the Duc carriage and groom, drawn from an Alfred de Dreux lithograph, for about seventy years), raise the perceived price (space, silence, restraint; see design-taste section 2), and address the many while serving the few (the campaign film is watched by millions who will never buy). For Mask360's premium clients (a Goa boutique hotel, a Mumbai beauty brand) the right call is usually "premium", with luxury's codes borrowed selectively under the rules in 1.13.

### 1.11 Distinctiveness versus differentiation, and availability

Byron Sharp, "How Brands Grow" (2010), with Jenni Romaniuk, "How Brands Grow Part 2" (2016), and Romaniuk, "Building Distinctive Brand Assets" (2018), from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. The claims the evidence supports across categories: brands grow by acquiring many light buyers (people who buy from the brand once or twice a year at most); buyers see brands as more alike than marketers believe; loyalty follows penetration (the share of category buyers who buy the brand in a period), which is the double jeopardy law; differentiation as perceived by buyers is weak and short-lived.

Distinctiveness: being recognisable as yourself quickly. Differentiation: being meaningfully different in what you offer. Sharp's position is that distinctiveness is the reliable lever and differentiation is overrated. Mark Ritson's position is that both matter and that Sharp's evidence is strongest in packaged goods and other repertoire categories (where buyers rotate among several brands). The house follows Ritson's synthesis with Sharp's discipline: differentiate in the product and the position; be ruthlessly distinctive in the identity.

Distinctive brand assets (DBAs): the non-name elements (colour, shape, typeface, character, sound, pattern, tagline) that trigger the brand name in memory. Romaniuk measures each on two axes, fame (the percentage of category buyers who link the asset to the brand) and uniqueness (the percentage of those links that go only to this brand), and plots them on a grid with four quadrants: use or lose (high fame, high uniqueness: protect it and use it everywhere), investment potential (low fame, high uniqueness: spend to build it), avoid solo use (high fame, low uniqueness: the asset is also another brand's), and ignore or test (low, low). For a mature brand, the audit in 1.7 should end with this grid.

Mental availability: the propensity for the brand to be noticed or thought of in buying situations, built by linking the brand to many category entry points (CEPs: the cues a buyer starts from, such as "anniversary dinner", "gift for mother-in-law", "weekend in the hills"). Physical availability: how easy the brand is to find and buy where and when the buyer looks. For a premium brand with a single boutique, physical availability is the site, the Instagram DM and the WhatsApp line; the identity must be instantly recognisable in a 1080 by 1350 feed post because that is where the brand lives.

### 1.12 Brand equity and the identity models

Brand equity: the differential effect that brand knowledge has on how buyers respond to the brand's marketing (Kevin Lane Keller, Journal of Marketing, 1993). Put simply, the extra a buyer will pay, forgive or choose because the name is on it. Sections 7 and 9 are about protecting and measuring it. Three canonical models:

| Model | Source | Components | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand equity dimensions | David Aaker, "Managing Brand Equity" (1991) | Awareness, perceived quality, associations, loyalty, plus other proprietary assets (trademarks, patents) | A board conversation about what the brand is worth and why |
| Customer-based brand equity pyramid | Keller, 1993 paper; pyramid published 2001 (Marketing Science Institute) | Salience at the base; performance and imagery; judgements and feelings; resonance at the top | Diagnosing which level is weak: a brand with salience and no resonance needs different work from one with the reverse |
| Brand Identity Prism | Jean-Noël Kapferer, "Strategic Brand Management" (English edition 1992; "The New Strategic Brand Management", 5th edition 2012) | Six facets: physique and personality describe the sender; reflection (the buyer the brand shows) and self-image (how buyers see themselves using it) describe the receiver; relationship and culture bridge the two | Luxury and heritage brands, because the culture facet carries origin and history |

House rule, the same as for the platform in 1.2: use one model per client, make every facet forbid something, and fit it on one page. The prism is the house default for luxury hospitality, fashion and jewellery clients; the pyramid for a brand whose problem is diagnosed in research (9.3).

### 1.13 The premium playbook

Most Mask360 clients are premium, which is a strategy of its own. Michael Silverstein and Neil Fiske, "Luxury for the Masses" (Harvard Business Review, April 2003) and "Trading Up" (2003, with John Butman), named masstige and "new luxury": goods priced between mass and old luxury and bought for an emotional benefit. Kapferer and Bastien (1.10) separate three strategies: luxury (superlative and incomparable), premium (best in its category, compared and proved) and fashion (new each season). A premium brand may compare, cite proof, show price, promote within limits and make comparative claims, so its identity can carry a tagline, numbers and product benefits that luxury forbids. Indian premiumisation in luggage (Mokobara), coffee (Blue Tokai) and beauty is masstige in this sense.

How a premium brand treats each luxury code (8.1):

| Code | Premium treatment | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Monogram | Adapt: a pattern only after years of use on product | A new monogram is a lettermark with ambition (3.7) |
| Heritage date | Avoid without a real history | A date with no story reads as costume |
| Maison and the city | Adapt: the real city of making (Jaipur, Kochi, Dubai Design District) | Place works as proof only when it is true |
| Serif wordmark | Keep where the product's forms support it | The face follows the brand's history (7.5) |
| Colour ownership | Keep: the strongest lever at any price | Mokobara owns colour in a black category (7.3) |
| Restraint | Adapt: restraint in the identity, specifics in the copy | The premium buyer compares and needs the proof |
| Price | Show it | Premium wins the comparison on value |
| Logo prominence | Moderate and consistent | Han, Nunes and Drèze's tiers (8.1) |

## 2. Naming

A name is the only identity element that appears in speech, search, law and the URL at once. It is also the element clients change most reluctantly, so the process has to be rigorous enough that the final name survives the founder's spouse, the lawyer and the Hindi-speaking driver.

### 2.1 Types of name

| Type | Definition | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Says what the thing is | Bank of Baroda, Dubai Mall, Blue Tokai Coffee Roasters (the descriptor half) | Instant comprehension | Weak or unregistrable as a trademark (India Trade Marks Act 1999, section 9(1)(b)); no room to grow |
| Suggestive | Hints at a benefit or quality | Amazon (scale), Nykaa (from nayika, heroine), Ounass (Arabic for "people") | Meaning plus protectability | Hint can date or translate badly |
| Abstract or coined | Invented word | Kodak, Zoya, Mokobara, NEOM (Greek neos plus the M of Arabic mustaqbal, future) | Strongest trademark; empty vessel for meaning | Costs money to fill with meaning |
| Founder | A person's name | Sabyasachi, Hermès, Tata, Chanel | Provenance, authorship | The person's reputation is the brand's; hard to sell the company |
| Acronym or initialism | Letters | HDFC, LVMH, IHCL | Short | Meaningless until famous; many clash |
| Compound | Two words fused | Facebook, Snapdeal, PhonePe | Memorable, available | Can feel start-up generic |
| Place or heritage | A place or tradition | Jaipur Rugs, Amangiri (Sanskrit: aman, peace; giri, mountain), Raw Mango | Rootedness | Geographic names are hard to register (section 9(1)(b) again) |
| Metaphor | An object or idea standing in | Apple, Jaguar, Oberoi's Trident, Tiger (Sabyasachi's crest) | Story and image come free | Crowded: there are many Tigers and Lotuses on the register |

The spectrum of distinctiveness maps name types to how strongly the law protects them. It comes from the US case Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc. (537 F.2d 4, Second Circuit, 1976, Judge Henry Friendly), and Indian examiners apply the same logic under sections 9 and 32 of the Trade Marks Act 1999 (section 32 lets a mark refused as descriptive be registered on evidence of acquired distinctiveness, meaning buyers have come to read it as a brand through use):

| Rank | Category | Example | Protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1, weakest | Generic | "Coffee" for coffee | Never protectable |
| 2 | Descriptive | "Fresh Roast" for coffee | Only with acquired distinctiveness |
| 3 | Suggestive | Nykaa, Amazon | Protectable; the buyer makes a mental step |
| 4 | Arbitrary | Apple for computers, Jaguar for cars | Strong: a real word used out of context |
| 5, strongest | Fanciful or coined | Kodak, Mokobara | Strongest: no meaning to dispute |

Sound symbolism: the link between speech sounds and meaning that holds across languages. Wolfgang Köhler ("Gestalt Psychology", 1929) found that people match the invented word "takete" to a spiky shape and "maluma" (the 1947 edition's word) to a rounded one; V. S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard (Journal of Consciousness Studies, 2001) repeated it as "kiki" and "bouba", with 95 to 98% of people agreeing.

For names, Richard Klink ("Creating Brand Names with Meaning", Marketing Letters, 2000) showed that front vowels (i, e) read smaller, lighter, faster and more feminine than back vowels (o, u), and that fricatives (f, s, v, z) read smaller and faster than stops (p, t, k, b, d, g); Eric Yorkston and Geeta Menon (Journal of Consumer Research, 2004) showed these sounds shape product judgements without the buyer noticing. Use them in territory briefs: a light, quick serum wants front vowels and fricatives; a heavy watch wants back vowels and stops. The effects are probabilistic and Hindi and Arabic phonology (2.3) shifts them, so they guide generation and the linguistic check still decides.

### 2.2 The process

Eight steps, four to six weeks, run in parallel with strategy because the name must express the position:

1. Brief (2 days): position, audience, languages the name will be spoken in, markets and Nice classes it must be registrable in, hard constraints (must work as a handle, must survive a 16px favicon as an initial), names the client already loves and hates, and why.
2. Territories (3 days): five to seven conceptual directions ("the craftsman's tool", "a Rajasthani word for light", "an invented word with a hard consonant"), each with a rationale tied to the position and a sound brief (2.1).
3. Generation (1 week): 500 or more candidates across territories. Below 300 the shortlist is the designer's taste; above 500 the strong ones surface by comparison. Sources: dictionaries in every relevant language, etymologies, place names, trade vocabulary of the craft, mythologies, coinage by morpheme recombination, and generative models used as a thesaurus (they produce the obvious first; push them to the fourth and fifth round).
4. First screen (2 days): to 40 to 60. Kill criteria: more than four syllables, ambiguous spelling when heard, an existing famous brand in any category, an unintended meaning in Hindi, Urdu, Arabic, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil, Bengali, Punjabi, Malayalam or Kannada (the languages Mask360's audiences speak; section 2.3).
5. Linguistic and cultural check (1 week): native speakers, written report per language.
6. Availability (3 days): domain, handles, trademark pre-screen by class (section 2.4).
7. Shortlist of 8 to 12 with written rationale, pronunciation guide, a mock headline, a mock URL and a mock Instagram handle for each. Presented as section 2.5 describes.
8. Legal clearance (2 to 4 weeks with counsel) on the final three, in the order the client prefers. Never one name alone; one in three fails full clearance.

Naming specialists whose method is worth studying: Lexicon Branding (Sausalito, founded 1982 by David Placek; Pentium, PowerBook, BlackBerry, Swiffer, Dasani, Sonos), which runs parallel teams on the same brief, some told a decoy brief so they do not converge on the obvious; and Igor (San Francisco), whose free "Igor Naming Guide" sets out a name taxonomy and evaluation criteria.

### 2.3 Linguistic checks across Hindi, Arabic, Urdu and regional languages

The check is for four things in each language: an unintended meaning, a rude homophone, a pronunciation that breaks the name, and a script problem when transliterated.

- Hindi and Urdu share phonology and differ in script and register; check both, because a Mumbai audience hears Hindi and a Dubai South Asian audience reads Urdu-register words. Aspirated and retroflex consonants (the difference between d and dh, t and ṭ) change meanings; a Latin spelling cannot encode them, so the Devanagari and Nastaliq spellings must be fixed and written into the guidelines.
- Arabic: no p, v or hard g in Modern Standard Arabic (p becomes b, v becomes f, g becomes gh, j or k depending on dialect); a name with p or v will be respelt by every Gulf speaker. Short vowels are unwritten, so an unfamiliar name read in Arabic script can be vocalised several ways. Check Gulf, Levantine and Egyptian pronunciations separately: a Lebanese creative director and an Emirati buyer will say the name differently. Fix the Arabic spelling in the guidelines as a drawn wordmark (section 3.4), because a name left to be typed will be typed four ways.
- Regional Indian languages: at minimum Marathi and Gujarati for Mumbai, Rajasthani and Hindi for Jaipur, Konkani for Goa, Punjabi for Delhi; Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam when the brand sells nationally. Each check costs a few thousand rupees with a native-speaking copywriter; skipping it has cost brands relaunches.
- Transliteration tests: write the name in Devanagari and Arabic script and read it back cold with three speakers each. If two of three say it differently, the Latin spelling needs to change or the spoken form needs to be fixed by a tagline that contains it.

### 2.4 Availability and trademark pre-screening

Order of checks, cheapest first:

1. Domain: the exact .com, .in and .ae; then .co and .ae alternatives. A premium exact .com on the aftermarket runs from about USD 2,000 to USD 50,000 in 2026 depending on the word; budget for it or name around it.
2. Handles: Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn, X, TikTok (TikTok is unavailable in India since June 2020 and active in the UAE). Instagram handles are limited to 30 characters; aim for the exact name under 15.
3. Trademark pre-screen by class. Trademarks are registered per class under the Nice Classification (WIPO; 45 classes: 1 to 34 goods, 35 to 45 services; the 13th edition, version NCL(13-2026), came into force on 1 January 2026, and WIPO publishes a new version each January). A hospitality brand needs at least class 43 (hotel, restaurant services), 35 (retail, advertising) and often 3 (cosmetics, for amenities), 25 (clothing, for merchandise) and 41 (events).
   - India: the public search at the IP India portal (tmrsearch.ipindia.gov.in) offers wordmark search (starts with, contains, match with), phonetic search (a search on how the name sounds, the most important for Indian names because Indian examiners object on phonetic similarity) and Vienna code search for device marks. Search every relevant class with the name, its phonetic variants and its common misspellings.
   - UAE: the Ministry of Economy and Tourism (the trademark office; formerly the Ministry of Economy) runs the online search within its trademark e-services.
   - Saudi Arabia: the Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property (SAIP) runs online search and filing (2.6).
   - WIPO Global Brand Database (branddb.wipo.int): international registrations under the Madrid system plus many national collections; also an image similarity search for device marks.
4. Common-law use (rights earned by trading under a name without registering it): a Google search in quotes, plus the app stores and Instagram, for an unregistered business using the name in the same category. In India, prior use defeats a later registration (section 34 of the Trade Marks Act 1999), so an unregistered Jaipur boutique with the name since 2015 is a real obstacle.

A pre-screen filters the list; clearance is counsel's work (section 2.6).

### 2.5 Presenting names

Names die in the room for bad reasons: read in a list, in the wrong typeface, by a client who hears them for the first time with no context. Rules:

- Never a list. Each name on its own page: the name set in a neutral face at large size, the pronunciation, the territory it came from, two sentences of rationale, a mock headline, the URL and the handle, and the pre-screen result.
- Show it in use before showing it alone: on a shopfront, on a box, in a search result, in a WhatsApp message.
- Say each name aloud three times before discussing it. Most objections are to the first hearing.
- Present 8 to 12; recommend three; say which one the studio would choose and why.
- Forbid the "ask the team" email. Names polled by a staff of forty return the safest one.
- Record the client's objections verbatim; "it sounds like a pharma brand" is data about the category codes, and a cue for the next round.

### 2.6 The law of naming in India, the UAE and Saudi Arabia

As of October 2026. Verify fees on each registry's site and confirm every point with local counsel before quoting.

| Attribute | India | UAE | Saudi Arabia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Trade Marks Act 1999 (in force 15 September 2003); Trade Marks Rules 2017 | Federal Decree-Law No. 36 of 2021 on Trademarks (in force 2 January 2022) | GCC Trademark Law as implemented in the Kingdom, with SAIP's regulations |
| Registry | IP India (Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks) | Ministry of Economy and Tourism | Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property (SAIP) |
| Government fee | Rs 4,500 (individual, startup, small enterprise) or Rs 9,000 per class, e-filing | About AED 6,500 per class in total (UAE law-firm figures, 2025) | Not confirmed for this file; read SAIP's fee schedule |
| Multi-class filing | Yes | Yes, since 2022 | Confirm with counsel |
| Opposition window | 4 months from publication (Rule 42) | 30 days from publication | Confirm with counsel |
| Term | 10 years from application, renewable (section 25) | 10 years, renewable | Confirm with counsel |
| Madrid Protocol | Member since 8 July 2013 | Member since 28 December 2021 | Member since 8 October 2026 |

India:

- Absolute grounds for refusal (section 9; objections to the mark itself, whatever else is on the register): marks devoid of distinctive character, descriptive marks, customary terms, marks that deceive, that hurt religious susceptibilities, that are scandalous or obscene, or prohibited under the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act 1950 (which bars names and emblems such as the national flag, Ashoka Chakra, Mahatma Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, the UN and WHO, and the Prime Minister's name). Relative grounds (section 11; objections from conflict with an earlier mark): similarity to an earlier mark causing likelihood of confusion; well-known marks (marks a substantial segment of the relevant public knows) are protected across all classes.
- Fees (First Schedule, Trade Marks Rules 2017): Form TM-A per class; physical filing costs Rs 5,000 and Rs 10,000 against the e-filing rates in the table. Fees are per class per mark.
- Timeline: examination report typically in 1 to 3 months; publication in the Trade Marks Journal; opposition; registration in roughly 12 to 24 months if unopposed. The TM symbol can be used from filing; the registered symbol (R in a circle) only after registration, and its misuse is an offence under section 107.
- Company name: reserved separately through the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (SPICe+ Part A) under the Companies Act 2013 and Rule 8 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules 2014 (undesirable names, names implying government patronage). A reserved company name gives no trademark right and vice versa.
- Madrid: an Indian applicant can extend to the UAE and, from 8 October 2026, to Saudi Arabia through WIPO from an Indian base application.

UAE (fee figures from law-firm summaries; the house could not confirm the current schedule on the Ministry's own pages in October 2026, so it is re-checked on the e-services portal before any proposal):

- The 2021 law replaced Federal Law No. 37 of 1992. It introduced multi-class applications (the UAE and the other GCC states, the Gulf Cooperation Council of Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, were single-class jurisdictions before), recognised non-traditional marks (signs beyond words and images: 3D shapes, sound, smell, holograms) and added a grievance committee route before court.
- Prohibited: marks offending public morals or religion, religious symbols, names and flags of states and international organisations, Red Crescent and Red Cross emblems, geographic names that would confuse as to origin, and marks identical or similar to well-known marks.
- Fees reported as about AED 750 at filing, AED 750 for publication and AED 5,000 at registration.
- Madrid: the UAE acceded on 28 September 2021, with effect from 28 December 2021 (WIPO-Lex).
- Trade name versus trademark: a trade name is licensed by the emirate's economic department (Dubai's DET) or the free zone and must avoid religious terms, names of rulers, and foreign names without justification; it confers no trademark right. Arabic trade names are mandatory on signage with the Latin version, which affects lockup design (section 3.4).

Saudi Arabia:

- Registry: SAIP (saip.gov.sa), with online search and filing; its IP Gazette covers trademark applications filed from 19 December 2023 (read October 2026).
- Madrid: Saudi Arabia deposited its accession on 8 July 2026, in force from 8 October 2026 (WIPO-Lex, read October 2026). From that date an Indian or UAE base application can designate the Kingdom; before it, a Saudi filing had to be national.
- Trade names: a new Trade Names Law and Commercial Register Law were reported in force from April 2025; whether and how a trade name may carry Latin characters is a question for counsel under them.
- Signage: Arabic leads on commercial signage under municipal rules (verify the Riyadh and Jeddah signage regulations for size and position before artwork).
- Prohibited marks follow GCC norms: religious terms and symbols, state emblems and flags, and marks contrary to public order or morality.

House rule across all three: file the wordmark in black and white first (which, in Indian and UAE practice, covers all colours; a colour claim limits protection to those colours), file the device mark separately, and file in every class the business will plausibly enter within five years. Filing early is cheaper than renaming.

### 2.7 Renaming and transition

Changing a name is where value leaks: search rankings, recognition, legal rights and the habits of loyal buyers all attach to the old one. The transition method:

1. Dual branding for 12 to 24 months: "New, formerly Old" on the site header, the email signature, the app store listing and the fascia.
2. Keep the old domain for good and 301-redirect (permanently redirect) every old URL to its exact new equivalent, page by page; use the Change of Address tool in Google Search Console, and add the old name as an alternateName in the site's schema.org structured data so search and AI answers connect the two.
3. Park the old social handles. On day one, update the Google Business Profile, the app store listings and the WhatsApp Business display name.
4. Record the change of name against every existing trademark registration, and re-register every regulated label before the new name goes on sale (alcohol excise labels in India, 4.16).
5. Brief staff with the reason and the one sentence they will say when a guest asks.

Cases:

- Facebook Inc to Meta Platforms (28 October 2021): the corporate rename left the Facebook app's name intact, the same corporate-layer pattern as Zomato to Eternal (7.3).
- Etisalat to e& and back (2022 to 2026): on 24 February 2022 Etisalat Group renamed itself e& to present a technology and investment group, with the UAE telecom operator kept as "etisalat by e&", an endorsed-brand transition. On 6 October 2026, in its fiftieth year, the group announced in an Abu Dhabi Securities Exchange filing that it would return to the Etisalat name in the UAE and internationally, citing its heritage and its AI strategy (The National, 6 October 2026). Lesson: a name with five decades of fame was set aside for four years; the endorsed form kept the old name in daily use, which kept the way back open.

## 3. Logo and mark design

The logo is the smallest part of an identity and the one the client will judge the whole project by. It must work at 16 pixels and 16 metres, in one colour, embroidered, debossed, and read correctly by a stranger in a second. Taste rules for marks are in design-taste section 1 (principles 4 and 8) and section 5; this section covers construction.

### 3.1 Typology

| Type | What it is | Examples | Use when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wordmark (logotype) | The name set in custom or chosen letterforms | Google, Coca-Cola, Sabyasachi, Saint Laurent | The name is short and distinctive | Needs a separate device for small spaces |
| Lettermark | Initials as letterforms | HBO, IBM, HDFC | Long name, famous initials | Reads as finance or government |
| Monogram | Initials interlaced into one form | LV (1896 canvas), CC Chanel, GG Gucci, YSL (Cassandre, 1961), Loewe Anagram (Vicente Vela, 1970) | Luxury and fashion, where the monogram becomes a pattern | Crowded territory; the interlock is hard to draw well |
| Pictorial mark | A logo drawn as a recognisable object (Peirce's icon, 1.8) | Apple, Shell, the Air India jharokha window (2023) | The object carries the story | Literal objects date; an object in the name is redundant |
| Abstract mark | A non-representational form (Peirce's symbol) | Nike swoosh, Pepsi globe, Mastercard circles | Global brands that must avoid any one language | Needs decades of spend to mean anything |
| Mascot | A character used as a brand mark | Michelin Man (1898), Amul girl (1966), Air India Maharajah (1946), Reddit's Snoo | Warmth, story, social content | Needs a character bible and a 3D model in 2026 |
| Emblem | Type inside a shape, crest-like | Porsche crest, BMW roundel, Royal Enfield, Harley-Davidson, Burberry Equestrian Knight (1901) | Heritage, engineering, clubs | Illegible under 20mm unless a simplified version exists |

| Responsive suite | One mark drawn at 4 to 6 levels of detail for size | Joe Harrison's responsivelogos.co.uk demonstrations (2014) showed the method with Disney, Coca-Cola and others | Every brand in 2026; the favicon, app icon and billboard need different drawings | Teams deploy the wrong level |

### 3.2 Construction: grids, geometry and optical correction

A construction grid: the geometric scaffold a mark is drawn on. Use it to make decisions repeatable; it proves nothing about quality. The "golden ratio circles" overlay on the Twitter bird (2012) and the Apple mark started a decade of after-the-fact grids pasted on finished logos; a grid presented in a case study with 40 circles is a tell that the grid came last.

Geometry versus optics: a mark that is mathematically regular looks wrong, because the eye is not a ruler. Optical correction is the set of adjustments that make the form look regular (the perception behind them is in 02-perception-and-psychology.md):

| Correction | Rule of thumb | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Overshoot | Round and pointed forms extend past the baseline and cap line by about 1 to 3% of cap height (type designers use 2 to 3% at text sizes) | A circle that touches the line looks smaller than a square that touches it |
| Circle versus square | A circle of the same width as a square looks about 2 to 3% smaller; draw it that much larger | Area read by the eye is lower for the circle |
| Horizontal strokes | Draw horizontals about 5 to 10% thinner than verticals | Equal horizontals read heavier |
| Joins | Thin the strokes where two meet (the junction of a V or the crotch of an A) or cut an ink trap | Ink and pixels both gain weight at joins; the join reads as a blot |
| Optical centre | Place a centred element about 2 to 5% above geometric centre | The eye weights the upper half |
| Diagonals | A diagonal of equal stroke width reads heavier than a vertical; thin it slightly | Same reason as horizontals |
| Visual weight balance | Balance areas: a black circle next to a black square of equal width must be enlarged; a thin wordmark next to a solid mark must be set heavier or the mark lightened | Mass is what the eye weighs |
| Triangle and point | A point needs overshoot and a flat cap is cut off beyond the line | Acute forms vanish at the apex |

Work at 1,000 units cap height in Illustrator or Glyphs, correct at that size, then check at 24px and 8mm; corrections that are invisible at large size are what save the small one.

### 3.3 Clear space and minimum sizes

Clear space (exclusion zone): the margin around the mark that nothing else may enter. Define it from an element of the mark itself so it scales: the x-height of the wordmark, the cap height, or the height of the mark's main form. The common rule is clear space equal to the cap height on all sides, or the x-height on tight digital layouts; emblems need more (1.5 times the height) because their edges are busy.

Minimum sizes, with the physical reason:

| Context | Minimum | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Print, mark alone | 8 to 10mm wide | Offset printing loses strokes under about 0.25pt (0.09mm); at 8mm a hairline in the mark is already at that limit |
| Print, wordmark | 20 to 25mm wide for a 6 to 12 letter name | Letters need counters open at 300dpi |
| Print, emblem with text | 15 to 20mm, with a simplified version below that | Text inside a crest drops out first |
| Screen, mark alone | 24 to 32px | The thinnest stroke needs at least 1 device pixel at 1x; at 24px a mark with a stroke at 1/24 of its height is 1px |
| Screen, wordmark | 80 to 120px wide | Same reason, across more letters |
| Favicon | 16px, drawn separately | At 16px only a solid initial or a silhouette survives; Google requires a square of at least 8 by 8px for search results and recommends larger than 48 by 48px |
| Embroidery | 25mm minimum, no stroke under 1mm, no detail under 2mm | Thread width is about 0.4mm; satin stitch (long parallel stitches laid side by side to fill a narrow shape) needs at least 1mm |
| Engraving, debossing | No stroke under 0.3mm, no counter under 0.5mm | Tool radius |
| Signage | Legible at 10m per 25mm of cap height for a wordmark (a common signage rule) | Reading distance scales with cap height |

The responsive suite (3.1) exists because of this table: the full mark, a simplified mark, a glyph, and a favicon drawing.

### 3.4 Lockups, including bilingual and co-brand lockups

Lockup: the fixed relationship between mark and wordmark (and strapline, if any). Supply horizontal, stacked and mark-only versions; define the gap as a multiple of a mark unit; forbid new lockups in the guidelines.

Script adjustments for Mask360's markets, against a Latin wordmark:

| Script | Size against the Latin | Alignment line | Companion families |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arabic | 10 to 20% larger point size, then matched by eye | Shared baseline; Arabic has a smaller tooth height (its visual x-height), taller ascenders (alef, lam) and deeper descenders | 29LT, Boutros, TPTQ Arabic; Google's Noto Naskh Arabic and Noto Kufi Arabic for free |
| Devanagari | 5 to 10% smaller | The shirorekha (the headline bar the letters hang from) at or slightly below the Latin cap height; shared baseline | ITF's Kohinoor, Ek Type's Mukta and Baloo; Google's Noto Sans Devanagari for free |

Latin and Arabic:

- Placement. Arabic reads right to left, so in a horizontal bilingual lockup the Arabic sits on the right and the Latin on the left, each reading inwards from its own edge, with the mark in the middle or at the far left. In a stacked lockup the Arabic usually sits above: the Gulf convention on signage, and the UAE trade name rule puts Arabic on signage with equal or greater prominence (2.6).
- Weight. Match stroke weight by eye; equal point sizes give unequal weights.
- Typeface. Choose an Arabic face designed as a companion, or commission a drawn Arabic wordmark (designers to commission in section 11); a Latin wordmark with a default Arabic font under it reads as an afterthought and the Gulf buyer sees it instantly.
- Calligraphic style. Kufi (geometric, rectilinear) pairs with grotesks (sans serifs in the 19th-century German and British tradition, such as Akzidenz-Grotesk); Naskh (the rounded cursive book hand) pairs with serifs; Thuluth (large, sweeping, architectural) and Diwani (the dense, flowing Ottoman chancery hand) are display scripts for a crest or a seal. Type detail in 03-typography.md; production failures in 3.11.

Latin and Devanagari: align the shirorekha and the baselines as in the table so the two scripts share a band. Devanagari at the same point size looks larger than Latin because its full forms sit between the headline and the descender zone. Check every conjunct (two or more consonants joined into one glyph) in the name itself; a wordmark with a broken conjunct is a wordmark with a spelling mistake.

Three scripts (Latin, Devanagari, Arabic) occur on Indian hospitality brands in the Gulf; stack them in a fixed order (Latin, then the market script) and show at most two on any consumer surface.

Co-brand lockups. Collaborations, licences and partnerships are weekly work in hospitality, fashion and F&B. Rules:

- The joining word carries meaning: "x" for an equal collaboration, "by" for an authored line or a licence (Bugatti Residences by Binghatti), "for" for a commission, "with" for a partner.
- Match the two marks by optical cap height or optical area (3.2); bounding boxes mislead, because one mark's box holds more empty space than the other's.
- Divide them with a hairline rule or with the joining word set at about 50% of cap height. Each brand keeps its own clear space.
- The host brand leads on its own surfaces (on the left in Latin layouts, on the right in Arabic ones). Both brands approve the final lockup in writing.

Cases. Louis Vuitton x Supreme (shown in January 2017 at the Paris men's show) put Supreme's red box logo onto the Monogram at equal weight and brought a street code into the house. Fendi x Versace, "Fendace" (September 2021, Milan), was a swap: Kim Jones and Silvia Venturini Fendi designed for Versace and Donatella Versace designed for Fendi, and the two names fused into one word. Sabyasachi x H&M, "Wanderlust" (August 2021), sold out online within minutes and drew public complaints about fabric quality and price. Lesson: in a mass collaboration the partner's product quality becomes the luxury brand's quality, so the quality standard is written into the contract with the lockup.

### 3.5 Colour versions

Supply, in this order and with the rule for each: full colour (primary); mono black (print on one colour, fax-era but still used for forms, engraving and newspaper); reversed (white on the brand's dark colour and on black); one-colour brand colour; and, if the full-colour mark uses tints, a solid version for embroidery and vinyl. Specify Pantone solid coated and uncoated, CMYK, RGB, HEX and, for signage and paint, RAL (the German colour standard for paints and powder coatings) and NCS (the Natural Colour System, the Scandinavian standard architects specify). Colour system detail in 04-colour.md.

Accessibility. WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.4.3 exempts logotypes: "text that is part of a logo or brand name has no contrast requirement". The house still requires the reversed and one-colour marks to reach at least 3:1 against their grounds, so they survive glare and poor screens. Every SVG logo carries a title element (or an aria-label) with the brand name, and the header logo link is labelled with the brand name and "home". Method in 11-accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md.

### 3.7 The monogram tradition in luxury

Luxury's monograms are property marks that became patterns: Louis Vuitton's Monogram canvas (Georges Vuitton, 1896, drawn to defeat counterfeiters), Goyard's chevron (1892), Gucci's GG (1960s, from Guccio Gucci's initials), Fendi's FF Zucca (Karl Lagerfeld, 1965), Dior Oblique (Marc Bohan, 1967), Celine's Triomphe (1972, from the chain around the Arc de Triomphe), Burberry's TB (Peter Saville, 2018, since retired in favour of the knight).

The lesson for a new premium brand: a monogram works when it is a repeat pattern as well as a mark, when it interlocks two or three letters at most, and when the brand has the patience to put it on product for a decade before expecting it to mean anything. A monogram launched with the brand and used only on the website is a lettermark with ambition. Indian houses with a working monogram: Sabyasachi's Bengal tiger functions as a crest, with no interlocking letters; Zoya and Tanishq use wordmarks with a secondary device. The Gulf's working monograms live in hospitality (the Jumeirah and Emaar property marks) and in Arabic calligraphic seals.

### 3.8 Wordmark craft

A custom wordmark is the cheapest route to distinctiveness, because nobody else can own the letterforms. Craft rules:

- Start from a typeface with the right skeleton, then redraw: adjust widths, cut custom terminals, lower or raise the crossbars so the name's own letters harmonise (the R and the A in a name often need different angles than the font supplies).
- Kern by hand, pair by pair, at three sizes (16px, 48px, 300mm). Kerning is the adjustment of space between specific letter pairs; tracking is uniform spacing across a word, and it leaves the bad pairs bad. Watch the pairs that always fail: AV, AT, LT, Ta, Yo, rn (which reads as m at small size), and any pair with a round next to a straight.
- Check spacing by the squint test (blur the mark; the grey should be even) and by inverting (white on black exaggerates uneven gaps).
- Choose the skeleton by the brand's own history. The 2012 to 2019 convergence on sans wordmarks, the reversal from 2022, and the house rule on choosing between serif and sans are in 7.5.
- Draw the Arabic and Devanagari wordmarks with the same care (3.4), with a calligrapher or a script type designer; a Latin designer's instinct for stroke contrast does not transfer across scripts.

### 3.9 Trademark drawing

The trademark drawing is the version of the mark filed with the registry. Rules: a clean vector in black on white (covers all colours in India and the UAE; a colour filing limits protection), no clear-space marks, no gradient (gradients are described in words if claimed), the wordmark and the device filed as separate applications so each is protected alone, and the exact lockup filed if the lockup is the famous form. Figurative elements are classified under the Vienna Classification (WIPO; tenth edition, VCL10, in force since 1 January 2026); the examiner uses these codes to search for similar devices, which is why the pre-screen in 2.4 includes a Vienna code search. Provide the filing files as PDF and JPG at 300dpi, 8 by 8cm, which both registries accept.

### 3.10 Ownership, copyright assignment and AI-generated marks

A trademark protects the mark in trade; copyright protects the drawing as an artistic work; and by default they can sit with different owners. Under India's Copyright Act 1957, section 17, the author is the first owner of a work unless it was made under a contract of service (employment), so a logo drawn by an outside studio belongs to the studio until it is assigned.

The assignment must be in writing and signed (section 19(1)). If the deed is silent on term, the assignment is deemed to run for five years (section 19(5)); if silent on territory, it is deemed to cover India only (section 19(6)); and if the client does not exercise the rights within one year, the assignment is deemed to lapse unless the deed says otherwise (section 19(4)). A Mumbai client with a three-line email as its "assignment" owns a five-year, India-only licence to its own logo. In the UAE, Federal Decree-Law No. 38 of 2021 on Copyright and Neighbouring Rights likewise requires a transfer of economic rights to be in writing; counsel drafts the clause for each market.

House contract rule: on full payment the studio assigns all rights in the final mark, the final system and the delivered files, worldwide, for the full term of copyright, waiving the author's right to be named on the mark where the law allows; the studio keeps the rejected routes, its methods, and a portfolio right to show the work. Rejected routes are never offered to a competitor in the same category, because the client has seen them. Typefaces are licensed from the foundry: a wordmark built on a commercial font's outlines needs a foundry EULA (end user licence agreement) that permits logo and trademark use, and some foundries sell that as an extended licence, so the licence is read before the route is presented.

AI-generated marks are contested. The US Copyright Office's report "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability" (January 2025) holds that material generated wholly by AI from prompts is not protected, while human selection, arrangement and modification can be. India's Copyright Act names the author of a computer-generated work as "the person who causes the work to be created" (section 2(d)(vi)), which reads as more permissive but is untested for generative models.

Two further risks are practical: a model reproduces famous marks from its training data, and a device that resembles one surfaces only at the Vienna code search or at opposition. The house position: generative tools are used for exploration in the territories phase (method in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md); the final mark is drawn by hand in vector by a named designer; the working files are archived as evidence of human authorship; and no unmodified AI output is ever filed as a device mark. Contract and pricing practice is in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md.

### 3.11 Script production failures

Arabic is joined writing: each letter takes an isolated, initial, medial or final form depending on its neighbours, and software without a complex-script shaping engine sets it as disconnected letters in left-to-right order. Disconnected or reversed Arabic is the most common Gulf tell in agency work, and a Gulf buyer reads it in under a second. Rules:

- In Adobe Illustrator and InDesign, switch on the Middle Eastern and South Asian (Indic) language features and set paragraphs with the Adobe World-Ready Paragraph Composer (the composer that applies complex-script shaping). The default Latin composer also misplaces the Devanagari short-i matra (the vowel sign written before the consonant it follows in speech) and the reph (an r written as a hook above the following consonant).
- Check every Arabic and Devanagari string with a native reader on an outlined file, and pass it between tools as outlines; live text copied between applications is where shaping breaks.
- Never track or letter-space Arabic: spacing breaks the joins. Lengthen a word only with kashida (tatweel, U+0640, the horizontal extension stroke), and only between the letters where calligraphic practice allows it.
- Leave out diacritics (harakat, the short-vowel marks) unless the name is ambiguous without them, and fix the dot positions (i'jam) in the drawn wordmark.
- State in the signage guideline whether numerals are Western digits or Eastern Arabic-Indic digits (U+0660 to U+0669), and use one system per surface; mixed systems on a room-number sign read as an error.
- Check that the Devanagari font supports the name's conjuncts and nukta forms (the dot below a letter that marks sounds borrowed from Persian, Arabic and English), and that the shirorekha joins without gaps at display sizes.

Record the composer setting in the AI master's alignment layer; QA item 9 (12.1) checks it.

## 4. Identity system design

A logo is a signature; a system is the handwriting. The system is what lets a junior designer at the client produce a correct Instagram story at 11pm without calling the studio. Every element below is specified in its own corpus file; this section sets what the identity project must decide about each and how they lock together.

### 4.1 Colour system

Decide in the identity project: the owned colour (one; the Hermès orange, Tiffany blue, Bottega green model), the two to three working neutrals, the accent (one), the functional colours for digital (success, warning, error, which must never collide with the brand colours), and the dark-mode mapping. Specify every colour in Pantone, CMYK, RGB, HEX, and as design tokens (named values stored once and exported to code, Figma and the guideline; 6.2). Full method, contrast and token structure in 04-colour.md; taste caps (two plus one accent) in design-taste section 2.

### 4.2 Typography system

Decide: the primary face and its licence (desktop, web, app, and the number of page views or users the licence covers; web licences for a mid-size brand run from about USD 300 to 3,000 per year in 2026, and a custom typeface from a foundry from about USD 20,000 upward), the secondary face if any, the Arabic and Devanagari companions, the type scale, and the fallback stack for email and documents. The identity project also delivers the wordmark's relationship to the typeface (same family or deliberately different). Full method in 03-typography.md; pairing taste in design-taste, references/type.md.

### 4.3 Layout and grid system

Decide: the base unit, the margin ratio (the taste rule is in design-taste section 2), the logo position rule (one corner, always), the grid for the three master formats (A4 portrait, 1080 by 1350 feed, 1920 by 1080 screen), and the alignment rule for bilingual pages (mirrored grids for Arabic, where the margin logic flips). Full method in 05-layout-grids-and-composition.md.

### 4.4 Imagery and photography direction

Decide: the light (natural, one direction), the lens (the 35 to 50mm look for interiors and people, macro for product), the colour treatment (one grade, written as a LUT, a look-up table file that maps input colours to graded output, and a Lightroom preset; grading method in 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md), the subjects (what is never shown), the ratio of people to place to product, and the retouching rule (skin texture kept, no symmetry correction). Deliver a shot list with 20 to 40 reference frames and the grade. The art-direction skill (anthropic-skills:art-direction) carries the method for briefing a shoot; the identity project's job is the rule set the shoot is briefed from.

### 4.5 Illustration style

Decide whether the brand illustrates at all. If yes: the one style, the subjects it is used for and never used for (never for product in luxury; product is photographed), and one illustrator or a documented method so the style survives a change of hands. Fix the line weight as a ratio of the format, for example 0.25% of frame width (about 2.7px on a 1080px post, about 0.5mm on A4 portrait), so the line reads the same at every size; fix the fill rule (line only, flat, or two-tone) and the degree of abstraction with three reference drawings. Named example: Mailchimp's 2018 identity by Collins paired the Cooper Light wordmark and Cavendish Yellow with loose, hand-drawn illustration, which carried the brand when the logo was absent. Illustration taste (what is dead, what is rising) is in design-taste section 6.

### 4.6 Iconography

Decide: the grid (24px is the convention, with a 20px live area and 2px padding; Material Symbols use 24 with a 20 safe area), the stroke weight (1.5 or 2px at 24px, consistent), corner radius, terminal style (round or square), and the optical rule (circles and diagonals drawn slightly larger to match squares, as in 3.2). Deliver as an SVG set with a naming convention and a Figma component library. Icons must share DNA with the mark (terminal style, stroke contrast) or they look bought. Decide also whether the brand uses an existing open set (Phosphor, Lucide, Material Symbols) with a customised weight, which is the right answer for most clients because a bespoke set of 200 icons costs more than the logo.

### 4.7 Pattern and graphic devices

A graphic device: a recurring shape, line or pattern derived from the mark that carries the identity when the logo is absent. Air India's jharokha window (used since 1971 on aircraft, made the central device of the 2023 system), Burberry's check (1920s lining, registered as a trademark), Etihad's facet pattern (the 2014 identity by Landor, drawn from Abu Dhabi's desert and architecture), Reddit's speech-bubble counter. Rules: derive it from the mark or the name so it is defensible; specify it as a vector with a scale rule; give it a minimum and maximum size relative to the format; and ration it (a device on every surface becomes wallpaper in a year).

### 4.8 Motion identity

Decide: how the mark animates (one reveal, under 1.5 seconds, with the easing specified), the house easing curve, the transition vocabulary (two or three moves), the type behaviour (static, kinetic, or performative as in Studio Dumbar's work), and the sound pairing. Deliver the logo animation as Lottie and ProRes with alpha (3.6). Motion rules in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md; animation production and export in 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md.

### 4.9 Sound identity

Decide whether the brand has a sonic logo (a 1 to 3 second audio mark), a music palette and a voice (for IVR, interactive voice response, the automated phone menu; for video; for apps). A sonic logo in 2026 costs from about USD 15,000 with a specialist studio and is justified for hospitality groups and consumer apps, rarely for a single boutique. Method in 15-lighting-and-sound.md.

### 4.10 Verbal identity

Decide: the tone (1.4); the naming hierarchy (1.6); the house vocabulary (20 to 40 words used and 20 to 40 banned, each with its reason); the bilingual rule (which language leads on which surface, and whether Arabic is a translation or a parallel composition written by an Arabic copywriter from the same brief); and the strapline if there is one. Named example: Emirates carries its Arabic and English names as two drawn wordmarks of matched weight on every aircraft (7.4), and Dubai's trade-name rule puts Arabic on every fascia (2.6), so for a Gulf client the Arabic copy is written in parallel from the brief and signed off with the English. Method in 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md; house copy rules in the mask360-copywriting skill.

### 4.11 Packaging and print

Decide: the substrate family (paper weights, finishes, the one special process such as blind deboss, an impression pressed into the board with no ink or foil, or edge paint, colour applied to the cut edges of a thick board), the box architecture (rigid, folding, drawer), the ribbon or closure, the unboxing sequence, and the print colour management. Luxury's box is a distinctive asset with a higher fame score than most logos: the Hermès orange box was adopted during the Second World War when the usual cream card ran out; the Tiffany blue box and Cartier's red follow the same logic. Method, dielines and press specs in 20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md.

### 4.12 Digital and product

Decide: the design tokens (colour, type, space, radius, shadow, motion) exported from the identity so the product team builds from the same values; the component priorities (buttons, forms, cards, navigation); the accessibility floor (WCAG 2.2 AA contrast of 4.5:1 for body text, which constrains the pale luxury palette on screens; see 11-accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md); the dark mode; and the email system (the most-seen brand surface for hospitality, and the least designed). Method in 06-ui-design-craft.md and 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md.

### 4.13 Environmental and retail

Decide: the signage family (fascia, totem, directional, room numbers, statutory) with materials, the lighting temperature (2700 to 3000K for hospitality and jewellery, 3500 to 4000K for galleries and offices), the material palette (which should share the brand's colour logic), the wayfinding type size by viewing distance (3.3), the bilingual signage rule (UAE and Saudi Arabia: Arabic with the Latin, equal or larger), and the scent (section 10.5). The architect and the identity studio must share one material board; the usual failure is a brown-and-brass interior under a monochrome identity.

### 4.14 Uniforms and merchandise

Decide: who wears the mark and where (chest, cuff, collar; embroidered at 25mm minimum, 3.3), the uniform colour drawn from the palette's neutrals and kept off the accent (the accent is for moments), and the merchandise rule (what the brand will put its name on; a luxury brand puts its name on fewer things). Named examples: Manish Malhotra designed Air India's cabin and cockpit crew uniforms for the 2023 identity, unveiled in December 2023 in the identity's palette (7.3); Vivienne Westwood's uniforms for Virgin Atlantic (2014) made the uniform a press story of its own. Merchandise is a distinctiveness test: if the tote works with the logo covered, the system works.

### 4.15 The brand world beyond the logo

Collins (New York and San Francisco) and Koto (founded London 2015) work from the same premise: the identity is a world with its own physics (what moves, what sounds, what the light is like, what the brand would never do), and the logo is one object in it. Collins's Spotify identity (2015, with Spotify's in-house team) shipped the duotone image treatment as a vocabulary of moves before any logo rule; both studios' client lists are in section 11. The method, which the house follows:

1. Write the brand idea as one sentence of feeling (design-taste section 4 calls this the single idea).
2. Build the world's physics: light, material, motion, sound, humour, the forbidden list.
3. Design the five surfaces that matter most for this brand (for a hotel: arrival, the key, the room, the breakfast menu, the Instagram grid) before touching the logo.
4. Draw the mark to fit the world.
5. Write the rules from what was made, so the guidelines describe a working system, with every rule drawn from a made thing.

### 4.16 Statutory marks and regulated surfaces

Mask360's core sectors carry marks and numbers that the law puts on the layout. An identity that leaves no room for them breaks at regulatory review, so the master grid for a hoarding, a pack and a social ad carries a reserved statutory zone sized at layout stage, and the regulatory reviewer sees the layout before the client signs it.

| Market and sector | Mark or number | Rule | Identity consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| India, real estate | RERA registration number and the authority's website address | Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016, section 11(2): every advertisement or prospectus shows both prominently | A fixed field on every hoarding, print ad, social post and site page |
| Maharashtra, real estate | MahaRERA QR code | MahaRERA has required a QR code on project advertisements since 2023 (verify the current order on its site) | QR at least about 2cm on hand-held print, and about one-tenth of the reading distance on hoardings (a common scanning rule) |
| Dubai, real estate | Trakheesi permit number, with QR where required | Dubai Land Department permit before any property advertisement is published (verify the current DLD rule) | A permit field in every master layout |
| India, packaged food | FSSAI logo and licence number; veg mark (green square with a filled green circle) or non-veg mark (brown square with a filled brown triangle) | Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020 | A green or brown brand palette is kept away from the marks so it cannot be confused with them |
| UAE, food | Halal mark | UAE national halal scheme under the Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology (verify) | Space on pack and menu |
| India, alcohol | State excise label registration | Each state's excise department registers labels; a change of mark, or of the legal entity's name, means re-registration state by state before sale | Rebrands and corporate renames are scheduled around the excise calendar |
| India, gold jewellery | BIS hallmark and the six-character HUID (hallmark unique identification) | Mandatory in phases since June 2021 (8.2) | Space on tags, certificates and product pages |

The alcohol warning: Bira 91's maker converted from B9 Beverages Private Limited to B9 Beverages Limited ahead of a planned listing, which required fresh label registrations state by state; the Indian business press reported (2025) that sales stopped in several states for months and that FY2024 revenue fell by about 22% (verify figures before citing). The name change was a single word in a legal entity name.

## 5. Process for an identity project

### 5.1 Phases, durations and deliverables

A full identity for a premium brand runs 12 to 16 weeks; a refresh or a small brand runs 8 to 10. The durations below assume a studio team of a creative director, a strategist, two designers and a project lead at about 60% allocation, and a client who returns feedback within three working days.

| Phase | Duration | Activities | Deliverables | Sign-off gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Discovery and immersion | 2 to 3 weeks | 8 to 15 stakeholder interviews (founder, heads of sales, operations, the best front-line staff, 3 to 5 customers, 1 to 2 lapsed customers), the four audits (1.7), a competitive board, a half-day workshop on ambition and the forbidden list, site visits | Discovery report (20 to 40 pages), audit boards, interview synthesis, a one-page problem statement | Client agrees the problem statement |
| 2. Strategy | 2 to 3 weeks | Positioning options (2 to 3), platform, architecture decision, naming if needed (section 2), tone | Strategy document: positioning statement, onlyness statement, platform on one page, architecture diagram, verbal identity principles | Client signs the positioning; nothing visual starts before this |
| 3. Creative territories | 2 weeks | Three directions, each one idea, each a moodboard of 15 to 25 references with the borrowed thing named, a sketch of the mark, one applied surface, a line of copy | Territory presentation (3 directions) | Client picks one territory, with reasons recorded |
| 4. Concept development | 2 to 3 weeks | The chosen territory developed: 2 to 3 mark routes, type, colour, imagery direction, 6 to 10 applications each; route testing where agreed (5.5) | Concept presentation | Client picks one route; up to 2 rounds of amends |
| 5. Refinement | 1 to 2 weeks | The mark drawn to final (3.2, 3.8), optical tests at all sizes, colour versions, bilingual lockups, script checks (3.11), legal pre-screen on the device (2.4) | Final mark approval sheet (the mark at 10mm, 24px, 1m; all versions) | Mark sign-off; after this the mark does not change |
| 6. System build | 2 to 3 weeks | Colour, type, grid, imagery, icons, devices, motion, tokens, statutory zones (section 4) | System document, Figma library, token file | System review |
| 7. Applications | 2 weeks | The 15 to 30 surfaces the brand lives on, designed for real: site key screens, app icon, social grid for 12 posts, stationery, packaging, signage, uniforms, email, deck, menu or lookbook | Application set, print-ready and dev-ready | Application review |
| 8. Guidelines | 1 to 2 weeks | Section 6 | Guidelines (PDF and web), asset pack, file suite (3.6 and 12.2) | Guidelines accepted |
| 9. Rollout and launch | 2 to 8 weeks, client-paced | Launch protocol (5.4); phased replacement (digital day one, print as stock runs out, signage last); launch content | Rollout plan, launch assets, press kit, reversal criteria | Launch date |
| 10. Handover | 1 week | Training for the client's team (2 sessions), a 90-day support window, a quarterly review | Training deck, handover register, a named contact | Project close |

Rounds: two rounds of amendments per gated presentation, written into the contract, with a day rate for a third. Sign-off is in writing from one named decision maker; a committee cannot sign off a logo. Decision makers who join at phase 4 restart the clock to phase 2.

### 5.3 What goes wrong

- Visual work starts before positioning is signed; the client judges logos against an unwritten brief and every route loses.
- Three territories presented with one favourite; the client senses it and picks another to assert control. Present three the studio would be proud to build.
- Feedback by committee email. Insist on a live review with the decision maker.
- The mark drawn at 1,000px and never at 16. The favicon exposes it at launch.
- Arabic or Devanagari added in week 14. Scripts are designed in phase 5 with the Latin.
- The guidelines written as a theory. Write them from the applications made in phase 7.
- The logo launched alone, as a single image on social, with no plan for the first 72 hours (5.4).

### 5.4 Launch and backlash protocol

The first 72 hours decide how an identity is remembered; Gap, Jaguar and Cracker Barrel were judged there (7.1, 7.2). The protocol:

1. Staff first. The internal launch, with the story deck (6.4), happens at least a day before the public one.
2. Brief the design and trade press under embargo with the system, the applications and the rationale: Brand New, Dezeen, Creative Review, It's Nice That and Design Week; in India afaqs!, Campaign India, exchange4media and Kyoorius; in the Gulf Campaign Middle East and Communicate.
3. Launch the world: a film, five applications in use, and the product. A logo released alone invites a verdict on the drawing alone.
4. Prepare answers to the three questions every launch gets: what it cost, why change, and what happens to the old one.
5. Run social listening for 72 hours with a named owner, reporting volume, sentiment and the three most repeated objections twice a day (tools in 9.5).
6. Write and sign the reversal criteria before launch, and name who decides. Reverse only on evidence from sales, recognition (the distinctive asset test, 9.2) or law. Comment volume alone is never a criterion: Gap reversed in about a week on comment volume (7.2); Jaguar held its ground against comment volume and then met a real sales gap that had other causes as well (7.1).

### 5.5 Testing identity routes

Whether to research logo routes is contested, and clients raise it on almost every project. The positions:

- Kapferer and Bastien (anti-law 20, "Don't test"): consumer research cannot judge what it has not yet seen, and luxury leads taste.
- Ehrenberg-Bass (Romaniuk, 2018): test, for distinctiveness and for the recognition that existing buyers would lose.
- Common agency and client practice: preference testing ("which do you like?"), which rewards the familiar and kills the new.

House rule: test for comprehension, for legibility at a glance (the Kia failure, 7.2), for unintended readings in every market language and in every rotation, and for recognition loss among current buyers. Preference is never a test question. Methods: five-second tests (a route shown for five seconds, then recall and reading questions) in Lyssna (formerly UsabilityHub) or Maze, with 50 to 100 respondents per route; monadic exposure (each respondent sees one route only, so each route is judged on its own); rotation and inversion checks for accidental shapes; and an unintended-shape review by five people outside the team, including native speakers of the market languages. Myntra (7.3) is the case for the last check. For luxury clients the house tests legibility and unintended readings only, and follows Kapferer on the rest.

## 6. Brand guidelines

Brand guidelines: the document, site or system that records every rule the identity runs on, so that people who were not in the room can produce work that belongs. A guideline that nobody opens has failed at its one job. The guideline is written from the applications made in phase 7, which is why it comes after them in the schedule (5.1).

### 6.1 Structure

| Section | What it holds | Typical length | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Positioning statement, platform on one page, architecture diagram, audience in two paragraphs, the forbidden list | 4 to 6 pages | Twenty pages of workshop output nobody reads |
| Logo | Primary mark, versions (3.5), lockups, bilingual and co-brand lockups (3.4), clear space, minimum sizes (3.3), placement rule, misuse page with 8 to 12 real examples | 8 to 12 pages | A misuse page of imaginary crimes; the real ones are stretching and recolouring |
| Colour | Every colour in Pantone C and U, CMYK, RGB, HEX, RAL and NCS where signage exists; proportions (the 60 30 10 split or the brand's own); approved text-on-colour pairs with WCAG ratios; dark-mode mapping | 4 to 6 pages | Six accents with no proportion rule, so every designer uses all six |
| Typography | Families, weights, licences and who holds them, the scale by format, bilingual rules and script production rules (3.11), fallbacks for email and Office | 6 to 8 pages | A face nobody at the client has a licence for |
| Layout | Grids for the three master formats (4.3), margins, logo position, the bilingual mirrored grid, the statutory zone (4.16) | 4 to 6 pages | A grid drawn for A4 and applied to 1080 by 1920 |
| Imagery | Photography direction, 20 to 40 approved frames, 6 to 10 rejected frames with the reason, retouch rule, illustration style if any | 8 to 12 pages | Stock frames shown as examples because the shoot has not happened |
| Generative use | Where generated imagery is allowed (mood, backgrounds, with approval) and forbidden (product, people presented as real, the mark, the type); approved style inputs (Midjourney style reference codes, an Adobe Firefly custom model trained on approved frames where licensed, the Canva Brand Kit); prompt formulas with forbidden terms; disclosure rules (platform AI labels; India's draft rules on labelling synthetically generated content, published October 2025, status to be checked); the archive of human-authorship evidence (3.10); method in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md | 2 to 4 pages | No rules, so off-brand generated output becomes the main source of drift |
| Iconography | The set, the grid, stroke, naming, where icons may and may not appear | 2 to 4 pages | Icons from three open sets mixed |
| Motion | Logo animation, the easing curve as a value (for example cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1)), durations by move, examples as video files and Lottie | 2 to 4 pages plus files | Motion described in adjectives |
| Voice | Principles, the six sentences in six situations (1.4), vocabulary used and banned, bilingual rule, examples by content type | 6 to 10 pages | Adjectives with no sentences |
| Applications | The 15 to 30 surfaces from phase 7 shown finished, with specs | 10 to 20 pages | Mock-ups of surfaces that were never built |
| Masters | Editable files: deck, document, social formats, email, menu, signage | Files, with a one-page index | Masters that drift from the guideline within a month because they were made last |
| Do and do not | 12 to 20 paired examples across all elements | 2 to 4 pages | Only logo misuse; colour and type misuse are more common |
| Crisis mode | A monochrome mark and avatar set made in advance; a dark site banner; the list of campaigns and scheduled posts to pause, with a named owner; the tone for statements (10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md); the conditions for returning to colour | 1 to 2 pages plus files | Made on the day of the crisis, by whoever is online |
| Contacts and governance | Who approves what, the version number, the change log, where the assets live, the licence register (every licensed asset with its holder, scope, renewal and cost; 6.4) | 1 to 2 pages | No owner named, so the guideline freezes at launch |

A premium single brand's guideline runs 40 to 80 pages as a PDF; past about 120 pages it stops being read, because the people who need it are designers at the client working at speed. A group with several brands splits into a master guideline and one 20 to 30 page guideline per brand.

### 6.2 Digital-first guidelines

Digital-first guideline: a guideline published as a website, with assets downloadable from the page they are described on, and values held as design tokens (named values for colour, type, space, radius, shadow and motion, stored once and exported to Figma, code and the guideline itself; method in 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md). Three reasons the house delivers digital-first and treats the PDF as an export:

1. A PDF is out of date the day the first new application ships; a site is edited in an hour.
2. "Can you send the logo" is the most common client email after a launch; a guideline site with the file suite (3.6) on the logo page ends it.
3. A token change (a hex value, a spacing step) propagates to the product, the Figma library and the guideline from one edit, so the three cannot drift.

Platforms as of October 2026: Frontify (Swiss, founded 2013; the most used brand guideline platform among larger brands, with asset management and editable masters built in), Bynder (asset management first), Brandpad and Standards (standards.site; lightweight guideline sites for studios), Zeroheight (design system documentation that reads Figma libraries), Supernova (tokens and documentation together), and a static site built by the studio (cheapest, needs a developer for every change). A Frontify guideline for a single brand runs from about USD 3,000 to 10,000 a year in licence, which a Mask360 client must accept before the format is chosen; a studio-built static site costs once and dates slowly.

A living system goes one step further: the Figma library, the code components (06-ui-design-craft.md), the token file and the guideline are one versioned thing, released together with a change log. This is right for a client with a product team and wrong for a boutique with one designer; for the boutique, a 40-page PDF, a Canva brand kit and a shared folder with the file suite is the right deliverable.

### 6.4 The agency deliverable format

## 7. Rebrand case studies

Rebrand: a change to the identity, from a refresh (the same assets redrawn) to a replacement (new name, mark or system). Each case below is read for one lesson, with the facts dated. Verdicts on taste live in design-taste; this section records what happened and why, and 7.7 turns the cases into a decision rule.

### 7.1 Luxury fashion

- Yves Saint Laurent to Saint Laurent (2012). Hedi Slimane renamed the ready-to-wear house Saint Laurent Paris, set it in a plain grotesk and moved the design studio to Los Angeles. The press and the fans objected; the Cassandre YSL monogram (1961) stayed on beauty and accessories, where the money was. Kering's reports show the house's revenue roughly doubling across Slimane's tenure (about EUR 470 million in 2012 to about EUR 1.2 billion in 2016; verify against Kering's annual reports before quoting). Lesson: the "new" name was the 1966 Rive Gauche name, so the rename was a return; a famous monogram can be retired from one surface and kept where it earns.
- Balenciaga (2017). Under Demna, a wide, plain sans wordmark (credited to Bureau Borsche, Munich) replaced the previous lettering, and the logo moved from the label to the product (the logo hoodie, the cap). The November 2022 campaign crisis showed that an identity's restraint cannot absorb a creative misjudgement in a campaign, and that a brand with no crisis protocol (6.1) improvises in public. Lesson: in the blanding era the wordmark became merchandise; the identity's job was to be printable at 400mm across a chest.
- Celine (2018). Slimane dropped the accent from Céline, redrew the wordmark in a 1960s-referencing sans, wiped the brand's Instagram and started again. The #oldceline reaction lasted about two seasons; the Triomphe (1972) was revived as a hardware and canvas asset and became the house's best-selling signifier. Slimane left in 2024; Michael Rider was appointed creative director in October 2024. Lesson: the accent was an asset with fame and should have been kept; the dormant Triomphe was the asset that mattered; deleting the archive injured the brand more than the wordmark did, because a luxury brand's history is its moat.
- Bottega Veneta (2018 to 2026). Daniel Lee (2018 to 2021) made a green (the "Bottega green") and the intrecciato (the house's hand-woven leather) the identity, kept the wordmark small and plain, and in January 2021 deleted the brand's social media accounts, replacing them with a quarterly digital magazine, Issue. Matthieu Blazy (2021 to 2025) carried the method on product and was announced as Chanel's artistic director in December 2024; Louise Trotter took over in 2025. Lesson: absence can be a code; the distinctive assets here are a colour and a texture, and the brand bought mental availability by leaving the channel every competitor used.
- Burberry (2018, then 2023). Riccardo Tisci's 2018 identity (Peter Saville: a sans wordmark and a TB monogram) was replaced in February 2023 under Daniel Lee by a serif wordmark referencing the house's earlier lettering and the Equestrian Knight, which Burberry dates to a public competition circa 1901 and had dropped in 2018. Saville told Dezeen (13 June 2025) that replacing the 2018 system before it had fully rolled out, which left "at least three different Burberrys circulating in the world", was "totally and utterly irresponsible". Lesson: the 2018 system threw away assets with high fame; the 2023 system brought them back; the sales decline of 2023 to 2024 traced to product and price, and the identity was the fastest and cheapest thing to fix, which is why it was fixed first.
- Ferrari (the case for leaving the mark alone). The Cavallino Rampante came from the aviator Francesco Baracca's emblem, offered to Enzo Ferrari by Baracca's mother in 1923, and first appeared on cars in 1932 on the canary yellow of Modena. Two badge formats persist: the shield for Scuderia Ferrari and the rectangle for road cars. Ferrari's Centro Stile has been led by Flavio Manzoni since 2010, and the brand's expansion into fashion (first collection shown at Maranello in June 2021 under Rocco Iannone) left the mark untouched. The house found no verifiable logo redesign through October 2026. Lesson: a brand whose product is the identity extends into new categories (fashion, Maranello 2021) and keeps the mark fixed.
- Zara (2019). Baron & Baron (Fabien Baron) set the wordmark in a high-contrast Didone (the serif style of Bodoni and Didot: hairline serifs, vertical stress) with overlapping letters. The design press called it illegible; the fascia and the bag are where a fashion retailer's wordmark lives, and there it worked; it is still in use in 2026. Lesson: the internet's verdict and the shopper's verdict differ; judge a wordmark on the surfaces that carry it.
- Jaguar (2024 to 2026). On 19 November 2024 Jaguar released a film with no car under the line "Copy nothing", a mixed-case "JaGUar" wordmark, a redrawn leaper, a pastel palette and the phrase "exuberant modernism"; the Type 00 concept was shown on 2 December 2024 at Miami Art Week.
  - Sales: the company had already wound down its model line ahead of an all-electric relaunch, so the registration collapse that followed (49 cars in Europe in April 2025 against 1,961 a year earlier, per data from ACEA, the European automobile manufacturers' association, reported by Motor1) was partly planned and partly the cost of a gap with nothing to sell. A cyberattack on JLR in September 2025 halted production for weeks.
  - People: Gerry McGovern, the chief creative officer who led the reset, was asked to leave on 1 December 2025 (Dezeen, 4 December 2025), weeks after PB Balaji took office as JLR's chief executive (appointment announced in 2025, effective November 2025); no reason was given.
  - Product: the production four-door GT, the Type 01, was revealed in New York on 6 October 2026, with production due to start at Solihull in 2027 (model year 2028).
  - Lesson: a rebrand with no product makes the identity the product, and the identity broke every category code at once, so the existing buyer had nothing to hold; the sequence (film, then concept, then a near two-year wait for a production car) ran backwards; distinctiveness was bought by discarding every asset with fame.

### 7.2 Global consumer and technology

- Pepsi (2023). PepsiCo's in-house Design and Innovation team under Mauro Porcini returned the wordmark to the globe, in black and an electric blue, with a custom typeface, rolling out in North America from autumn 2023 and globally through 2024; the first change in 14 years. The black reads as Pepsi Zero Sugar's colour promoted to the master brand. Lesson: the 2008 Arnell identity (the "smile", with its leaked rationale of golden ratios) is the cautionary case; the 2023 mark is the 1970s to 1990s asset redrawn to pulse in motion, so the return was a return of fame.
- Kia (2021). The new wordmark's joined letterforms read as "KN"; Carthrottle reported about 30,000 US Google searches a month for "KN car" after the launch. Lesson: legibility is tested with people who have never seen the name (5.5); a wordmark is read at a glance by a stranger, and elegance that costs a letter costs the brand.
- Reddit (2023). Pentagram (Natasha Jen's team) rebuilt Snoo as a 3D character with a range of expressions, redrew Reddit Sans so the counters take a speech-bubble shape, drew a companion headline face, Reddit Display, with the same bubble counters, and cut the secondary palette from more than 100 colours to 15 (five hues in three shades each) around OrangeRed, ahead of the March 2024 listing (pentagram.com, read October 2026). Lesson: a mascot is a distinctive asset that needs a character bible and a 3D model; cutting a palette from 100 to 15 is where the system work is.
- Johnson & Johnson (2023). On 14 September 2023 the company replaced its 136-year-old script signature with a red sans wordmark whose letters are drawn as single pen strokes, keeping the red and the ampersand, after the consumer health business was separated as Kenvue (whose own identity Wolff Olins designed). Lesson: architecture drove identity; the script had high fame and poor screen legibility, and once the consumer products left, the parent needed to look like a healthcare company.
- Twitter to X (2023). On 23 and 24 July 2023 the bird (its 2012 drawing by Martin Grasser with Todd Waterbury and Angy Che) was replaced with an X glyph taken from a user's post, with no system behind it; the verb "tweet" outlived the logo. Lesson: an asset with near-total fame was discarded for an owner's preference, and the verbal assets (tweet, retweet) were not planned for; the rename without a system is the most expensive kind.
- Tropicana (2009). In January 2009 PepsiCo replaced the Pure Premium carton's orange pierced by a straw with Arnell Group's design: a glass of juice, a cap shaped like an orange, and the name set vertically. Sales of Pure Premium fell about 20% between 1 January and 22 February 2009, about USD 33 million (IRI data reported by Ad Age, 2009), and PepsiCo announced the return of the old pack on 23 February 2009. Lesson: on a shelf the pack is the logo; loyal buyers navigated by the orange and the straw, and could no longer find the product.
- Gap (2010). On 4 October 2010 a Helvetica wordmark with a small gradient blue square replaced the blue box; after online protest it was withdrawn on 11 October 2010 and the blue box returned. Lesson: a mark changed without a strategy or a system has nothing to defend it, and the reversal on comment volume alone taught the market that the brand could be pushed.
- Cracker Barrel (2025). In August 2025 the restaurant chain replaced its logo with a text-only wordmark that dropped the "Old Timer" figure leaning on a barrel, the illustration it had used since 1977; the new logo ran from 19 to 26 August 2025 and was withdrawn after about a week of customer backlash with a political edge, alongside the cancellation of planned restaurant remodels. Lesson: in a heritage category the figure was the distinctive asset with the most fame; on Romaniuk's grid (1.11) it sat in "use or lose", and the company lost it for a week.

### 7.3 India

- Air India (2023). FutureBrand's identity, unveiled in New Delhi on 10 August 2023 by Tata Sons chairman N. Chandrasekaran: "The Vista" mark drawn from the peak of the jharokha window frame used on the fleet since 1971, a palette of deep red, aubergine and gold, a chakra-derived pattern, a custom Air India Sans, and the Maharajah mascot (1946) kept with a new look in a reduced role. The first aircraft in the livery, an Airbus A350-900, was scheduled to join the fleet in December 2023 (AirlineGeeks, 10 August 2023). Lesson: a heritage asset was transformed and kept, and the public argument was about the demoted mascot, which proves it was the asset with the most fame; the livery is the largest surface and was designed first.
- Air India AI171 (June 2025). On 12 June 2025 flight AI171, a Boeing 787-8, crashed shortly after take-off from Ahmedabad. The airline's public voice in the first days was the Tata Group chairman's statements and the compensation commitments, and the airline was reported to have switched its social profile images to black and paused promotional content (verify before citing). Lesson: an identity needs a prepared crisis mode (6.1), because the brand's colours and campaign calendar keep running unless someone has the authority and the files to stop them.
- Zomato to Eternal (2025). The board approved renaming the listed parent Eternal Ltd on 6 February 2025; shareholders approved in March 2025 with over 99% of votes in favour; the ticker moved to ETERNAL and the consumer brand Zomato kept its red wordmark alongside Blinkit, Hyperpure and District. The name had been used internally since the 2022 Blinkit acquisition. Lesson: corporate and consumer names separate when a holding owns more than one consumer brand (1.6); the consumer mark did not move.
- Cred (2018 onward). Kunal Shah's credit-card payments app took luxury's codes (black, white, restraint, no tagline, slow motion) into fintech and open-sourced its NeoPOP design system in 2022, while its campaigns (Rahul Dravid as the "Indiranagar ka gunda", 2021) did the opposite. Lesson: a quiet identity and loud campaigns are a workable tension when both are deliberate and the identity holds still while the campaign moves.
- Myntra (2021). After a complaint to the Mumbai Police cyber cell in December 2020 alleged that its M logo was offensive to women, the fashion platform changed the mark's shape and colour in late January 2021. Lesson: run an unintended-shape review with people outside the team (5.5) before launch, because a reading nobody in the studio saw becomes the only reading once it is public.
- Tata Neu (2022). The group's superapp launched on 7 April 2022 with a purple identity and NeuCoins rewards, endorsed by the Tata name across groceries, flights, hotels and jewellery. Lesson: an architecture problem (one app for a dozen categories with their own brands) cannot be solved by an identity; the brand first had to mean a reward programme before it could mean anything else.
- Mokobara (2020 onward). The Bengaluru luggage brand made colour the asset in a category of black suitcases, with a lowercase wordmark and premium pricing. Lesson: in a category with no colour code, own the colour range; the product is the medium.
- Blue Tokai Coffee Roasters (2013 onward). Founded in Delhi by Matt Chitharanjan and Namrata Asthana; every pack names the estate, the altitude and the roast date, under a peacock-feather mark. Lesson: transparency broke the commodity-coffee code and the pack carried the identity; the name's descriptor half (Coffee Roasters) did the explaining so the mark could stay quiet.
- India's place brands. Incredible India (2002), developed for the Ministry of Tourism by Ogilvy & Mather under V. Sunil with Amitabh Kant at the ministry, uses an exclamation mark as the i of India and set the pattern state tourism campaigns still follow. Kerala Tourism's "God's Own Country" line dates from the late 1980s (credited to Walter Mendez at Mudra Communications; verify). The rupee sign, designed by D. Udaya Kumar and adopted by the Union Cabinet on 15 July 2010, joins the Devanagari ra and the Latin R with a double bar: a national identity act in two scripts. Lesson for hotel clients in Goa and Jaipur: a property's identity borrows the state brand's codes or deliberately counters them, and state co-marketing comes with lockup rules of its own (3.4).

### 7.4 The Gulf

- Emirates (1985 onward). The Arabic calligraphic wordmark, paired with a serif Latin "Emirates", has stayed in place for four decades on the largest surfaces in aviation. Lesson: calligraphy drawn once and well is a permanent asset; the livery is the medium.
- Etihad (2014). Landor's identity (Landor was Landor & Fitch from 2021 to 2023 and is Landor again, part of WPP) drew a facet pattern from the UAE's desert and Abu Dhabi's architecture, in sand and gold, with a modern calligraphic Arabic wordmark. Lesson: a national carrier's identity is a nation's; the pattern system did the differentiating that a wordmark could not, because every Gulf carrier has a calligraphic wordmark.
- Saudia (2023). In September 2023 the Saudi national carrier returned its logo and livery to its 1970s design, a heritage move in step with Vision 2030's story of national pride (the agency was not confirmed for this file). Lesson: in the Gulf, a carrier's own past is the most defensible code it owns.
- Riyadh Air (2023 to 2025). Announced by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman on 12 March 2023, the new carrier showed its first livery, in purple, on 4 June 2023 and flew its first commercial service on 26 October 2025. Lesson: a carrier built as a brand two and a half years before its first flight, with a colour that steps away from the region's gold and green aviation codes.
- e& (2022 to 2026). Etisalat Group's 2022 rename to e&, with "etisalat by e&" as the endorsed operator, was the UAE's clearest architecture case, and its return to the Etisalat name on 6 October 2026 is now its clearest reversal (method and lesson in 2.7).
- Emaar. The master brand sits on every tower it builds (Burj Khalifa, Dubai Mall), and endorses its hotel brands (Address, Vida, Palace, and Armani Hotel Dubai as a co-brand). Lesson: real estate's identity is the skyline; the sign on the building is the logo, and the endorsement system (1.5) lets each hotel keep its own codes.
- Visit Dubai. The Department of Economy and Tourism (formed by merger in 2021) runs the city brand as a wordmark with its Arabic form and a campaign engine ("Dubai Presents", 2021, with Jessica Alba and Zac Efron). Lesson: a city brand is a wordmark and a release schedule; the city supplies the imagery, so the identity's job is to stay out of the way and be recognisable in the corner of a film.
- Saudi Vision 2030 brands. Vision 2030 (announced April 2016) sits above a house of brands funded through the Public Investment Fund (PIF): NEOM (announced October 2017; the name from neo, new, plus M from the Arabic mustaqbal, future), The Red Sea, Diriyah, Qiddiya and ROSHN, each a place brand for one of the giga-projects (the term for PIF-funded developments at the scale of a city or region) built before the place exists. Above them sit the Saudi Tourism Authority's "Saudi, Welcome to Arabia" (2022) and Riyadh Season, the General Entertainment Authority's annual festival since 2019, which runs a new identity each edition with a release cadence of announcements, line-ups and nightly content across a season of several months.
  - The NEOM warning: chief executive Nadhmi Al-Nasr was replaced by Aiman Al-Mudaifer in November 2024 (The Times); the Financial Times reported The Line substantially scaled down (November 2025) and the wider project being scaled back (January 2026); the 2029 Asian Winter Games moved from Trojena to Almaty, Kazakhstan (Deutsche Welle, 2026); and in April 2026 the PIF governor Yasir Al-Rumayyan was reported as saying The Line was not vital before 2030.
  - Lesson: state-funded brand building at a scale no private client reaches, with a house of brands under a parent most buyers never see; a place brand with no place yet lives on renders and film, the arena Mask360's Gulf clients compete in for attention. A place brand promises the place, so when the build programme slips the identity carries the credibility gap; write place-brand platforms around what already exists (the coast, the heritage, the people) as well as the renders.

### 7.5 The blanding era and the reversal

Blanding: the convergence of luxury and technology wordmarks on near-identical geometric or grotesk sans between about 2012 and 2019 (Saint Laurent 2012, Balenciaga 2017, Burberry 2018, Celine 2018, Berluti 2018, Balmain 2018; in technology, Google 2015, Airbnb 2014 and a generation of start-ups set in Circular or Inter). The term is usually credited to Thierry Brunfaut of Base Design (2018). The reasons were real: an app icon at 60px, a feed post at 1080px, global legibility, and a cost-free way to signal "modern".

The cost was distinctiveness: on Romaniuk's grid (1.11), ten houses with the same sans share one asset, so the fame of each is also the fame of the others. The reversal began with Ferragamo's 2022 wordmark (Peter Saville, which dropped "Salvatore") and Burberry's 2023 serif and knight, and by 2026 the serif is the dominant luxury code (1.8) and a sans is the emergent one again; design-taste, references/currency-2026.md lists expressive serifs as rising and warns against the cream-and-serif default. The house rule: the face follows the brand's own history; a brand without a history takes the skeleton its product's forms already have.

### 7.6 The Brand New review lens and how to read a jury

Brand New (underconsideration.com/brandnew) has reviewed identity work daily since 2006; it is run by Bryony Gomez-Palacio and Armin Vit from Bloomington, Indiana, and was publishing daily as of 29 October 2026. The format is fixed and worth copying for internal critique: the before and after side by side, the mark at small size, the wordmark alone, the applications, the designer's own rationale quoted, then a verdict on drawing, spacing, system and story, followed by a reader poll (Great, Fine, Bad). What the lens sees well: craft (a badly drawn join, uneven spacing, a lockup that fails at 24px), the gap between a studio's rationale and its result, and the case study film's tricks. What it cannot see: the strategy, the business reason, the constraints, how the client's staff will use the system, and sales. Jaguar (7.1) and Zara (7.1) were widely panned there and both remained in market; Brand New's "Bad" is a craft verdict with no information about commerce. The house critique format is in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md.

Reading any jury (D&AD, Cannes Lions Design, the Transform Awards, Kyoorius in India, Dubai Lynx in the Gulf): entries are judged from a case study film and a board, so the studio's framing is part of the work; juries reward systems, motion and a single idea told in under two minutes; a studio's awards tell a client that its peers rate it, and nothing about whether it will listen. The house uses juries as a currency signal (what the profession rewards this year, cross-referenced with design-taste, references/currency-2026.md); a jury result says nothing about whether an identity worked.

### 7.7 When to rebrand and what to keep

Clients ask "should we rebrand?" constantly. The decision rule drawn from the cases above:

| Trigger | Response | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Merger, demerger or separation | Replace or re-architect | Johnson & Johnson and Kenvue, 2023 |
| Repositioning into a new tier | Replace or substantially evolve | Burberry, 2023 |
| Legal conflict over the name or mark | Replace the conflicting element only | Pre-screen failures (2.4) |
| Architecture change | Rename the corporate layer; keep consumer marks | Zomato to Eternal, 2025; Facebook to Meta, 2021 |
| The mark fails at small sizes or on screens | Refresh: redraw and build the responsive suite (3.1) | Mastercard, 2016 |
| Dated execution | Refresh | Pepsi, 2023 |
| A new owner, CMO or creative director wants a change | Not a trigger on its own | Twitter to X, 2023 |

Method: run Romaniuk's fame and uniqueness test (9.2) on every current asset first. Anything in "use or lose" is redrawn and kept: Burberry's knight, Pepsi's globe and the Air India jharokha survived their rebrands. The Twitter bird, Tropicana's orange and straw and Cracker Barrel's Old Timer were discarded, and two of the three were back within weeks. Mastercard is the model of patience: it dropped the name from the interlocking circles in January 2019 only after its research showed that more than 80% of people recognised the symbol without the word.

## 8. Luxury brand identity codes

Codes here are the signs the luxury category shares (1.8), listed so that the house can decide which to keep, which to break, and which to borrow for a premium client without faking the economics (1.10, 1.13).

### 8.1 The European codes

| Code | Canonical example | What it signals | How it is misused |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monogram | LV (1896), GG, CC, Triomphe (1972), Loewe Anagram (1970); see 3.7 | Property, lineage, a pattern that sells without a logo | A two-year-old brand launching a monogram on a website |
| Heritage date | "Depuis 1854" (Louis Vuitton), "Maison fondée en 1837" (Hermès), Tiffany's 1837 | Time as proof; the brand outlived its first customers | "Est. 2023" set in a crest; a date with no story behind it |
| Maison and the city | "Maison", "Paris", "Milano", "London" under the wordmark | Place as guarantor of craft; the city is a reason to believe | A Dubai brand writing "Paris" because the founder studied there |
| Serif and the 2023 reversal | Burberry 2023, Ferragamo 2022, the Didones of Dior and Valentino | Continuity, print, the hand; sans signalled "modern" from 2012 to 2019 and signals "everyone" in 2026 | A serif chosen from the default list; see design-taste, references/type.md |
| Colour ownership | Hermès orange (the box, 4.11), Tiffany Blue (1845 Blue Book; Pantone 1837 custom), Bottega green (2019), Valentino's PP Pink (2022, with Pantone), Cartier red | One saturated colour, held for decades, that triggers the name without it | Three accents; a colour chosen for a season; a palette |
| Restraint | A mark under 10mm on a 300mm page (the houses that do it are in design-taste, references/canon.md) | Confidence; price expressed as space | Restraint without an idea, which reads as empty |
| Logo prominence | Han, Nunes and Drèze (2010): in Louis Vuitton and Gucci handbags and Mercedes-Benz and BMW cars, the cheaper models carried the louder branding | Quiet signals for peers who can read them; loud ones for buyers who want to be seen | The largest logo on the most expensive product |
| The campaign film | One film a season, a named director (Sofia Coppola for Dior; Wes Anderson for Prada's "Candy", 2013; Jonathan Glazer for various houses) | Closeness to the arts (anti-law 17); the dream (anti-law 9) | A product video with a piano track; see 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md |
| The lookbook | White or grey backdrop paper, one crop, every look at the same scale | The collection as a document; the buyer's tool made public | Lifestyle shots mixed into the lookbook, so neither function works |
| The boutique | Peter Marino for Chanel, Dior, Louis Vuitton; the facade as the largest ad in the city | The store is the medium where selling happens (anti-law 9) | A boutique designed by the mall's fit-out contractor |
| The box | Hermès orange, Tiffany blue, Cartier red (4.11); Louboutin's red sole (registered as a shape and colour mark in several jurisdictions) | The gift is the brand; the box outlives the purchase on a shelf | A box with a printed logo on a stock carton; see 20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md |

Logo prominence is evidence, beyond taste. Young Jee Han, Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze (Journal of Marketing, 2010, on brand prominence) sorted buyers into four types: patricians (wealthy, low need for status, want quiet signals only peers can read), parvenus (wealthy, high need for status, want loud signals), poseurs (high need for status without the means, the market for counterfeits) and proletarians (who do not signal). Jonah Berger and Morgan Ward ("Subtle Signals of Inconspicuous Consumption", Journal of Consumer Research, 2010) showed that insiders prefer subtle signals because outsiders cannot read them. The 2023 "quiet luxury" wave (Loro Piana, The Row, Brunello Cucinelli) was this cycle turning. House rule: logo size on product falls as price rises; entry products may carry the monogram, and the top tier carries a code insiders read (a weave, a colour, a piece of hardware).

### 8.2 India's luxury codes

India's dominant luxury code is abundance held under discipline, which inverts the European code of restraint; the emergent code is restraint with a single Indian material.

- Sabyasachi (founded 1999, Kolkata): the Bengal tiger crest, the heritage prints, the maximalist interiors (the Mumbai flagship at Horniman Circle, the New York store on Christopher Street from 2022), the jewellery line, the Sabyasachi Art Foundation. The brand builds a world (4.15) at a density no European house attempts, and the discipline is in the repetition: the same tiger, the same wall colour, the same mannequin, in every city. Lesson: world building, with one crest held constant.
- Raw Mango (Sanjay Garg, 2008, Delhi): handloom saris, a near-empty frame, a lowercase wordmark, a palette drawn from the cloth. The brand broke the bridal code (gold, red, ornament) with plain weaves and silence, and became the emergent code's reference. Lesson: restraint reads as luxury in India when the material is unmistakably Indian.
- Zoya (Titan, 2009): fine jewellery sold in boutiques designed as salons, with one-to-one appointments and a quiet wordmark. Lesson: service design (section 10) as the identity in a category where product photography is interchangeable.
- Tanishq (Titan, 1994): the T mark, the Rivaah bridal sub-brand, the wedding films, and the trust codes (purity, buy-back) in a category where trust was local and family-held. Lesson: a national brand in jewellery is built on trust mechanics made visible, and the identity carries them.
- A code specific to Indian jewellery: the BIS hallmark and HUID (4.16) are trust marks the identity must accommodate on tags, certificates and product pages, and a premium jeweller's packaging design starts from where they sit.
- The Indian hospitality codes: the Taj's palace heritage (Taj Mahal Palace, Mumbai, 1903), the Oberoi's service codes, Aman's Indian properties (Aman-i-Khás, Amanbagh) as the restraint reference, and the new boutique hotels of Goa and Jaipur, where the emergent code is the single material (lime plaster, terrazzo, cane) and a hand-drawn mark.

### 8.3 Gulf codes

- Arabic calligraphy done well: the Emirates wordmark (1985), the Dubai Expo 2020 logo (drawn from a 4,000-year-old gold ring found at Saruq Al Hadid, unveiled 2016), the Etihad wordmark. The code is a wordmark that is calligraphy first and a translation second; the tell is a Latin wordmark with an Arabic font typed under it (3.4, 3.11).
- Amouage (Oman, founded 1983): the perfume house whose bottle references the khanjar dagger and the minaret, relaunched under creative director Renaud Salmon from 2019 with European perfumery's codes (the flacon, the campaign, the maison) and Omani materials (frankincense). Lesson: Gulf heritage expressed through the category's global codes, with the local material as the proof.
- Ounass (Al Tayer Group, 2016): luxury e-commerce for the Gulf, named from the Arabic for "people", with a bilingual lockup and a delivery promise measured in hours for Dubai. Lesson: in the Gulf, service speed is a luxury code, and the identity's job is to look as considered as the brands it sells.
- Bilingual lockups and the mandatory Arabic trade name on signage (2.6, 3.4) are a legal fact and a design code at once; the houses that do it well give the Arabic equal craft and often the leading position.
- Gold is the dominant colour code of Gulf luxury; the emergent code is matte, sand, stone and dark green, visible across the Saudi giga-project brands (7.4) and the newer Dubai hospitality launches. The house breaks gold quietly: one gold detail on a matte ground reads as the Gulf without reading as a souk.
- The heritage and future duality: the Gulf's place brands run two codes at once, the Nabataean tombs of AlUla and the renders of The Line, Sharjah's heritage districts and Dubai's towers. A brand picks one as its ground and borrows the other as its tension (design-taste section 1, principle 5).

## 9. Brand measurement

What the identity work is accountable for, and what it cannot be. Numbers here are the ones the house can defend in a client review.

### 9.1 Awareness and salience

Awareness: the share of the target who know the brand exists. Unaided (spontaneous) awareness: named without a prompt ("which jewellers in Jaipur can you name"). Aided (prompted) awareness: recognised from a list. Top-of-mind: named first. Salience: Ehrenberg-Bass's refinement, the propensity to be thought of across buying situations, measured by asking which brands come to mind for each category entry point (1.11) and counting the links.

Jenni Romaniuk's "Better Brand Health" (Oxford University Press, 2022) gives the current salience metrics: mental market share (the brand's share of all brand-to-CEP links in the category), mental penetration (the share of category buyers who link the brand to at least one CEP) and network size (the average number of CEPs linked to the brand among buyers who link any). Method: find 10 to 15 CEPs with the 7W framework (why, when, where, while, with whom, with what, how feeling), ask category buyers which brands come to mind for each, and track the counts. For a Goa hotel, mental penetration on "monsoon weekend from Mumbai" is the number that matters.

Sampling arithmetic the house states before any tracker is sold: at a 95% confidence level (the share of repeated samples whose interval would contain the true value), a sample of 300 carries a margin of error (the half-width of that interval) of about plus or minus 5.7 percentage points on a 50% result, a sample of 1,000 about 3.1 points. A quarter-on-quarter awareness move of 4 points on n = 300 is noise. Trackers for premium brands therefore run two to four waves a year with n at 500 or more per wave, or they run annually.

### 9.2 Distinctive asset tracking

Romaniuk's method (1.11), run annually for a mature brand: show each asset without the name (the colour swatch, the pattern, the mark's silhouette, the typeface in a neutral word, the sonic logo) to category buyers and ask which brand it belongs to. Fame is the share who name the brand; uniqueness is the share of all names given that are this brand. Plot on the grid. An identity project for a mature brand should move at least one asset into the "use or lose" quadrant within two years, or the system has decoration in it.

### 9.3 Brand tracking surveys

| Tracker | Method | Cadence | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouGov BrandIndex | Daily online panel, metrics such as Buzz, Impression, Consideration, Current Customer | Continuous | Large consumer brands; India and UAE panels exist |
| Kantar BrandZ | Consumer survey plus financial data; Meaningful, Different, Salient | Annual | Valuation and the Difference metric, which is the nearest industry measure of differentiation |
| Interbrand Best Global Brands | Financial analysis, role of brand, brand strength; the 2026 edition (released 28 October 2026) leads with Apple at USD 514.6 billion, Microsoft USD 443.7 billion and Google USD 389.3 billion, with NVIDIA entering the top 10 at number 6 (USD 74.0 billion, up 71.5%, the largest gain in the top 10) | Annual | A valuation lens and a methodology to borrow for a client's own brand strength score (ten factors) |
| Brand Finance Global 500 | Royalty relief valuation (valuing a brand as the licence fee its owner avoids paying by owning it) | Annual | Valuation for boards |
| Custom tracker | Studio-written questionnaire, 12 to 20 questions, run through a panel (Kantar, Ipsos, YouGov, or a self-serve panel) | 2 to 4 waves a year | Premium brands with a definable buyer universe; cost in India from a few lakh per wave with a panel, more with recruitment of high-net-worth respondents |

The questionnaire a premium tracker needs: unaided and aided awareness, salience by three to five category entry points, consideration, the distinctive asset test, two to three image statements tied to the positioning, and a net promoter question (how likely, from 0 to 10, the respondent is to recommend the brand). Anything longer costs completion rate.

### 9.4 Share of search

Share of search: a brand's share of all search queries in its category (brand searches divided by the sum of searches for every brand in the set), proposed as a leading indicator of market share by Les Binet at the IPA's EffWorks Global in October 2020, using Google Trends data across categories including cars, energy and mobile phones. Share of search correlated with market share and moved ahead of it by months, with lead times that varied by category. Tools: Google Trends (relative index, free, 0 to 100), Google Ads Keyword Planner (monthly volumes), Google Search Console (the brand's own branded queries), and Semrush or Ahrefs for competitor volumes. Caveats: ambiguous names (Apple, Jaguar the animal), the Kia effect (7.2: searches for "KN car" are brand searches nobody attributes), and categories where the buyer searches on Instagram, where Google's data cannot see them, which includes most of Mask360's. The house reports share of search monthly for clients with a definable competitor set of 4 to 8 brands.

### 9.5 The limits of brand metrics for small premium brands

A Jaipur jeweller with 2,000 relevant buyers a year or a Goa hotel with 40 keys cannot be measured by a national panel; the relevant respondents are too few and too rich to sit on one. What works, reported monthly on one page:

- Branded search volume trend (Keyword Planner and Search Console), with the exact name and its misspellings.
- Direct and branded traffic share of site sessions.
- "How did you hear about us" asked at checkout, booking or the first WhatsApp message, coded into six answers.
- Instagram profile visits, saves and shares per post (saves signal intent for premium goods), DM and WhatsApp enquiry counts.
- Press and creator mentions, counted and weighted by the audience's fit.
- Social listening for share of voice and sentiment: Brandwatch, Talkwalker (acquired by Hootsuite in 2024), Meltwater or Sprinklr; WhatsApp and Instagram DMs are invisible to all of them.
- Platform brand-lift studies (Meta Brand Lift; Google and YouTube Brand Lift), which measure ad recall and awareness against a holdout group that saw no ads; eligibility needs a minimum spend, so check each platform's help centre for the current threshold before proposing one.
- Price realisation: average discount depth and the share of sales at full price (an identity that works lets the brand discount less).
- Repeat and referral rates.
- Resale prices where a secondary market exists (jewellery, bags, watches): the clearest external verdict on a luxury brand's equity.

The statement the house puts in a proposal: the identity will be judged on these proxies, on the first impression chain (10.2) walked quarterly, and on the brand's ability to hold price; a tracker is sold only when the buyer universe is large enough to sample.

### 9.6 Effectiveness and budget

The evidence a creative director uses to defend brand budgets against performance spend. Les Binet and Peter Field, "The Long and the Short of It" (IPA, 2013), drawn from the IPA Databank of effectiveness case studies: the average optimum split is about 60% brand building to 40% sales activation, varying by category. For B2B, "The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing" (Binet and Field for the LinkedIn B2B Institute, 2019) puts it at about 46% to 54%. Brand effects build over six months and longer and compound; activation effects are strong at once and decay within weeks.

Excess share of voice (ESOV: share of voice, the brand's share of category advertising, minus share of market) predicts growth: on average about 0.5 percentage points of annual market share for every 10 points of ESOV (Binet and Field, from the IPA data). John Philip Jones set out the relationship in "Ad Spending: Maintaining Market Share" (Harvard Business Review, 1990). The limit: these are databank averages for advertised brands in competitive categories. For a single-property premium client the house uses them to set the direction of the budget conversation and builds no forecast on them.

## 10. Brand experience design

Brand experience: the sum of what a buyer meets, in the order they meet it. The identity system (section 4) is the vocabulary; the experience is the sentence. Method for hospitality in 08-ux-research-and-strategy.md; this section carries the identity director's part of it.

### 10.1 Touchpoint mapping

Touchpoint map: every touchpoint listed by stage (aware, consider, buy, use, return, advocate), with its owner at the client, a photograph of its current state, a score of 1 to 5 for brand fit and 1 to 5 for function, and its frequency. A hotel has 60 to 120 touchpoints; a jewellery brand 40 to 70. Prioritise by frequency multiplied by emotional weight (the arrival weighs more than the invoice; the invoice is seen more often). The top 15 to 30 become the applications of phase 7 (5.1). Most identity projects fail at the long tail: the OTP (one-time password) SMS, the parking ticket, the hold music, the "do not reply" email.

The weighting rests on two findings. The peak-end rule: an experience is remembered by its most intense moment and its end, with its length largely neglected (Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End", Psychological Science, 1993; Redelmeier and Kahneman on colonoscopy patients, Pain, 1996). Design the peak (arrival, the unboxing, the first course) and the end (checkout, the final invoice, the departure) deliberately; the middle has to be correct.

Moments of truth: Jan Carlzon, who turned SAS around in the early 1980s, called each brief contact between a customer and the staff a moment of truth that decides the brand ("Moments of Truth", English edition 1987). P&G named the shelf the First Moment of Truth (2005), and Google's Jim Lecinski named the research before it the Zero Moment of Truth ("Winning the Zero Moment of Truth", 2011). The Zero Moment maps onto links 1 and 7 of the first impression chain (10.2); the First Moment onto links 4 and 5.

### 10.2 The first impression chain

The buyer meets the brand in a sequence, and the weakest link sets the impression. For a premium brand in 2026:

1. The search result: the title tag (under about 60 characters so it is not cut), the meta description (about 155 characters), the favicon (3.3), sitelinks, and for hospitality and retail the Google Business Profile (photos, hours, reviews), which is the brand's largest unmanaged surface. Review stars are read before the logo is.
2. The site: the hero as the single idea; Core Web Vitals (Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint under 200ms, Cumulative Layout Shift under 0.1, which are Google's thresholds) because a slow luxury site reads as a cheap one; craft in 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md.
3. Social: the first nine tiles of the Instagram grid, the highlight covers, the bio line, and the pinned posts; the grid is the lookbook for most of Mask360's clients and is audited as one (platform craft in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md).
4. Arrival: the approach, the door, the greeting within 30 seconds, the smell, the key or the fitting-room; for e-commerce, the confirmation page.
5. Packaging: the box, the tissue, the card, the sequence of opening; the surface most photographed by the buyer.
6. Email: the confirmation and the receipt are the most-read brand documents in hospitality and e-commerce and the least designed; they carry the identity on the smallest budget and highest open rate of anything the brand sends.
7. The AI answer: what ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google's AI Overviews say when a buyer asks about the brand by name or by category ("best boutique hotel in North Goa"). It is assembled from the site, the press, the reviews and the Google Business Profile, so links 1 to 3 feed it, and for a growing share of buyers it now comes before link 1. Run the same 10 prompts quarterly and record the answers; method in 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md.

The chain is walked in the audit (1.7), in phase 7, and quarterly after launch, by someone who did not design it, with a phone camera.

### 10.3 Service design for hospitality

Service blueprint (G. Lynn Shostack, Harvard Business Review, 1984): a diagram of the service across time with the customer's actions on top, the front-stage staff actions below the line of interaction, the back-stage actions below the line of visibility, and the support processes beneath. The identity director's contributions: the moments that matter (arrival, the first problem, departure), the staff as identity (uniform in 4.14, the greeting script that is forbidden, the vocabulary from 4.10), the printed and digital artefacts at each step, and the rule that brand standards (how it looks and sounds) and service standards (what happens) are written in one document, because a guest meets them as one thing. Full method in 08-ux-research-and-strategy.md.

### 10.4 Retail and spatial identity

The store is the medium where luxury sells (anti-law 9); the facade is the biggest advertisement the brand will ever own. Decisions the identity project must make with the architect (4.13): the fascia (in a Dubai mall, the landlord's tenant design criteria govern signage height, illumination and projection, so the lockup must have a version that passes them), the lighting temperature, the material board, the scent, the sound, the fitting-room (the most intimate brand surface), the point of sale (the receipt, the bag, the wrap) and the window programme (a calendar of 6 to 12 changes a year). Indian flagships to study for world building: Sabyasachi's Mumbai and Kolkata stores, Good Earth's Mumbai store, the Nicobar stores; Gulf: the Dubai Mall Fashion Avenue facades, Alserkal Avenue's galleries for the opposite register.

### 10.5 Scent and sound

Scent branding: a signature fragrance diffused in the brand's spaces, developed with a scent house (12.29 by Dawn and Samantha Goldworm; Air Aroma; ScentAir) and delivered through the HVAC (the building's heating, ventilation and air-conditioning) or standalone diffusers. Westin's White Tea (2006) is the best-known hotel case; the Edition hotels use Le Labo. Development costs run from about USD 10,000 upward per scent with a specialist house, plus diffusers and refills, by industry quotes; the rules are one scent, low intensity, kept out of food and beverage spaces, and documented in the guidelines with the supplier and the concentration. Sound: the sonic logo (4.9), the playlist programme (curated by a music agency or in-house, refreshed monthly, with a rule for time of day), the hold music, the notification and the door chime. Method in 15-lighting-and-sound.md.

### 10.6 Consistency versus coherence

Consistency: the same assets reproduced the same way everywhere. Coherence: the same character recognisable everywhere, with behaviour adapted to the medium. The positions: the mass-market school (and most guideline writers) prizes consistency because it is auditable; the brand-world school (Collins, Koto, 4.15) prizes coherence because a brand that behaves identically on a billboard and in a WhatsApp message reads as a machine. The house follows coherence with a fixed core and a flexible layer: the mark, the owned colour and the typeface are fixed; tone, imagery, motion and layout flex by surface within written ranges. The test: a stranger shown five touchpoints with the logos covered says they come from one house, and each touchpoint behaves correctly for its medium.

## 12. Identity QA checklist and file deliverables

### 12.1 QA checklist

Run before the final mark approval (phase 5) and again before handover (phase 10). Every item is a yes or the file does not ship.

Strategy and naming

1. The positioning statement and the onlyness statement are signed by the named decision maker, dated.
2. The name passed linguistic checks in every language the brand will be spoken in (2.3), with written reports on file.
3. Domain (.com, .in or .ae as relevant) and the Instagram handle are registered to the client, with the credentials handed over.
4. Trademark pre-screen done in every relevant Nice class in India (IP India phonetic search), the UAE and, where the brand trades there, Saudi Arabia, with Vienna code search on the device; counsel's clearance on file for the final name.
5. Every purpose, pillar and tagline claim has its evidence on file (1.2).

The mark

6. Drawn at 1,000 units with optical corrections (3.2), then checked at 24px, 16px favicon, 8mm print, 25mm embroidery and 1m signage.
7. Clear space defined from an element of the mark; minimum sizes stated for print, screen, embroidery and engraving (3.3).
8. Full colour, mono black, reversed and one-colour versions exist and each has been tested on a photograph, on the brand dark colour and on white; the reversed and one-colour marks reach 3:1 against their grounds and every SVG carries a title with the brand name (3.5).
9. Lockups: horizontal, stacked, mark only, bilingual and co-brand (3.4). Arabic and Devanagari verified as outlines by a native reader; no tracking applied to Arabic; conjuncts verified in the name itself; the composer setting recorded in the AI master (3.11).
10. The wordmark is kerned by hand at three sizes; the squint test and the inverted test pass (3.8).
11. Unintended-shape review done: the mark rotated through 90, 180 and 270 degrees, inverted, and shown to five people outside the team, including native speakers of the market languages (5.5).
12. The system survives with the logo covered: the colour, type and device alone identify the brand on a feed post (design-taste section 4, test 1).
13. No gradient, glow, bevel or shadow in the master mark; any effect is a separate, named variant.
14. Trademark drawing exported in black on white, 8 by 8cm, 300dpi PDF and JPG, with the wordmark and the device as separate files (3.9).

The system

15. Every colour is specified in Pantone C and U, CMYK, RGB, HEX and as a token; text-on-colour pairs pass WCAG 2.2 AA (4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text) and the pairs that fail are listed as decorative only.
16. Typeface licences cover desktop, web and app at the client's traffic, in the client's name, with renewal dates in the licence register.
17. Arabic and Devanagari companions chosen and tested in the three master formats; the mirrored grid for Arabic exists.
18. The type scale, grid and margin rules are proven in A4, 1080 by 1350 and 1920 by 1080, with real copy in both languages.
19. Photography direction documented with approved and rejected frames, and the grade delivered as a LUT (.cube) and a Lightroom preset (.xmp).
20. Icon set shares terminal style and stroke logic with the mark; exported as SVG with a naming convention.
21. The logo animation is under 1.5 seconds, delivered as Lottie, ProRes 4444 with alpha and H.264, with the easing curve written as a value.
22. Design tokens exported as JSON and loaded into the Figma library; the token names match the guideline.
23. Dark mode mapping defined for every colour and tested on the app icon, the site header and an email.

Applications and guidelines

24. The 15 to 30 priority touchpoints from the map (10.1) are designed for real, with print-ready and dev-ready files, and the first impression chain (10.2) walked end to end on a phone.
25. The confirmation email, the OTP SMS and the invoice carry the identity.
26. Every master layout for hoardings, packs and social ads carries a reserved statutory zone sized for the marks in 4.16, and the regulatory reviewer has seen it.
27. Signage meets the Arabic trade name rules (2.6) and the landlord's tenant criteria where the brand is in a mall.
28. The physical files exist: embroidery (DST or PES), single-line, DXF, seal and 3D model (3.6).
29. The guideline is written from the applications, versioned 1.0, dated, with an owner, a change log, the licence register, the generative-use rules and the crisis-mode set with a named owner (6.1).
30. The asset pack follows the folder structure in 6.4 and opens on a machine that has none of the studio's fonts installed.
31. The launch protocol and the signed reversal criteria exist before the launch date (5.4).
32. A stranger, given the guideline and the pack, produces a correct Instagram story in under 30 minutes. If they cannot, the guideline is rewritten.

## Terms introduced

- 7W framework: Romaniuk's prompt set for finding category entry points: why, when, where, while, with whom, with what, how feeling.
- Absolute grounds for refusal: objections to a trademark arising from the mark itself, such as descriptiveness, deception or offence.
- Abstract mark: a non-representational logo form, Peirce's symbol, which means only what spend teaches.
- ACEA: the European automobile manufacturers' association, whose registration data tracks car sales in Europe.
- Acquired distinctiveness: the state of a descriptive mark that buyers have come to read as a brand through use, which makes it registrable.
- AI answer: what generative assistants and AI search summaries say about a brand, treated as a link in the first impression chain.
- Aided awareness: recognition of a brand from a prompted list.
- Anti-laws of marketing: Kapferer and Bastien's 24 rules for luxury that invert mass-marketing practice.
- Arbitrary mark: a real word used out of context as a name, such as Apple for computers.
- Archetypes: twelve recurring character types (Mark and Pearson, 2001) used to describe brand personality.
- Avoid solo use: Romaniuk's quadrant for an asset with high fame and low uniqueness, shared with another brand.
- Awareness: the share of the target who know a brand exists; unaided, aided or top-of-mind.
- Blanding: the 2012 to 2019 convergence of luxury and technology wordmarks on near-identical sans serifs.
- Blind deboss: an impression pressed into board with no ink or foil.
- Brand architecture: the system that organises how a company's brands relate to each other and to the parent.
- Brand audit: an inventory of every live brand expression with judgement, in four lenses: visual, verbal, experiential, competitive.
- Brand equity: the differential effect that brand knowledge has on how buyers respond to the brand's marketing (Keller).
- Brand experience: the sum of what a buyer meets, in the order they meet it.
- Brand guidelines: the document, site or system that records every rule the identity runs on.
- Brand house: the diagram that arranges platform elements with essence at the top and pillars beneath.
- Brand Identity Prism: Kapferer's six-facet model: physique, personality, relationship, culture, reflection, self-image.
- Brand Key: Unilever's one-page brand model, running from competitive environment and target to discriminator and essence.
- Brand on a page: a single sheet carrying the positioning, mark, palette, type, images and voice.
- Brand platform: the written set of foundational statements (purpose, vision, mission, values, personality, promise, proposition, pillars, essence) a brand operates from.
- Brand tracker: a repeated survey measuring awareness, salience, consideration and image over time.
- Brand world: the identity understood as a world with its own physics, where the logo is one object.
- Brand-lift study: a platform survey that measures ad recall and awareness in an exposed group against a holdout group.
- Branded house: one master brand across every offer, with descriptors.
- Branded residences: homes sold under a hospitality or fashion brand licensed to a developer, with the licensor approving every touchpoint.
- Cash cow: a portfolio brand that funds the others with little investment.
- Category codes: the signs a category shares that let a buyer recognise it in under a second.
- Category design: the Play Bigger method of naming a problem and a new category and being seen as its leader.
- Category entry points (CEPs): the cues a buyer starts from when a need arises, which a brand links itself to.
- Clear space (exclusion zone): the margin around a mark that nothing else may enter, defined from an element of the mark.
- Co-brand lockup: the fixed arrangement of two brands' marks with a joining word or divider.
- Co-driver: a master brand that shares the purchase decision equally with a sub-brand.
- Coherence: the same brand character recognisable everywhere, with behaviour adapted to each medium.
- Combination mark: a symbol and a wordmark in a fixed lockup.
- Common-law use: rights earned by trading under a name without registering it.
- Computer-generated work: under India's Copyright Act 1957, section 2(d)(vi), a work whose author is the person who causes it to be created.
- Confidence level: the share of repeated samples whose interval would contain the true value, usually 95%.
- Conjunct: two or more Devanagari consonants joined into one glyph.
- Connotation: what a sign suggests beyond what it shows.
- Consistency: the same assets reproduced the same way on every surface.
- Construction grid: the geometric scaffold a mark is drawn on.
- Copyright assignment: the written transfer of copyright from the author; in India deemed to run five years and cover India only if the deed is silent.
- Core Web Vitals: Google's three page-experience metrics (Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint, Cumulative Layout Shift) with pass thresholds of 2.5s, 200ms and 0.1.
- Crisis mode: the prepared monochrome identity set, pause list and owner used when the brand must go quiet.
- Cultural strategy: Holt's method of building a brand by performing a myth that resolves a tension in the culture.
- Customer-based brand equity pyramid: Keller's model running from salience through performance and imagery, judgements and feelings, to resonance.
- Denotation: what a sign literally shows.
- Descriptive mark: a name that says what the product is, protectable only with acquired distinctiveness.
- Design tokens: named values for colour, type, space, radius, shadow and motion, stored once and exported everywhere.
- Didone: the high-contrast serif style of Bodoni and Didot, with hairline serifs and vertical stress.
- Differentiation: being meaningfully different in what the brand offers.
- Digital-first guideline: a guideline published as a website with downloadable assets and values held as tokens.
- Distinctive asset tracking: an annual test showing each asset without the brand name to measure fame and uniqueness.
- Distinctive brand assets (DBAs): non-name elements that trigger the brand name in memory, measured by fame and uniqueness.
- Distinctiveness: being recognisable as yourself, quickly.
- Diwani: a dense, flowing Ottoman chancery style of Arabic calligraphy, used for display.
- Dominant code: a category's current normal set of signs.
- Double jeopardy law: smaller brands have fewer buyers who are also slightly less loyal.
- dp (density-independent pixel): Android's unit of length, scaled to screen density.
- DST and PES: the Tajima and Brother machine-embroidery file formats made by a digitiser from the vector.
- Dual branding: running "New, formerly Old" on every surface for 12 to 24 months after a rename.
- DXF: the vector exchange format used for CNC routing and laser cutting.
- Dynamic identity: a system of rules that produces many versions of the mark.
- Eastern Arabic-Indic digits: the numerals used with Arabic script in the Gulf, Unicode U+0660 to U+0669.
- Edge paint: colour applied to the cut edges of thick board.
- Emblem: type set inside a shape, crest-like.
- Emergent code: a sign rising at the edge of a category.
- Endorsed brand: an independent brand carrying a visible parent signature.
- EULA (end user licence agreement): the foundry's licence that governs where a typeface may be used, including in logos and trademarks.
- Excess share of voice (ESOV): share of voice minus share of market, a predictor of share growth.
- Fame: the share of category buyers who link an asset to the brand.
- Fanciful or coined mark: an invented word used as a name, the strongest category for protection.
- Faux out-of-home: CGI films of impossible billboards and installations, made for social.
- First impression chain: the sequence of surfaces a buyer meets first, from search result to the AI answer.
- First Moment of Truth: P&G's term (2005) for the moment a buyer meets the product on the shelf.
- Five-second test: a route shown to a respondent for five seconds, followed by recall and reading questions.
- Flanker or fighter brand: a portfolio brand that defends the price flank against a cheaper competitor.
- Frame of reference: the category the buyer shops a brand against on the day they decide.
- GCC: the Gulf Cooperation Council of Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
- Generic name: the common name of a product, which can never be registered as a trademark.
- Giga-project: the term used for Saudi Arabia's PIF-funded developments at the scale of a city or region.
- GLB and USDZ: the 3D file formats read by web viewers and by Apple's AR Quick Look.
- Graphic device: a recurring shape, line or pattern derived from the mark that carries the identity when the logo is absent.
- Grotesk: a sans serif in the 19th-century German and British tradition, such as Akzidenz-Grotesk.
- Harakat: the Arabic short-vowel marks, usually left out of a wordmark.
- House of brands: independent brands under a parent invisible to buyers.
- HUID: the six-character alphanumeric hallmark unique identification on BIS-hallmarked gold jewellery in India.
- HVAC: a building's heating, ventilation and air-conditioning system, used to diffuse scent.
- Hybrid architecture: a deliberate mix of architecture models by business unit.
- I'jam: the dots that distinguish Arabic letters of the same shape.
- Icon (Peirce): a sign that resembles its object, the basis of a pictorial mark.
- Ignore or test: Romaniuk's quadrant for an asset with low fame and low uniqueness.
- Index (Peirce): a sign that points to its object by a physical trace or cause.
- Ink trap: a cut at a stroke junction that stops ink or pixels filling the join.
- Intrecciato: Bottega Veneta's hand-woven leather, the house's signature texture.
- Investment potential: Romaniuk's quadrant for an asset with low fame and high uniqueness, worth spending to build.
- IVR (interactive voice response): the automated phone menu, a voice surface of the brand.
- Kashida (tatweel): the horizontal extension stroke that lengthens an Arabic word, used in place of letter-spacing.
- Kerning: adjustment of space between specific letter pairs.
- Kirana: a neighbourhood grocery shop in India.
- Kufi: the geometric, rectilinear style of Arabic script.
- Law of leadership: Ries and Trout's claim that the first brand into a category owns it in the buyer's mind.
- Lettermark: initials set as letterforms.
- Licence register: the record of every licensed asset with holder, scope, renewal and cost.
- Light buyers: people who buy from a brand once or twice a year at most, the majority of its buyers.
- Linchpin brand: a portfolio brand that carries a major segment or the loyalty programme.
- Line of interaction: in a service blueprint, the line between the customer's actions and front-stage staff actions.
- Line of visibility: in a service blueprint, the line below which the customer cannot see the work.
- Linked name: an endorsement through a shared name root, such as McDonald's McCafé.
- Living system: a guideline, Figma library, code components and tokens released together as one versioned thing.
- Lockup: the fixed relationship between mark, wordmark and strapline.
- Logo prominence: the size and visibility of branding on a product, which falls as price rises in luxury.
- Lottie: a JSON animation format exported from After Effects for web and app.
- LUT (look-up table): a file that maps input colours to graded output.
- Luxury strategy: Kapferer and Bastien's claim that luxury is a business model with rules that invert mass marketing.
- Madrid Protocol: WIPO's system for extending one trademark application to member countries through a single international registration.
- Margin of error: the half-width of a confidence interval, about 5.7 points on n = 300 at 95%.
- Mascot: a character used as a brand mark.
- Masstige: Silverstein and Fiske's term for goods priced between mass and old luxury and bought for an emotional benefit.
- Masters: editable files (deck, document, social, email) built to the guideline.
- Matra: a Devanagari vowel sign attached to a consonant.
- Mental availability: the propensity for a brand to be noticed or thought of in buying situations.
- Mental market share: a brand's share of all brand-to-CEP links in its category.
- Mental penetration: the share of category buyers who link a brand to at least one CEP.
- Moment of truth: Carlzon's term for each brief contact between a customer and staff that decides the brand.
- Monadic exposure: a test design in which each respondent sees one route only.
- Monogram: initials interlaced into one form.
- Myth: a connotation presented as natural fact.
- Naming hierarchy: the grammar for how names stack from master to variant.
- Naskh: the rounded cursive book hand of Arabic script.
- NCS (Natural Colour System): the Scandinavian colour standard architects specify.
- Net promoter question: how likely, from 0 to 10, a respondent is to recommend the brand.
- Network size: the average number of CEPs linked to a brand among buyers who link any.
- Nice Classification: WIPO's 45-class system for registering trademarks by goods and services; 13th edition in force from 1 January 2026.
- Non-traditional marks: trademarks beyond words and images, such as 3D shapes, sounds, smells and holograms.
- Nukta: the dot below a Devanagari letter that marks sounds borrowed from Persian, Arabic and English.
- Onlyness statement: Neumeier's "our brand is the only [category] that [benefit]".
- Optical centre: a point about 2 to 5% above geometric centre where a centred element looks centred.
- Optical correction: adjustments that make a geometrically regular form look regular to the eye.
- OTP (one-time password): the code sent by SMS to confirm a login or payment, a neglected brand surface.
- Overshoot: the extension of round and pointed forms past the baseline and cap line.
- Parvenus: Han, Nunes and Drèze's wealthy buyers with a high need for status, who want loud branding.
- Patricians: Han, Nunes and Drèze's wealthy buyers with a low need for status, who want quiet signals only peers can read.
- PDF/X-4: the ISO print-exchange standard that keeps transparency and colour profiles.
- Peak-end rule: an experience is remembered by its most intense moment and its end.
- Penetration: the share of category buyers who buy a brand in a period.
- Perceptual map: a 2 by 2 grid plotting brands on two attributes.
- Permanent redirect (301): a server rule that sends an old URL to its new address and passes its search signals.
- Phonetic search: a trademark search on how a name sounds.
- Physical availability: how easy a brand is to find and buy where and when the buyer looks.
- Pictorial mark: a logo drawn as a recognisable object.
- Place brand: an identity for a city, region or development, often built before the place exists.
- Point of difference: the one attribute a brand claims that the alternatives in its frame cannot.
- Portfolio strategy: deciding which brands to hold, fold, and where each sits on price and audience.
- Poseurs: Han, Nunes and Drèze's buyers with a high need for status and no means, the market for counterfeits.
- Positioning: the place a brand occupies in the buyer's mind relative to alternatives.
- Positioning statement: the testable format "for [buyer], [brand] is the [frame] that [difference] because [proof]".
- Preference testing: asking respondents which route they like, which rewards the familiar.
- Premium strategy: being best in a category, compared and proved, as distinct from luxury's incomparability (Kapferer and Bastien).
- Price realisation: the share of sales at full price and the average discount depth.
- Prior use: in Indian trademark law, an earlier unregistered use that can defeat a later registration.
- Proletarians: Han, Nunes and Drèze's buyers with neither the means nor the need to signal status.
- ProRes 4444: Apple's high-quality video codec with an alpha channel for transparency.
- RAL: the German colour standard for paints and powder coatings.
- Reason to believe: the evidence that makes a positioning claim credible.
- Rebrand: a change to the identity, from a refresh to a replacement of name, mark or system.
- Relative grounds for refusal: objections to a trademark arising from conflict with an earlier mark.
- Repertoire category: a category where buyers rotate among several brands.
- Reph: a Devanagari r written as a hook above the following consonant.
- RERA: India's Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 and the state authorities that register projects under it.
- Residual code: a sign fading from a category.
- Responsive logo suite: one mark drawn at several levels of detail for different sizes.
- Royalty relief: valuing a brand as the licence fee its owner avoids paying by owning it.
- Salience: the propensity to be thought of across buying situations, measured through category entry points.
- Satin stitch: long parallel embroidery stitches laid side by side to fill a narrow shape.
- Scent branding: a signature fragrance diffused in a brand's spaces.
- Semantic versioning: numbering a guideline 1.0 at launch, 1.x for additions and 2.0 for a rebrand.
- Semiotics: the study of signs and how meaning is made from them.
- Service blueprint: a diagram of a service across time showing customer actions, front-stage and back-stage work.
- Shadow endorser: a parent known to buyers and absent from the child brand's product and signage.
- Share of search: a brand's share of all search queries for the brands in its category, a leading indicator of market share.
- Share of voice: a brand's share of all advertising in its category.
- Shirorekha: the headline bar from which Devanagari letters hang.
- Signifier and signified: Saussure's form of a sign and the concept it carries.
- Silver bullet: a portfolio brand used to change the image of another.
- Single-line version: a centre-line drawing of the mark for engraving, neon and foil hairlines.
- Social listening: monitoring public social posts for share of voice and sentiment.
- Sonic logo: a 1 to 3 second audio mark.
- Sound symbolism: the link between speech sounds and meaning that holds across languages.
- Spectrum of distinctiveness: the scale from generic to fanciful names that sets how strongly a trademark is protected.
- Squint test: blurring a mark to check that its grey is even.
- Statutory zone: the space reserved in a master layout for legally required marks and numbers.
- Strategic brand: the portfolio brand that carries the future of the business.
- Strong endorsement: a clearly visible parent signature on a child brand, as in Courtyard by Marriott.
- Sub-brand: a named offer under a master brand with its own personality.
- Suggestive mark: a name that hints at a benefit and asks the buyer for a mental step.
- Symbol (Peirce): a sign that means by convention alone, the basis of an abstract mark.
- Thuluth: a large, sweeping, architectural style of Arabic calligraphy, used for display.
- Token endorsement: a small parent signature on a child brand's pack.
- Tone of voice: the consistent manner in which a brand's words sound, separate from what they say.
- Tooth height: the visual x-height of Arabic letters.
- Top-of-mind awareness: the brand named first without a prompt.
- Touchpoint: any point where a buyer meets the brand.
- Touchpoint map: every touchpoint listed by stage, owner, current state, score and frequency.
- Tracking: uniform letter spacing across a word.
- Trade name: the licensed business name in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, distinct from a trademark.
- Trademark drawing: the version of a mark filed with a registry, black on white.
- Trakheesi: the Dubai Land Department permit required before a property advertisement is published.
- Unaided awareness: a brand named without a prompt.
- Uniqueness: the share of an asset's brand links that go only to this brand.
- Use or lose: Romaniuk's quadrant for an asset with high fame and high uniqueness, to be protected and used everywhere.
- Veg and non-veg marks: India's FSSAI symbols, a green square with a filled circle and a brown square with a filled triangle.
- Vienna Classification: WIPO's system of codes for the figurative elements of marks; tenth edition in force from 1 January 2026.
- Well-known mark: a mark a substantial segment of the relevant public knows, protected across all classes.
- Wordmark (logotype): the name set in custom or chosen letterforms as the mark.
- World-Ready Paragraph Composer: the Adobe composer that applies complex-script shaping to Arabic and Indic text.
- Zero Moment of Truth: Google's term (Lecinski, 2011) for the research a buyer does before meeting the product.
