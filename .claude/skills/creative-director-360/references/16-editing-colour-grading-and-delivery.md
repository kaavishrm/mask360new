## 1. Editing theory

### 1.1 Murch's Rule of Six

Walter Murch (editor and sound designer of Apocalypse Now, The Conversation, The English Patient) set out in In the Blink of an Eye (Silman-James, second edition 2001) six criteria for an ideal cut, weighted by importance:

| Rank | Criterion | Weight | What it asks of the cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Emotion | 51 | Is the audience feeling what the film needs them to feel at this moment |
| 2 | Story | 23 | Does the cut advance the story |
| 3 | Rhythm | 10 | Does the cut land at a moment that is rhythmically interesting and right |
| 4 | Eye trace | 7 | Does the cut respect where the audience's eye is on screen, so the next shot picks it up where it was left |
| 5 | Two dimensional plane of screen | 5 | Does the cut respect screen geography (the 180 degree line, left and right) |
| 6 | Three dimensional space of action | 4 | Does the cut respect where people and things physically are in the room |

The weights sum to 100 and the top item outweighs the other five combined. The practical reading: when a cut cannot satisfy all six, sacrifice from the bottom. A cut that crosses the line but keeps the emotion is right; a cut that keeps geography and kills the feeling is wrong. Murch's ordering says the same thing in numbers: three-dimensional continuity is given up first and emotion last. Eye trace is the one most often ignored in commercial work: if the viewer's eye is on the model's hand at bottom left, the next shot should place its point of interest near bottom left or deliberately pull the eye across the frame for a reason.

The blink as cut point: Murch's observation that people blink at the moment a thought completes, and that an audience blinks (or is ready to blink) at the same moment the editor should cut. A cut placed where the viewer would naturally blink is invisible; a cut placed mid-thought is felt as an interruption. In practice: watch the performance, find the moment the idea lands, cut one or two frames after it.

### 1.2 Kuleshov effect

Lev Kuleshov's experiment (Moscow, 1910s to 1920s; described by Vsevolod Pudovkin in 1929, no original footage survives) intercut the same expressionless shot of actor Ivan Mozzhukhin with a bowl of soup, a child in a coffin and a woman on a divan. Audiences read hunger, grief and desire from an identical face. The lesson: meaning is produced between shots, so the editor is the author of the viewer's inference. Alfred Hitchcock restated it on CBC's Telescope (1964, with Fletcher Markle): an old man's squint cut against a mother and baby reads as kindness; the same squint cut against a woman in a bikini reads as lechery. For Mask360 this is the mechanism of every product reveal: the face, then the object, and the audience supplies the desire.

### 1.3 Eisenstein's montage types

Sergei Eisenstein (Methods of Montage, 1929, collected in Film Form, 1949) classified montage by the dominant relationship between shots:

- Metric: cuts at fixed frame counts regardless of content; tension from pure tempo. Useful for a beat-locked fashion cut.
- Rhythmic: shot length set by the movement inside the frame as well as the count. The commercial default.
- Tonal: cuts governed by the emotional tone of the shot (light, softness, mood). The hotel film.
- Overtonal (associational): the accumulation of metric, rhythmic and tonal effects across a sequence.
- Intellectual: shots collide to produce an idea neither contains (his example: the slaughterhouse against the workers in Strike, 1925).

Eisenstein's position was collision (montage as conflict between shots); Pudovkin's was linkage (shots as bricks that build a sense). The house follows Pudovkin for story and Eisenstein for the one intellectual cut per film, because a luxury audience accepts a single collision and tires of a sequence of them.

### 1.4 Continuity editing and the invisible cut

Continuity editing is the system (Hollywood, codified by the 1920s) that hides the cut: the 180 degree rule (keep the camera on one side of the axis of action so screen direction holds), the 30 degree rule (change the angle by at least 30 degrees or the cut reads as a jump), shot reverse shot, eyeline match (a look off screen followed by what is looked at), match on action (cut during a movement so the movement carries the eye across the cut). Karel Reisz and Gavin Millar's The Technique of Film Editing (1953; second edition 1968) remains the manual. The invisible cut is the goal of this system: the audience follows the story and never notices the join.

### 1.5 Discontinuity as style

Jean-Luc Godard's jump cuts in Breathless (1960), the French New Wave generally, and the music video grammar of the 1980s onward made visible cutting a style. Discontinuity works when it is the idea: a fashion film about restlessness, a social cut that signals energy. It fails when it is used to hide coverage problems. A rule the house uses: discontinuity must be declared in the first three cuts or it reads as error.

### 1.6 The edit as the last rewrite

The edit is where the film is written for the final time. Scripts are hypotheses; dailies are evidence; the cut is the argument. This is why the editor should see the script and the brief, and why a creative director reviews the structure of a cut (what the film is saying, in what order) before any note on a single shot. Murch's practice of watching the first assembly in silence, without music, is the house rule for the first viewing of any cut: music hides structural faults.

## 2. Cut types and when to use each

| Cut | What it is | Use when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight cut (hard cut) | Shot A ends, shot B begins, no transition | Always, by default. The luxury default because it assumes the audience is intelligent and the images are strong enough to meet | Never avoid; the question is only where |
| Cut on action | Cut in the middle of a movement; B continues the movement | Any physical action: a hand reaching, a door, a turn. Hides the cut because the eye follows the motion | Static shots (nothing to carry the eye) |
| Match cut (graphic) | Shapes or compositions rhyme across the cut: a round dish to a round pool | Transitions between scenes or locations; the brand film that moves from product to place | More than twice in a film; it becomes a trick |
| Match cut (action) | A movement begun in A completes in B in a different space | Montage of a process (jewellery making), a reveal | When the two actions are not truly matched in speed and direction |
| Match cut (sound) | A sound bridges and motivates the cut: a tap becomes a drum | Scene changes; the opening of a 60 second film | When the sound is a gimmick with no story link |
| Jump cut (intentional) | Same angle, time removed, subject jumps | Energy, honesty, a founder talking straight to camera, social content | Any shot that wants to feel continuous; anything the client will read as a mistake |
| J cut | Audio of B leads before picture of B | Interviews, testimonials, any dialogue scene; it pulls the viewer forward | Never avoid; the most useful cut in documentary |
| L cut | Audio of A lags under picture of B | Reaction shots, the product seen while the voice continues | When the lag hides the fact that B has nothing to say |
| Cross cutting (parallel action) | Alternating two lines of action in different places | Two protagonists, before and after, preparation and arrival (hotel kitchen and dining room) | When the two lines never meet; the audience waits for a convergence |
| Smash cut | Abrupt cut from quiet to loud, slow to fast, or the reverse | The turn in a 30 second structure; one per film | More than one |
| Invisible cut (whip, object wipe) | Camera whips or an object crosses the lens, the cut hides inside the blur | Joining two locations into one take, the Birdman illusion | When the whip is not motivated; it reads as a 2015 wedding film |
| Dissolve | A fades into B over several frames | Passage of time; a dream; a very slow film where the dissolve is the pace. Rarely right because it softens two images at once and signals indecision | By default. Dissolves are what editors use when they cannot find the cut |
| Fade to black | Picture to black | The end; a chapter break in a long film | Between every scene |
| Fade from white | White to picture | Openings in beauty and some fashion; a wake-up | Any brand with black as its ground |
| Wipe | A line or shape pushes B across A | Never, except as a joke that knows it is Star Wars (1977) | Everywhere else |
| Speed ramp | Frame rate changes within a shot (fast to slow or slow to fast) | A reveal: the fabric at 100 percent to 25 percent as it settles. Shoot at 50 or 60 fps or higher so the slow section has real frames | Ramps on every shot; ramps that use optical flow on fine detail (see section 10) |
| Freeze frame | A single frame held | A punctuation mark; the end frame | Mid-film |
| Split screen | Two or more frames at once | The house split screen copy format (see mask360-copywriting): image left, one line right; comparison; before and after; the bilingual frame | Three or more splits unless the film is about multiplicity |

The hard cut is the luxury default because every other transition adds a layer between the viewer and the image, and luxury is the removal of layers. Aman's films, Loewe's product films and The Row's lookbook motion cut hard and hold long.

## 3. Pacing and rhythm

### 3.1 Average shot length

Average shot length (ASL) is the duration of a film divided by its number of shots. David Bordwell (Intensified Continuity, Film Quarterly, 2002) put Hollywood studio-era ASL at about 8 to 11 seconds (1930 to 1960) and 1990s features at about 3 to 6 seconds. James Cutting and colleagues (Attention and the Evolution of Hollywood Film, Psychological Science, 2010) measured the decline across 1935 to 2005 and found shot durations converging on the rhythms of human attention. Barry Salt's statistical style analysis and the Cinemetrics database (Yuri Tsivian and Gunars Civjans) hold per-film data.

House ASL targets for Mask360 work (targets, set by the house, measured on delivered films):

| Genre | ASL target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fashion film (60 to 90 s) | 1.5 to 3 s | Energy and garment variety; the audience reads a silhouette in under two seconds |
| Hotel or destination film (90 to 120 s) | 3 to 6 s | Space needs time; a room read in one second is a room not felt |
| Product film (30 to 60 s) | 2 to 4 s | Detail shots are legible in two seconds; a hero shot earns four |
| Social cutdown (6 to 15 s) | 0.8 to 1.5 s | Attention on a phone resets every second; every shot must be a new reason to stay |
| Documentary or founder film (2 to 4 min) | 4 to 8 s | Faces and speech need duration to be trusted |
| Jewellery macro film | 2.5 to 4 s | Light moving across metal needs time to complete a sweep |

A fashion film with a 5 second ASL reads as a hotel film; a hotel film with a 1.5 second ASL reads as an advertisement for anxiety.

### 3.2 Music driven and picture driven cuts

Music driven: the track is chosen first and the cut follows its grid. Cuts land on the beat (the downbeat, or the transient of the kick), on the off beat (the and), or on phrase boundaries (every 4 or 8 bars). The trap is cutting on every beat, which reads as a montage from 2008. The house rule: establish the grid with three cuts on the beat, then move cuts off the beat so the music feels found. Cut two to three frames before the transient, because the eye registers a cut later than the ear registers a hit, and a cut on the exact frame reads late.

Picture driven: the cut follows action, performance and eye trace, and the music is composed or edited to it. Required for testimonial and founder films, and for any film where a face carries the story.

### 3.3 Breathing room and holds

A hold is a shot left longer than its information requires. Holds are where luxury lives: the second after the hand leaves the frame, the beat before the door opens. Minimum hold on a hero shot: 3 seconds at 25 fps. Maximum before the audience feels abandoned: about 8 seconds without a change of light, movement or sound. A 60 second film should contain at least two holds of 3 seconds or more, and the end frame is a third.

### 3.4 The opening three seconds

On Instagram, TikTok and YouTube the first three seconds decide the view. Meta's own creative guidance has for years recommended front-loading the brand or the hook. The house opening: the strongest image, movement already in progress (never a shot that starts static and then moves), the brand readable by frame 50 at 25 fps (two seconds) either by a product, a place or a mark. Sound starts under the first frame, never at frame one with a hard music in (see section 14).

### 3.5 Structure by length

| Length | Shots (at house ASL) | Structure | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90 s | 25 to 40 | Open 10 s, build 50 s, turn at about 60 s, payoff 20 s, end frame 5 s | The hero. Everything else derives from this |
| 60 s | 18 to 30 | Open 5 s, build 35 s, turn at 40 s, payoff 15 s, end frame 4 s | The broadcast and YouTube pre-roll length |
| 30 s | 10 to 15 | Hook 3 s, build 15 s, turn 20 s, payoff 7 s, end frame 3 s | The TV spot; three acts in thirty seconds |
| 15 s | 6 to 10 | Hook 2 s, one idea 9 s, end frame 4 s | Stories, Reels ads, YouTube non-skippable |
| 6 s | 2 to 4 | One image, one line, logo | YouTube bumper; the image must be the idea |
| Loop (Reels, 7 to 12 s) | 6 to 10 | Ends on a frame that cuts cleanly back to the first | The loop should be invisible: match the last and first frames in composition and motion so the replay feels like a continuation |

### 3.6 Cutdown strategy

Shoot and edit the hero first, derive the rest. A 60 second cut is the hero's structure re-timed, so the turn still falls at two thirds. Build the hero to picture lock, then the 30, then the 15, then the 6, each with its own turn and end frame. Vertical versions are reframed from the hero with keyframed position, never auto-reframed alone (see section 10.5). Cutdowns are budgeted in the scope at the start: a hero plus 30, 15 and 6 in 16:9 and 9:16 is ten deliverables, and the client sees that number in the estimate.

### 3.7 Pattern interrupts

A pattern interrupt is a deliberate break in the rhythm (a hold after fast cuts, a silence, a frame of black, a change of format) that resets attention. Place one at the turn and nowhere else in a 30; one every 10 to 15 seconds in a long social piece. The social mechanics (hooks, retention curves, caption cadence) are in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md.

## 4. Structure for commercial lengths

### 4.1 Three acts in thirty seconds

Hook (0 to 3 s): the image that stops the thumb or holds the remote. Build (3 to 20 s): the world, the product in use, the accumulation. Turn (about 20 s): the reversal, the reveal, the line that recontextualises. Payoff (20 to 27 s): the product or place at its most desirable, the single line of copy. End frame (27 to 30 s): mark, name, nothing else, held. The turn at two thirds is the structural constant across all lengths because the audience's attention curve dips at the middle and needs a reason to climb.

### 4.2 The brand film arc

The 90 to 120 second brand film (hospitality, fashion house, jeweller) follows a different shape: an opening that establishes place and light without product (10 to 15 s), a sequence of human moments each with a hold, a central passage where the product or craft appears in its own time (the goldsmith, the chef, the atelier), a quiet climax (a face, a landscape, a single object), and an end frame. There is no turn in the advertising sense; the structure is a slow build to stillness. Reference: Aman's property films, Loro Piana's craft films, Hermès' films of its artisans (see design-taste, references/canon.md for the studios).

### 4.3 The testimonial arc

Context (who, where, in one line), the problem or desire (in their words), the moment of change, the result, and the line they would say to a friend. Cut on J cuts so the next voice begins before the face, and never let a testimonial run as a single talking head for more than 8 seconds without a cutaway that proves the claim. Captions are mandatory (see section 11) because feeds autoplay muted and a large share of viewers never turn the sound on (Digiday reported in 2016 that publishers saw as much as 85 percent of their Facebook video viewed without sound).

### 4.4 The product reveal

Withhold, then show. The reveal has four parts: the anticipation (hands, light, a surface, a sound), the first glimpse (a detail, never the whole), the full reveal (one shot, held 3 seconds or more, light moving), and the context (the product in its world). Kuleshov applies: a face looking, then the object, and the audience feels the want. The reveal shot is graded and retouched to the highest standard in the film because it will be the frame the client pulls as a still.

### 4.5 The silent first five seconds

A film that opens without music (room tone, a footstep, fabric) reads as confident and expensive, because it refuses to sell. On broadcast and in cinema this works. On social it is a risk: a silent open with no caption and no motion reads as a broken upload, and the feed advances. The house compromise on social: the first five seconds carry diegetic sound (sound belonging to the scene) and a motion already in progress, with music entering at the first cut.

## 5. Workflow from ingest to archive

### 5.1 Ingest and backup

The 3-2-1 rule: three copies of every card, on two different kinds of media, one of them off site (or in a different building, or in cloud storage). On set: the DIT or loader copies each card with a checksum-verified tool (Hedge, ShotPut Pro, Silverstack, or Resolve's Clone Tool), which computes a hash (xxHash or MD5) of the source and the copy and refuses to report success unless they match. A card is never formatted until two verified copies exist. On Mask360 shoots in Mumbai, Jaipur, Goa and Dubai a second person carries the second drive back, so one lost bag cannot lose the job.

### 5.2 Transcoding and proxies

Camera originals (ARRIRAW, REDCODE RAW, Blackmagic RAW, ProRes RAW, Sony X-OCN, H.265 from mirrorless and phones) are either edited natively (Resolve and Premiere decode most of them) or transcoded to proxies: lightweight copies at lower resolution and bitrate that cut smoothly, relinked to the originals at conform. The standard proxy: Apple ProRes 422 Proxy at 1920 by 1080 (about 38 Mbps at 25 fps, from the Apple ProRes white paper) or H.264 at 1080p for remote editors. Proxies must carry the same timecode, reel name and file name as the originals so relinking is automatic. Apple ProRes data rates at 1920 by 1080, 25 fps, from Apple's ProRes white paper: Proxy about 38 Mbps, LT about 85, 422 about 122, 422 HQ about 184, 4444 about 275, 4444 XQ about 413. At 3840 by 2160, 25 fps: 422 HQ about 737 Mbps, 4444 about 1,106.

### 5.3 Bins, naming and logging

Bins (folders inside the project) by day, then by scene or setup, then by camera. Clip names keep the camera file name (A001_C003_0612AB) and gain a scene and take in metadata, never in the file name. Logging: watch every frame once, rate with stars (1 unusable, 2 coverage, 3 usable, 4 good, 5 hero) and add keywords (person, location, action, product, light). Resolve's Smart Bins and Premiere's Media Intelligence (natural language search over footage, 25.2, April 2025) and Resolve 21's IntelliSearch (search by face, object or spoken word, 2026) make keyword logging faster; the star rating still needs a human eye because the AI does not know which take has the feeling.

### 5.4 Selects, stringouts, assembly

Selects are the 4 and 5 star clips pulled into a timeline. A stringout is every select laid end to end in script or scene order, often two to five times the target length. The assembly is the stringout cut to structure (the sections of section 4) at roughly 1.5 times the target length. These three timelines are kept and never overwritten; a later note ("was there a wider shot of the pool") is answered from the stringout in a minute.

### 5.5 Rough cut, fine cut, picture lock

Rough cut: structure right, length within 20 percent, temp music, no grade, no titles, no effects. Fine cut: every cut placed to the frame, music edited or composed, titles in place as cards, sound roughly balanced. Picture lock: no further changes to the timing of picture. Lock is the gate to sound and colour because both work to timecode; a single frame change after lock costs a re-conform. The house marks lock in the project name (v07_LOCK) and in the review tool.

### 5.6 Turnover to sound and colour

Turnover is the hand-off of the locked edit to the sound mixer and the colourist. Formats:

- AAF (Advanced Authoring Format): the standard for audio turnover to Pro Tools, carries clips, handles, levels, fades and track layout. Export with handles of 48 to 96 frames so the mixer can extend any clip.
- OMF (Open Media Framework): the older audio interchange, limited to 2 GB per file and 16 bit in most implementations. Only when a facility insists.
- XML (Final Cut Pro XML or FCPXML): the picture turnover from Premiere or Final Cut Pro to Resolve. Carries cuts, speed changes, basic transforms; loses most effects.
- EDL (CMX3600 edit decision list): the oldest, one video track per file, cuts and dissolves only, universally understood. The fallback when XML fails.
- OpenTimelineIO (OTIO): the open interchange format started at Pixar and now hosted by the Academy Software Foundation, imported and exported by Resolve since 18.5 and arriving in other NLEs (check the build's export menu before promising it to a facility); the likely replacement for XML because it carries nested structure and metadata that CMX and FCP XML drop.

Deliver a reference render (the locked cut as a 1080p H.264 with burned-in timecode) with every turnover so the colourist and mixer can verify their conform against the picture.

### 5.7 Conform, relink, online

Conform is the rebuilding of the locked timeline from the camera originals in the finishing application. Relink is the mechanical step: the application matches each proxy clip to its original by file name, reel name and timecode. Online is the finishing stage: full resolution, final effects, titles rendered from vector, the grade applied. A conform check: play the online against the reference render in split screen or difference mode and watch for any cut that drifts by a frame.

### 5.8 Versioning and the change list

Versions are numbered v01 to vXX, never "final", "final2", "FINAL_revised". Each version is accompanied by a change list: a numbered list of what changed and at what timecode, written by the editor, sent with the link. The change list is the record that protects the agency in a dispute about rounds.

### 5.9 Review tools and comment discipline

Frame.io (Adobe; V4 current as of October 2026; plans verified on frame.io/pricing in October 2026: Free up to 2 members and 2 GB, Pro 15 USD per member per month for up to 5 members with 2 TB included, Team 25 USD per member per month for up to 15 members with 3 TB included, each extra member adding 2 TB, annual billing 13 percent less, Enterprise on request; Mounted Storage, which presents the project as a drive on the desktop, is on every plan from September 2026 at 2 GB Free, 250 GB Pro and 500 GB Team) gives frame-accurate comments, annotations on the frame, version stacking and a Premiere panel. Vimeo Review is the alternative for clients who already live in Vimeo. Blackmagic Cloud Presentations covers Resolve-native pipelines with shared review links and comments inside the Resolve project.

Comment discipline, written into the client agreement: one consolidated set of comments per round, from one named approver, with timecodes, within five working days. Comments like "make it pop" are returned with a question ("brighter, warmer, faster, or more saturated?"). The editor replies to every comment in the tool so the record is complete.

### 5.10 Client rounds in scope

Two rounds of changes are in scope for every edit. A round is one consolidated set of comments and one revised version. A change of structure (new order, new script line, a different ending) after fine cut is a new round at the day rate. The scope letter states this in one sentence, and the producer restates it when sending v01.

### 5.11 Archive

At delivery: the project file, the consolidated media (originals used in the cut with 48 frame handles, via Resolve's Media Management or Premiere's Project Manager), the masters, the stems, the graphics sources, the LUTs and the fonts, in a dated folder on an LTO tape or an archive drive plus cloud cold storage. Resolve's Project Archive bundles the project with its media, render cache, LUTs and gallery stills in one package. Keep for seven years for Indian tax and contract purposes (the Income-tax Act, 1961 and the Companies Act, 2013 both set multi-year record retention); confirm the period with the accountant.

## 6. Tools as of October 2026

### 6.1 Adobe Premiere (formerly Premiere Pro)

Current name: Adobe Premiere. Adobe dropped "Pro" with version 26.0, released on 20 January 2026 ahead of the Sundance Film Festival, to give one name to the desktop application, the iPhone app (September 2025) and the Android app (released 22 September 2026, Android 13 or later; it replaced Premiere Rush, which stopped working on 30 September 2026). The product page, read in October 2026, states that "Adobe Premiere Pro is now simply called Adobe Premiere" and lists the single-app plan at 22.99 USD per month on an annual plan (the first-year rate for new subscribers). The features that matter to a finishing house:

- Text-Based Editing (since 23.4, 2023): footage is transcribed on import, the transcript is a document, words are selected to cut and text pasted to build a rough cut. Right for interviews and founder films; useless for fashion.
- Generative Extend: generally available in 25.2 (April 2025), adds generated frames to the head or tail of a clip and generated ambient sound, using the Firefly Video Model; Adobe's published limits are up to 2 seconds of video and 10 seconds of audio, sources at 12 to 30 fps, 8 bit SDR output. Use it for a half-second of missing handle on a static shot. Do not use it on skin, fabric or text, where the generated frames differ in texture.
- Generative Media Tool (released 8 September 2026, ahead of IBC): select a range on a track, describe the shot, pick a model (Firefly or a partner model: Google Veo, Kling, Runway or Luma, per Adobe's blog), and the generated clip lands on the timeline; Generate Sound Effects ships with it, while Generate Music and Generate Soundscape are in public beta, and the Firefly credit cost shows before the editor commits. None of it belongs in a luxury master except as a placeholder or a disclosed background plate (see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md).
- Object Mask (26.0, January 2026): hover and click to mask and track a moving person or object with an on-device model, with add, subtract and intersect modes and a mode for correcting tracked keyframes; Adobe says the redesigned shape masks track up to 20 times faster (Adobe's claim, no independent benchmark found). This replaces most rotoscoping for secondary colour and selective blur.
- Media Intelligence (25.2, April 2025): natural-language search over footage by objects, locations, camera angles and metadata.
- Colour: Premiere Color Management (generally available 25.2) transforms log and raw from most cameras to SDR or HDR on import, with a wide-gamut ACEScct pipeline option; Color Mode, a grading workspace, is in beta on the product page as of October 2026. Adequate for a one-light (a single base correction applied across all the footage so it can be viewed and cut); the grade still goes to Resolve.
- Captions and speech: automatic transcription, caption translation into 18 languages (product page) and Enhance Speech for dialogue cleanup, which shares Voice Isolation's failure mode (section 6.2) above about 50 percent.
- Review: Frame.io V4 and Adobe Stock panels inside the application from 26.0.

## 7. Colour science for grading

### 7.1 The pipeline

Every grade runs through four stages, whether the colourist sees them or not:

1. Camera log: the camera encodes scene light with a logarithmic curve (ARRI LogC3 and LogC4, Sony S-Log3 in S-Gamut3.Cine, RED Log3G10 in REDWideGamutRGB, Blackmagic Film Gen 5, Canon Log 2 and 3, Panasonic V-Log, Fujifilm F-Log2, Nikon N-Log, DJI D-Log2, Apple Log on iPhone 15 Pro onward) so that 14 or more stops fit in a 10 or 12 bit file. Log footage looks flat and grey because it is a container that the output transform later renders into a picture.
2. Input transform: the mathematics that converts the specific camera's log and gamut into the working space. In ACES this is the Input Transform (formerly IDT); in Resolve it is the Input Color Space on the clip or the first Color Space Transform node.
3. Working space: the colour space and gamma in which the grade is performed. Wide gamut, log-like gamma, so that no colour the camera saw is clipped and so that the grading tools behave evenly across the range.
4. Output transform: the conversion from the working space to the display standard of the deliverable (Rec.709 gamma 2.4 for SDR, PQ or HLG for HDR). It includes a tone mapping (compression of the scene's highlight range into the display's) and a gamut mapping (compression of out-of-gamut colours).

Scene referred means the values describe light in the scene (linear or log, unbounded above white). Display referred means the values describe what the display should emit (0 to 100 percent, bounded). The grade should be performed scene referred or in a log intermediate and converted to display referred once, at the end, because display referred tools clip at white and black and because every creative decision made in 709 is locked to one display and cannot be re-rendered to HDR.

### 7.2 Colour management in Resolve

Three modes, set in Project Settings, Color Management:

- DaVinci YRGB: no management. The colourist applies conversion LUTs or CST nodes by hand. Full control, full responsibility; the mode most colourists use with a manual CST pipeline.
- DaVinci YRGB Color Managed (RCM): Resolve reads each clip's camera metadata (or the tagged Input Color Space), converts to the chosen timeline colour space, and converts to the output colour space at the end. Set the timeline to DaVinci Wide Gamut and DaVinci Intermediate; set the output to Rec.709 Gamma 2.4 for SDR masters. Since version 17 (2020).
- ACES (ACEScct): the Academy pipeline, below.

DaVinci Wide Gamut (DWG) is Blackmagic's working gamut, larger than any camera gamut so that every camera's colours fit without clipping. DaVinci Intermediate (DI) is the matching log curve. Together they are the house working space because the grading tools (log wheels, contrast, saturation) were tuned for them, and because mixed-camera projects (ARRI plus Sony plus iPhone, the Mask360 reality) land in one space before any grade.

Color Space Transform (CST) is the ResolveFX node that converts between any two colour spaces and gammas with optional tone mapping and gamut mapping. The manual pipeline: first node CST from camera log to DWG and DI; grade; last node CST from DWG and DI to Rec.709 Gamma 2.4 with tone mapping set to DaVinci (or the luminance mapping of choice).

### 7.3 ACES

The Academy Color Encoding System, from the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences. Its parts:

- AP0: the primaries of ACES2065-1, the archive and interchange space; the smallest triangle enclosing the entire CIE 1931 spectral locus, so two of its primaries are imaginary (outside visible colour). Linear. Used for exchange between facilities and for the archive master, never for grading.
- AP1: smaller primaries, still wide gamut, with no negative values for real colours. Used by ACEScg (linear, for VFX and compositing), ACEScc (pure log, for grading) and ACEScct (log with a toe near black that mimics the minimum density of negative film, so that lift behaves like a film grader expects). ACEScct is the grading working space.
- Input Transform (IDT): camera to ACES2065-1.
- Reference Rendering Transform (RRT): the scene referred to display referred rendering, an S-curve with a film-like look, shared by all outputs.
- Output Device Transform (ODT): the RRT output mapped to a specific display (Rec.709, P3-DCI, Rec.2100 PQ).
- Look Modification Transform (LMT): a systematic look applied before the RRT, the ACES equivalent of a creative LUT, placed correctly in the pipeline.

ACES 2.0 was released on 4 April 2025. Its output transform (the RRT plus ODT combination) was rewritten on a CIECAM16 colour appearance model with Hellwig 2022 modifications, with a less aggressive tone scale, reduced mid-tone contrast and a gentler highlight roll-off than ACES 1.x, and new presets including Rec.2020 ST 2084 variants and sRGB gamma 2.2. The same scene-linear EXR looks different through 1.x and 2.0 outputs, so a project must declare which it uses and never mix them. Fusion Studio gained OCIO 2.5 with ACES 2.0 workflows in Resolve 21.1; confirm which ACES output transform version the Color page applies before a job shared with a VFX vendor. The house uses ACEScct when the project involves VFX vendors or a feature-style pipeline, and DWG and DI for everything else, because DWG and DI need fewer settings to get right and the output looks the same when the colourist does the work.

### 7.4 Rec.709 and gamma 2.4 as the SDR delivery standard

ITU-R BT.709 defines HD television colour: primaries red (0.640, 0.330), green (0.300, 0.600), blue (0.150, 0.060), white point D65 (0.3127, 0.3290), and a camera OETF (opto-electronic transfer function, the encoding curve) with a linear segment below 0.018 and a 0.45 power above. BT.709 does not define the display curve. ITU-R BT.1886 (approved March 2011) defines the reference EOTF (electro-optical transfer function, the display decoding curve) for HD studio monitors as a gamma of 2.4. So "Rec.709 gamma 2.4" means: 709 primaries and white, decoded by the display at 2.4, in a dim room. This is the SDR master standard for broadcast, streaming and the house. sRGB and gamma 2.2 are computer display conventions and are the source of the "my render looks lifted on my laptop" complaint (section 12.5).

Legal (video) range: in 8 bit, luma is coded 16 to 235 and chroma 16 to 240; in 10 bit, 64 to 940 and 64 to 960. Full range codes 0 to 255 or 0 to 1023. A master encoded full range and flagged legal, or the reverse, shows crushed or milky blacks on every player; set the data level explicitly on export (Resolve: Video for legal, Full for full; ProRes and H.264 deliverables are legal range).

### 7.5 Why grade in log or an intermediate space

Three reasons:

1. Precision. A 10 bit log file spreads its 1,024 code values across 14 stops; converting to 709 first throws away the top two stops and the deepest shadows before the grade begins. Grading in log keeps the highlight headroom for a later decision (the window that can be brought back; the sky that can be held).
2. Tool behaviour. Lift, gamma and gain were designed for display referred signals; offset and the log wheels (shadows, midtones, highlights with adjustable range splits) behave predictably on a log signal, and contrast around a pivot behaves like printer lights. The same saturation move in log is even across the tonal range; in 709 it blows the already-saturated highlights.
3. Future deliverables. A grade performed in DWG and DI or ACEScct renders to Rec.709 today and to PQ 1000 nits tomorrow by changing the output transform and trimming. A grade performed in 709 must be redone.

## 8. Grading tools and technique

### 8.1 Node structure in Resolve

Node types: serial (one after the other, the default), parallel (several nodes fed by the same input and mixed by a Parallel Mixer; for secondaries that must not see each other's changes), layer (stacked with compositing modes, the top wins; for a look over a base), group (pre-clip and post-clip nodes applied to every clip in a group; timeline nodes applied to everything). Shared nodes share one correction across clips. Compound nodes fold a tree into one.

The house standard tree, left to right:

| Node | Name | Does |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | CST in | Camera log to DWG and DI (skip in RCM) |
| 02 | Balance | Offset and temperature and tint until the grey card or a neutral reads neutral on the parade |
| 03 | Exposure | Offset (log) or gain to place skin and key at target levels on the waveform |
| 04 | Contrast | Contrast and pivot; the pivot set to where the skin sits so skin does not move |
| 05 | Saturation | Global saturation, usually down for the house grade; colour boost for low-saturation areas if needed |
| 06 to 08 | Secondaries | Parallel nodes: skin (qualifier plus window), product or metal, sky or window |
| 09 | Look | Layer node: split tone, film emulation, grain, halation, at reduced key |
| 10 | CST out | DWG and DI to Rec.709 Gamma 2.4 with tone mapping (skip in RCM) |

Balance and exposure are done per shot; contrast, saturation and look are usually shared or grouped so the film holds together. A node labelled is a node that can be explained to a client in a session.

### 8.2 Primaries

- Lift, gamma, gain: the three-way corrector. Lift moves the shadows (and everything else a little), gamma the midtones, gain the highlights. Display referred by design; in a log working space use them lightly or not at all.
- Offset: shifts every code value equally; the equivalent of a printer light on film. In a log space it is the correct exposure and colour balance tool because it moves the whole image without changing contrast.
- Log wheels: shadows, midtones and highlights with range controls (Resolve defaults: low range 0.33, high range 0.66) so each wheel's influence is bounded. The precise tool for the muted grade's cool shadows and warm highlights.
- Contrast and pivot: contrast scales the image around the pivot value (Resolve default 0.435). Set the pivot to the skin's luminance so that adding contrast darkens shadows and brightens highlights while skin stays put.
- Saturation and colour boost: saturation scales all chroma equally; colour boost scales low-saturation areas more than high ones, so it adds colour to a muted image without pushing the already-saturated red dress into clipping.
- Temperature and tint: a blue to amber and a green to magenta shift modelled on camera white balance. Balance first with these, then refine with offset.

### 8.3 Curves

- Custom curves: an RGB or per-channel tone curve. The S-curve in the master curve is contrast with control; the per-channel curves are the split tone tool when a toe is pulled blue and a shoulder pushed warm.
- Hue vs hue: rotate one hue toward another (a magenta-leaning skin toward orange; a cyan sky toward blue).
- Hue vs sat: desaturate or saturate one hue (the house's first move on green foliage in Goa and on the saturated red of a Jaipur wall).
- Hue vs lum: brighten or darken one hue (the black-and-white conversion's main tool, section 9.6).
- Lum vs sat: saturation by brightness; pull saturation out of the deepest shadows and brightest highlights so they go neutral, the single most effective move toward a film look.
- Sat vs sat: compress the most saturated values; a gentle gamut-aware roll-off that keeps a saturated garment inside 709.
- Sat vs lum: brightness by saturation; rarely used.

### 8.4 Qualifiers, keys, windows, tracking

A qualifier selects pixels by colour (HSL, RGB, luminance, or the 3D picker); the result is a key (a matte of what is selected). Blur and shrink the key edge, and view it (the highlight toggle) before grading through it. A power window is a geometric mask (linear, circle, polygon, gradient, curve) that confines a correction. Tracking moves the window with the subject: the cloud tracker for most shots, the point tracker for a precise object, IntelliTrack for people. Magic Mask replaces most windows on people and objects. A secondary correction is any correction confined by a key, a window, or both. The rule: every secondary is checked at 400 percent on the edges and at full speed for chatter.

### 8.5 The skin tone line and skin handling

On the vectorscope (the scope that plots chroma as hue angle and saturation radius) healthy skin of every ethnicity falls along a line at roughly the same hue angle between red and yellow (the I line on vectorscopes that show it, at about 123 degrees in some displays' conventions). Skin varies in saturation (distance from centre) and luminance (not shown on the vectorscope; read it on the waveform), and very little in hue. Grade skin by: isolate with a qualifier, confirm the vector cluster sits on the line, move hue with hue vs hue if it drifts magenta (fluorescent light) or green (mixed light), set saturation with care, place luminance on the waveform where the look wants it (key-lit faces usually 55 to 70 IRE in a bright grade, 40 to 55 in a moody one). Section 9.1 gives the complexion-specific handling.

### 8.6 Matching shots, balance before look, split toning

Before any look, every shot in a scene must match: same black level, same skin level, same white balance, same saturation. Tools: Resolve's Shot Match (a starting point, never the end), split screen and wipe in the viewer against a reference still from the gallery, the parade scope (which shows red, green and blue waveforms side by side so a colour cast shows as unequal heights). A scene that cuts between a warm wide and a cool close-up reads as cheap before anyone can say why. Balance, then match, then look.

Why the order holds: a look is a relation between tones and hues, and a relation laid over unbalanced shots produces as many looks as there are shots. Balance (neutral greys, placed exposure) makes every shot a common starting point; match makes the scene one piece; the look then goes on once, on a shared or group node, and moves every shot the same distance.

Split toning is tinting the shadows one hue and the highlights another. The house split is cool shadows and warm highlights, built with the log wheels (shadow wheel a few degrees toward blue-cyan, highlight wheel toward amber, each at low strength) or with per-channel custom curves (blue raised slightly at the toe and lowered slightly at the shoulder). The test: a grey card placed in shadow reads faintly cool, the same card in the key reads faintly warm, and a white shirt in the midtones stays neutral on the parade. Skin is protected by keeping the skin branch outside the split (section 9.1). A complementary split pushed to strength is teal and orange (section 8.9); the house split is strong enough to be seen only when the look node is toggled off and on.

### 8.7 LUTs

A lookup table maps input values to output values. A 1D LUT maps each channel independently (contrast and gamma); a 3D LUT (.cube, usually 33 by 33 by 33 points) maps colour in three dimensions and can rotate hues.

- Technical (conversion) LUTs: camera log to Rec.709 (ARRI's LogC4 to Rec.709 LUT, Sony's S-Log3 to 709 LUTs), one working space to another. Exact, invertible in intent, placed where the pipeline needs the conversion (a CST node does the same with more control and no clipping).
- Creative LUTs: a look (a stock emulation, a colourist's style). Placed at the end of the tree (before the output CST in a managed pipeline, or on the timeline node) at reduced key (30 to 70 percent) so the look is a tint on a balanced grade.

Why not to grade through a creative LUT: a 3D LUT clips any value outside its input range (so a highlight pushed up after the LUT is gone), it is not invertible, its decisions about gamut and contrast are baked and cannot be adjusted per shot, and shots that were not balanced first inherit different looks from the same LUT. A LUT applied as the first node is the single most common amateur tell in grading.

### 8.8 Film emulation

Print film emulation reproduces the response of a projection print stock, which is where "the film look" mostly lives (the negative is comparatively neutral). The references:

- Kodak Vision Color Print Film 2383: the standard print stock, moderate contrast, clean colour, a slight cyan lean in shadows. Resolve ships a 2383 emulation LUT (several camera-specific variants).
- Kodak Vision Premier Color Print Film 2393: higher contrast and saturation than 2383; punchier blacks.
- Fujifilm Eterna-CP 3513DI: Fuji's digital intermediate print stock, softer, cooler, gentler reds. Fuji ended motion print production in 2013; the emulations remain.

Tools: Dehancer (a plugin for Resolve, Premiere and Final Cut Pro that models negative stocks, print stocks, halation, bloom, grain, gate weave and film damage as separate stages; prefer its negative plus print chain at reduced strength), FilmConvert Nitrate (from 119 USD one-time, stock emulation adapted to the camera's colour science via camera packs, with a grain model that varies by exposure and colour; a full log pipeline), and Resolve's Film Look Creator (ResolveFX since 19, 2024: a single node with film stock, print, halation, bloom, grain, gate weave and flicker controls built for DWG and DI). The house default: Film Look Creator or a 2383 LUT at 40 to 60 percent key, grain from the same node, halation and bloom at the lowest setting that is visible on a reference monitor and invisible on a phone.

Halation is the red-orange glow around bright highlights caused by light scattering back through the film base; bloom is the soft spread of highlights in the lens and emulsion; grain is the visible silver halide structure. All three signal photochemical origin and all three read as fake when they are large. Restraint: if a client can name the effect, it is too strong.

### 8.9 Looks the house avoids

- Bleach bypass: skipping the bleach stage in processing leaves silver in the image, raising contrast and cutting saturation (Seven, 1995; Saving Private Ryan, 1998). Strong, dated, hostile to skin and to gold. Out.
- Teal and orange: pushing shadows cyan and skin orange to maximise complementary contrast; the blockbuster default of 2005 to 2015. It turns every interior into an aquarium and every face into a tan. The house avoids it because it is the most recognisable grade in the world and recognisable means cheap.
- The Instagram filter problem: pre-baked looks (lifted matte blacks, faded highlights, orange skin, a vignette) that an audience raised on filters recognises instantly. The audience's literacy means a filtered grade reads as a filter, so the house grade must look like no grade at all.

### 8.10 The muted luxury grade

The house grade, defined in scope targets (read on scopes with the output transform applied):

- Saturation lowered: global saturation about 80 to 90 percent of the balanced image, then colour boost if faces go grey. The most saturated object in frame is the product or a single accent.
- Blacks lifted with care: the deepest black between 2 and 5 IRE on the waveform (64 to about 90 in 10 bit code values), never crushed to 0 and never lifted into milk above 6. A true black exists somewhere in frame (a shadow, a lash) so the image has an anchor.
- Warm skin, cool shadows: log wheels push shadows a few degrees toward blue-cyan and highlights toward warm; skin stays on the line. The split is small enough that a neutral grey wall still reads grey.
- True whites: a white object reads neutral on the parade (equal RGB) at 90 to 100 IRE; no cream cast on linen, no cyan cast on marble.
- Contrast: moderate, with a soft shoulder (the highlight roll-off) and no hard clip; the window in a hotel room is bright and still has texture.
- Texture retained: no noise reduction beyond what the sensor needs, grain fine and visible only on a monitor.

References: the stills of Aman, the films of Loro Piana and Brunello Cucinelli, the editorial grade of The Gentlewoman's video work. The grade should be mistaken for good light.

### 8.11 HDR grading

PQ (perceptual quantizer, SMPTE ST 2084, standardised 2014, developed by Dolby) is an absolute transfer function: a code value maps to a fixed luminance from 0 to 10,000 cd/m2 (nits). HLG (hybrid log-gamma, developed by the BBC and NHK, ARIB STD-B67) is relative: a gamma curve below, a log curve above, with a nominal peak of 1,000 nits in ITU-R BT.2100 and some compatibility with SDR displays. BT.2100 (2016) defines both, with Rec.2020 primaries. HDR10 uses PQ with static metadata; Dolby Vision uses PQ with dynamic metadata (per shot or per frame) and supports up to 12 bit; HDR10+ is Samsung's dynamic metadata.

Mastering: a 1,000 nit grade on a 1,000 nit reference display is the practical standard. Dolby's published Tier 1 reference display requirement (professional.dolby.com, read October 2026) is peak luminance of at least 1,000 nits, black level at or below 0.001 nits and intra-frame contrast of at least 1,000,000:1; 4,000 nit monitors exist for feature work. Dolby's named targets are Rec.709 SDR at 100 nits, HDR PQ in P3 or Rec.2020 at 600 nits, cinema SDR P3 at 48 nits and Dolby Cinema PQ P3 at 108 nits; the full artistic trim controls need the Dolby Vision trims licence, a one-time 1,000 USD purchase usable on several machines in one facility (same page). Diffuse white (a white card) sits at about 203 nits per ITU-R BT.2408, and skin around 100 to 200 nits; the headroom above is reserved for speculars and light sources, and the picture as a whole stays near its SDR brightness. Dolby Vision analysis generates L1 metadata (per-shot minimum, average and maximum luminance) automatically; L2 and L8 trims are manual per-scene adjustments that control how the content maps to 100 nit, 600 nit and other targets. Resolve's MultiMaster Trim Manager (21) produces the SDR and HDR trim deliverables from one timeline. The HDR versus SDR trim: the SDR derivative from an HDR grade needs its own pass (highlights compressed, saturation checked) and the house delivers SDR as the primary and HDR only when the platform and the client's displays justify it (YouTube, Apple TV, in-store screens that are actually HDR).

### 8.12 Monitoring

- Reference monitor: a calibrated display (Flanders Scientific, Sony BVM or PVM, Eizo ColorEdge CG, or a consumer OLED calibrated with a probe and a LUT box as a budget option) fed by a video output card (Blackmagic DeckLink or UltraStudio), never the GUI display, because the GUI path is colour managed by the operating system and the video path is not.
- SDR targets: 100 nits peak white, D65 white point, gamma 2.4 (BT.1886), in a dim room with a neutral grey surround per ITU-R BT.2035; a bias light at D65 behind the monitor.
- The Mac display caveat: Apple displays default to a P3 gamut and a colour managed pipeline with a 1.96 to 2.2 interpretation of 709 material (section 12.5). The HDTV Video (BT.709, BT.1886) reference mode on Pro Display XDR and MacBook Pro XDR panels approximates the standard and is acceptable for review; it is still a GUI path and still not a reference monitor. Resolve's colour managed viewer (Use Mac Display Color Profiles for Viewers) reduces the mismatch for the operator.
- Phone check: every social deliverable is viewed on an iPhone and an Android phone at 50 percent and 100 percent brightness before approval, because that is the display the audience has.

### 8.13 Reading scopes

- Waveform (luma): brightness per column of pixels, 0 to 100 IRE (or 0 to 1023). Read black level, white clip, skin placement.
- RGB parade: three waveforms side by side; unequal heights on a neutral object mean a cast. The balance scope.
- Vectorscope: hue and saturation, with targets for the 75 percent bars and the skin line. Read skin, casts and over-saturation.
- Histogram: distribution of values; useful for seeing clipping at either end, little else.
- CIE chromaticity: the gamut diagram; shows whether colours fall outside Rec.709 (and Rec.2020 for HDR) so the sat vs sat curve or a gamut mapping can bring them in before the encoder clips them.

## 9. Grading by category

### 9.1 Skin across Indian and Gulf complexions

Most Mask360 talent sits at Fitzpatrick phototypes III to VI (Thomas Fitzpatrick's classification of skin by its response to sun, types I to VI, proposed in 1975 and published in Archives of Dermatology in 1988). On the scopes this means: the hue angle is the same as any skin (the line, section 8.5), chroma is higher for deeper skin, and luminance is lower, so deeper skin occupies the lower, more saturated end of the same vector. Two failures follow from treating all skin as one setting.

Grey skin. Causes: global desaturation applied before skin is isolated (the muted grade pulls deeper skin toward grey first, because its chroma is a larger share of its signal); the blue-cyan shadow split landing on skin in shadow (a face half in shadow turns green-grey on one side); underexposure lifted with gain, which adds grey without adding colour; mixed light (the green spike of fluorescent tubes in Mumbai offices, the magenta of some LED panels). Fixes, in order: balance on a grey card or a known neutral; qualify skin (HSL qualifier, soft edge, blur 3 to 5) and confirm the cluster sits on the line; restore chroma on the skin key with hue vs sat (plus 5 to plus 15 on the skin range) after the global desaturation, so skin is the one surface the muted grade does not mute; keep the shadow split off the skin key (the skin node is a parallel branch, and the look node's key excludes it); correct mixed light with tint on the skin key or hue vs hue.

Orange skin. Causes: warming with gain or the highlight wheel (deeper skin's speculars live in the highlight range, so a warm highlight move lands on the face); teal and orange (section 8.9); saturation added globally with no skin key; warm LUTs at full key. Fixes: warm with offset in log, which moves the whole image and keeps skin's relation to the ground; cap skin chroma so the cluster never reaches further from the centre than the most saturated product colour in frame; read the parade on the face: red should lead green and green lead blue, each by a small margin; a face with red far above green and blue near the floor is orange.

Luminance placement by phototype, read on the waveform with the output transform applied, key-lit faces in a bright grade (house targets): I to II at 60 to 70 IRE, III to IV at 50 to 62, V to VI at 35 to 52. These follow the lighting; the rule on set (expose for the deepest skin in the frame and light the fairer skin down with negative fill) is cheaper than any grade. Never lift a deeper face to match a fairer one in a two-shot: grade each face to its own placement and let the light carry the difference. The reference is the balanced still of each person under daylight, held in the gallery and wiped against every shot they appear in.

### 9.2 Jewellery and gold

Gold reads as gold only against a true neutral. The yellow cast problem: a warm white balance on set (a 3200 K setting under 4300 K light, or a warm LUT) pushes the whole frame yellow, and the gold has nothing to be yellower than. Rule: white balance to a grey card under the key, keep a white surface in frame (the display pad, a cuff, marble) and confirm it reads neutral on the parade; the gold then separates by itself.

Metals by colour: 22 karat (91.6 percent gold, the Indian bridal standard, hallmarked by the Bureau of Indian Standards; hallmarking became mandatory in phases from 16 June 2021, and the six-character HUID code became the only valid hallmark from 1 April 2023) is a deeper yellow-orange than 18 karat (75 percent, the Gulf and European norm), which is paler and slightly greener; rose gold's copper reads pink; white gold is rhodium plated and must read neutral white, as must platinum and silver. Grade each to its physical sample under the bias light, because a buyer compares the film to the piece in the vitrine.

Speculars on metal clip to white, never to yellow: pull chroma out of the top of the range with lum vs sat (a roll-off from about 85 IRE to 100), so the highlight is the white a reflection is expected to be. Diamonds: the fire (dispersion of white light into spectral colours) is the product, so the lum vs sat roll-off is relaxed on a diamond window and the small spectral flashes keep their chroma. Coloured stones: emerald, ruby and sapphire are each a sale of a hue, so a saturated stone has its own window and is excluded from sat vs sat compression; the hue is checked against the gemmologist's description (a Zambian emerald is bluer-green than a Colombian), never against taste. Backgrounds: desaturated and 20 to 40 IRE below the metal so the metal is the brightest saturated object. The metal and stone palette is in 04-colour.md.

### 9.3 Beauty

Clean, slightly cool ground, skin warm against it: the background graded 100 to 300 K cooler than the skin (a small cyan-blue offset on a window that excludes skin) so skin reads warm without being warmed. Skin texture retained at 100 percent on the reference monitor (section 10.1). Highlights on skin white, never pink: pink speculars come from a magenta tint or a rose-leaning LUT, and are neutralised with lum vs sat at the top. Lips, eyes and the product colour are the saturated elements; everything else sits under them. Beauty is the category where the grade must be invisible: the correct beauty grade reads as a well-lit photograph. References: the clinical cleanliness of Aesop's and Glossier's film work, the restraint of Dior Beauty's campaign films (the beauty reference world is in design-taste, references/worlds.md).

### 9.4 Hospitality interiors

The materials are the story: teak, sandstone, marble, brass, linen. Each has a colour the guest has touched, so a cast reads as wrong before it is named. Rules: one white point per scene, usually the interior lamps (2700 to 3000 K), with windows allowed to go cooler by the equivalent of 1,000 to 2,000 K, which reads as daylight through glass; a window pulled down 0.5 to 1 stop with a luminance qualifier inside a soft power window, with the shoulder kept soft so the garden outside has texture (the on-set answer, neutral density gel on the glass or an exposure blend, is better); no HDR sheen (the tone-mapped, exposure-fused look of estate-agent photography: flat local contrast, halos at edges, every surface equally lit), which comes from pulling highlights and shadows toward the middle; the pool keeps its own colour (a pool reads cyan because of its tiles). Wood under tungsten reads orange if the white point is daylight; stone under daylight reads blue if the white point is tungsten; the scene is balanced to the light it is lit by. The look to reach: the room as the guest saw it at 6 pm. References: the property films of Aman and Six Senses, the sandstone of Amanbagh at dusk.

### 9.5 Food

Appetite colours: warm reds, oranges and browns, the yellow of ghee and saffron, the green of fresh herbs. A green cast kills appetite. It comes from fluorescent light (the mercury line at about 546 nm), from low-CRI LED (colour rendering index, a measure of how faithfully a light renders colour against a reference source, where food wants CRI 95 or above, or TLCI 95 or above on the television lighting consistency index) and from bounce off green walls or foliage. Fix: tint toward magenta on balance, then hue vs hue to pull the yellow-green of herbs back to true green, hue vs sat to raise reds and oranges 5 to 10 percent, a small midtone lift so the food is brighter than its plate. Steam, oil speculars and glass highlights read white. The house avoids the orange wash (everything warmed until the plate is orange) and the dark moody food look, which reads as a 2019 restaurant feed. References: the macro grade of Chef's Table (Netflix, 2015 onward); daylight thali photography from the house's own shoots.

### 9.6 Fashion

The editorial grade: lower contrast than a commercial, shadow detail retained, highlights with a soft shoulder, film emulation as the base, saturation low except on the garment. The garment's colour is the product: the sample is on the grading desk under the bias light and the hue on screen is checked against it, because a buyer returns a coat that is a different blue from the film. Skin and garment are graded on separate keys so the garment can be true while skin stays on the line.

Black and white conversion craft: never a desaturate, which maps every colour to the same tone by luminance alone. Convert through a channel mixer or hue vs lum so that colours map to tones by choice, as filters did on film: a red filter darkens blue skies and lightens skin, a yellow or orange filter is the portrait standard, a green filter darkens skin and lightens foliage. The house monochrome: a true black exists, the highlights have texture, the midtones are rich and separated, grain is fine and present, skin is one to two zones lighter than the ground. Convert before the look node so grain and halation sit on the monochrome. References: Peter Lindbergh's Pirelli calendar of 2017 (unretouched, monochrome), Paolo Roversi's low-contrast colour, Nick Knight's SHOWstudio films for saturated colour used as an idea (see design-taste, references/canon.md).

### 9.7 Real estate

Truthful colour is a legal matter as well as a taste one. In India, section 12 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 makes a promoter liable for loss caused by false or misleading statements in an advertisement or prospectus; in Dubai, the Real Estate Regulatory Agency under the Dubai Land Department requires a permit for every property advertisement and holds advertisers to accuracy. A grade that turns a grey sea blue or a brown lawn green is a misrepresentation that the agency shares in. Rules: sky, sea, grass and facade as shot; twilight blends (an exterior at dusk with the interior lights on, composited from a daylight plate and a dusk plate) are acceptable when every light shown exists; no HDR sheen; no sky replacement without disclosure to the client in the manifest (section 12.11); renders marked as renders in a super. The luxury real estate grade is the hospitality grade (9.4) applied to an empty building.

### 9.8 Social

Brighter and punchier within reason, because the display is a phone at 50 to 100 percent brightness in daylight (Mumbai and Dubai sun, where the iPhone 15 Pro and later reach 1,000 nits typical and up to 2,000 nits peak outdoors, and Samsung's 2024 and 2025 flagships claim 2,600 nits peak) and the viewing surround is bright, which lowers perceived contrast. The social trim from the master: contrast plus 5 to 10 percent around the skin pivot, saturation plus 5 to 10 percent with skin protected, midtones lifted 2 to 4 IRE, blacks held, no vignette, no filter. Phone display targets: iPhones are P3, colour managed, and show a Rec.709 file as intended; Android varies by maker and by the vivid display mode most users leave on, which adds saturation. Check on both before approval, outdoors, at noon. The muted luxury grade survives this trim; a filter does not.

## 10. Retouching and cleanup in post

### 10.1 Beauty retouching ethics and the natural skin standard

Law first, because Gulf and Indian luxury clients also advertise in Europe. France: decree no. 2017-738 (in force 1 October 2017) requires the label "photographie retouchée" on commercial images where a model's body shape has been made thinner or thicker, with a fine of up to 37,500 EUR or 30 percent of the advertising spend. Norway: the amendment to the Marketing Control Act in force from 1 July 2022 requires a standard label on advertising images and video where a body's shape, size or skin has been altered, including influencer posts. Israel: the law passed in 2012 and in force from 2013 requires labelling of altered images and a minimum body mass index of 18.5 for models. United Kingdom: no labelling law, but the Advertising Standards Authority upholds complaints against misleading retouching under the CAP Code (the 2011 rulings against Lancôme's Julia Roberts and Maybelline's Christy Turlington campaigns banned the ads for exaggerating the product's effect). India: the Advertising Standards Council of India code requires that advertisements be truthful and not mislead by implication, so a retouched "after" in a skincare ad is a claim. UAE: the National Media Authority (NMA), created by a Federal Decree-Law issued on 18 December 2025 to take over the functions of the UAE Media Council, the National Media Office and WAM, sets the content standards, which require honesty in advertising; anyone publishing promotional content on social media, paid or unpaid, needs the Advertiser Permit (launched by the Media Council on 30 July 2025, enforcement deadline extended to 31 January 2026, free for the first three years, fines reported at up to 1 million AED for a first offence).

The house natural skin standard, written into every beauty and portrait scope:

- Texture retained at 100 percent on the reference monitor: pores, fine lines, vellus hair.
- Removed: temporary marks only (a blemish, a scratch, a bruise, a stray hair across the eye), with the talent's agreement where the mark is on the face.
- Retained: moles, scars, pigmentation, asymmetry and every permanent feature, unless the talent asks in writing.
- No reshaping of body or face. Face Reshaper and its equivalents (section 6.2) stay off.
- Smoothing capped where texture remains visible at 100 percent; in practice 10 to 25 percent strength.
- A retouching log in the delivery manifest (section 12.11): what changed, on which shots, and why.

The reason beyond law: imperfection is the 2026 counter-signal to generated imagery (see design-taste, section 2), and a client who sees pores sees a real person, which is what a luxury campaign sells.

### 10.2 Skin smoothing in Resolve and its limits

Resolve Studio's Face Refinement ResolveFX detects and tracks faces and offers smoothing, shine removal and separate controls for eyes, lips and teeth; the Beauty ResolveFX smooths by frequency (low-frequency tone smoothed, high-frequency texture kept and restored with a texture control, the video equivalent of frequency separation in stills retouching); Blemish Removal (21) tracks and removes a single spot. Limits: above about 30 percent the skin turns to plastic, hair edges smear, the effect chatters when the face turns, and the node is heavy on the GPU, so it is render-cached. Workflow: smoothing on its own node, keyed to the face by Magic Mask inside a window, strength 10 to 25 percent, texture restore on, checked at 100 percent and 400 percent, and switched off on any shot that does not need it, because smoothing on a face already in soft light is a tell. Premiere has no native equivalent; Digital Anarchy's Beauty Box plugin is the standard there.

### 10.3 Object removal

Static camera: a clean plate (a frame of the set without the object) composited over the object through a mask, or Resolve's Patch Replacer. Moving camera: Resolve Studio's Object Removal ResolveFX (tracks the area and synthesises the background from neighbouring frames), After Effects Content-Aware Fill (since 2019, with a reference frame for hard cases), Runway's inpainting, or a paint node in Fusion for a frame-by-frame job. Adobe previewed object addition and removal for Premiere in 2025; its state in the current build is checked before it is relied on. Rules: check at 400 percent and at speed for shimmer; removing a thing that changes what is being sold (the construction crane beside the hotel, the neighbour's tower from the penthouse window) is a disclosure question for the account lead, and for real estate it falls under section 9.7.

### 10.4 Stabilisation

Premiere's Warp Stabilizer (Subspace Warp is its default method) warps the frame to counter motion; its tells are the wobble on verticals (a doorframe that breathes), background elements that move against the foreground, and the crop and scale it applies to hide the edges (often 3 to 10 percent). Resolve's stabiliser offers Perspective, Similarity and Translation modes plus Camera Lock; Similarity and Translation warp nothing, so they are the luxury choice, with the crop accepted. Rules: stabilise only footage with crop to spare (a 4K source for a 1080 delivery); check verticals against a straight edge; never stabilise a shot that was meant to be handheld, because the grammar of the take changes; shoot on a gimbal, a slider or a tripod when stillness is the idea. The best stabilisation is none.

### 10.5 Reframing and auto reframe for vertical

The arithmetic that decides the strategy: a 9:16 crop of a 1920 by 1080 frame is 608 by 1080 pixels, which must be scaled 178 percent to reach 1080 by 1920 and is soft; the same crop of a 3840 by 2160 frame is 1215 by 2160, delivered at 1080 by 1920 with no upscale. A 4:5 crop of 1080p is 864 by 1080 (scaled 125 percent to 1080 by 1350); of UHD, 1728 by 2160 (downscaled). Shoot in UHD or above for anything that will be reframed, with the 9:16 frame lines on the monitor.

Tools: Premiere's Auto Reframe (since 2019) and Resolve Studio's Smart Reframe (since 17) track the subject and keyframe the crop; Final Cut Pro's Smart Conform does the same. They suit b-roll with one subject. The house rule: hero shots, product shots and anything carrying a title are reframed by hand with position keyframes from the graded master, because an auto reframe that follows a face leaves the product at the edge and one that follows the product cuts the face. A composition with the subject at the edge of the 16:9 frame has no 9:16 version; another take is used. Reframing happens after the grade, from the graded master, so every ratio matches.

### 10.6 Speed changes with optical flow

Three methods: frame sampling (frames dropped or repeated; stutters), frame blending (dissolves between frames; ghosts), optical flow (new frames synthesised from estimated motion; Resolve Studio's Speed Warp is the current best). Optical flow tears on crossing edges, on fine repeated detail (a jewellery chain, fabric weave, hair against a window) and on fast motion; it is invisible on slow, large, soft-edged motion. Rules: shoot the frame rate the slow motion needs (50, 60, 100 or 120 fps) and conform in the timeline, which costs nothing; use optical flow only for ramps that stay above 50 percent speed and never on fine detail; check every ramp at full speed and at 400 percent. Frame-rate conversion is the related case: a 24 fps source on a 25 fps timeline is conformed at 104.17 percent speed with the audio pitch corrected, because interpolating 24 into 25 adds judder on every pan (section 14).

### 10.7 Noise reduction

Resolve Studio's temporal noise reduction (1 to 5 frames, motion estimation, luma and chroma thresholds) first, spatial second, both on a dedicated node early in the tree; Neat Video as a plugin where the native tools fail. The tells: waxy skin, ghosting on motion, a dead flatness where grain should be. The house rule: denoise then regrain, with the grain from the film emulation node, so the image keeps a structure; footage under ISO 1600 on a full-frame sensor usually needs none, and above 3200 gets temporal reduction at light settings. A face is never reduced more than its background.

### 10.8 Sharpening

Almost never. Cameras sharpen internally, encoders preserve edges, phones have 400 to 460 pixels per inch, and halos on edges are the first thing a trained eye sees. Where a shot is genuinely soft: a small amount of Resolve's Sharpen Edges on luma only, or a mid-frequency contrast move, with skin excluded; UltraSharpen and Motion Deblur (21) on inanimate detail only. A soft shot is usually better cut shorter than sharpened.

### 10.9 AI upscaling

Resolve Studio's Super Scale (2x, 3x and 4x with sharpness and noise controls) and Topaz Video AI are the working tools; both invent detail, so the result is checked on skin and on text, where invention shows. Legitimate uses: an archival 1080p master upscaled to UHD for YouTube (section 12.2, because YouTube's UHD ladder uses a better codec), a phone clip inside a brand film, a crop that went too far. Each is declared in the manifest as upscaled. Never on a product shot the client will zoom into; that is a reshoot. The generative tools and their disclosure rules are in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md.

## 11. Captions and titles in the edit

### 11.1 Burned in versus sidecar

Open captions are burned into the picture; closed captions travel as a sidecar file the player draws. SRT (SubRip) is a plain-text file of numbered cues, each with a start and end time in hours, minutes, seconds and milliseconds separated by the SRT separator token (two hyphens and a greater-than sign), then the text, with no styling. WebVTT (the W3C format, header line WEBVTT, a dot before the milliseconds) adds positioning and styling classes and is what HTML5 players and most streaming platforms use. Platform acceptance as of October 2026: YouTube takes SRT, VTT and SBV uploads and generates its own; Facebook takes SRT on uploaded video; Instagram and TikTok generate captions and allow editing in the app, with no sidecar upload for organic posts; LinkedIn accepts SRT on native video; X accepts SRT on uploaded video. Delivery rule: every social master in both forms, a burned-in version for Reels, Stories and TikTok (where the caption's design is part of the piece and the platforms' automatic Hindi and Arabic captions are unreliable) and a clean version plus SRT for YouTube, LinkedIn and the client's site. The clean version is the one that gets localised. WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.2.2 (captions for prerecorded video, level A) applies to the client's site; see 11-accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md.

### 11.2 Styling rules

Type: Space Grotesk 500 for English per the house layer (design-taste, section 7), with the Devanagari and Arabic companions set out in 03-typography.md. Size on a 1080 by 1920 canvas: 56 to 72 px for the English line (an x-height, which is what the eye measures, of about 30 to 38 px); never below 48 px, because the phone shows the frame at about 1,000 px tall. Lines: at most two, at most 37 characters each (the BBC subtitle guideline figure; Netflix allows 42 for English), broken at a phrase boundary. Colour: white on dark grounds, ink on light, no outline, no box; where legibility fails, a 20 to 30 percent darkening gradient band behind the caption zone, or a different shot. No word-by-word karaoke captions, no bouncing words, no emoji, no ALL CAPS unless the brand's voice is caps; the TikTok default styles are the cheap tell (design-taste, section 3). Arabic: the shaping is tested in the exact tool version, because Fusion's Text+ has a history of breaking Arabic ligatures and right-to-left ordering, while Premiere's Essential Graphics shapes Arabic and Devanagari correctly when the text engine is set for the script; Arabic titles are rendered from After Effects or a vector file if the editor cannot shape them. Hook captions and social cadence are in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md; the words on every caption follow mask360-copywriting.

### 11.3 Placement under platform UI

Captions sit inside the tightest safe zone of every placement the asset will run in (26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 5.6, verified 28 October 2026). On a 1080 by 1920 Reels or Stories ad the clear area starts 269 px from the top and ends 1,248 px from the top (the bottom 672 px are reserved), with 65 px each side; captions therefore sit with their bottom edge no lower than about 1,200 px, centred horizontally, which places the text in the viewer's lower-middle field and above the caption, audio and share chrome. TikTok's working default (top about 290, bottom about 480, right column about 200) is the more restrictive case on the right. A 4:5 feed asset has no overlay; captions sit at 85 to 90 percent of the height. The top 14 percent stays clear of anything that must be read.

### 11.4 Timing

On at the first frame of the spoken word, or 2 frames before; off 2 to 4 frames after the phrase ends, or at the cut, whichever comes first, because a caption that crosses a cut makes the cut visible (the BBC guideline asks that subtitles avoid straddling shot changes). Minimum duration 1 second (Netflix's minimum is five sixths of a second), maximum 7 seconds. Reading speed: up to 20 characters per second for adult English (Netflix's figure); the house uses 15 to 17 for Hindi and Arabic because Devanagari conjuncts and Arabic ligatures carry more per character than Latin, so character counts understate the reading load. A gap of at least 2 frames between cues so the eye registers the change.

### 11.5 Bilingual captions

English with Hindi, English with Arabic: the house delivers a separate version per language, because stacked bilingual captions exceed the two-line limit and halve the reading time for each. Where the client insists on one asset, the format is one line per language, the local language above (Hindi in Devanagari, or Arabic right-to-left and centred), English below, sizes equal, the whole block inside the safe zone. Hinglish lines follow the brand's voice: Devanagari for a Hindi line, Roman script where the voice is spoken Hinglish (mask360-copywriting decides). Arabic versions of a piece mirror any directional graphic (progress, reading-order layouts) and keep the numerals the client uses (Western Arabic numerals are the Gulf retail norm; Eastern Arabic numerals where the brand's Arabic identity uses them; see 03-typography.md).

### 11.6 Titles, supers and end frames

Supers (text over picture: a name, a price, a legal line) hold for a reading time of 1 second plus about 0.3 seconds per word, and the legal line for twice that; broadcasters set a minimum legal-text height and the channel's spec is checked before lock. Lower thirds in the house are one line, left aligned to the action-safe edge, Space Grotesk, no bar. End frame: mark, name, one line at most, held 3 seconds or more, with the sound resolving under it. Titles are rendered from vector (Fusion, After Effects or the Essential Graphics panel) at the master's resolution, never from a PNG scaled up, and the textless master is rendered first so every title can be re-laid for each ratio and language.

## 12. Delivery specifications

### 12.1 Masters

- Codec: Apple ProRes 422 HQ (10 bit, 4:2:2, about 184 Mbps at 1080p25 and about 737 Mbps at UHD 25) for every graded master; ProRes 4444 (12 bit, 4:4:4, alpha) for anything with an alpha channel, for heavy graphic content, and when the master will be regraded. DNxHR HQX is the Avid-world equivalent where a broadcaster asks for MXF.
- Resolution: native (UHD 3840 by 2160 or DCI 4K 4096 by 2160 for a 4K job; 1920 by 1080 for a 1080 job). Never a 1080 upscale labelled 4K.
- Frame rate: 25 fps for India and the UAE (the 50 Hz television regions) and for social in these markets, 24 fps for cinema and for films that will become a DCP, 30 fps only where a platform or a client's US market requires it. Decided at the shoot; converting later costs quality (section 10.6).
- Colour: Rec.709, gamma 2.4, legal range, tagged (section 12.5). HDR masters as PQ 1,000 nit in ProRes 4444 XQ, or as an IMF package (Interoperable Master Format, SMPTE ST 2067: a component master of picture, sound and subtitle tracks with a playlist per version) when a platform asks (section 8.11).
- Versions: titled; textless (clean of every title and super, the localisation base); with and without the end card; with and without burned captions; each ratio (section 12.6); each language.
- Sound: the full mix at broadcast loudness (-23 LUFS integrated, -1 dBTP) and at social loudness (about -14 LUFS, -1 dBTP), plus stems (dialogue, music and effects, called DME) as 48 kHz 24 bit WAV, with the music licence reference in the manifest. Loudness standards and measurement are in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 13.
- A mezzanine: an H.264 at 50 Mbps or a ProRes 422 at 1080p for the client's own re-encodes, so no one ever re-encodes from a social file.

### 12.2 Platform encodes

H.264 (AVC) High profile, level (the cap the profile places on resolution, frame rate and bitrate) 4.2 for 1080p up to 60 fps and level 5.1 or 5.2 for UHD, 2-pass variable bitrate (the first pass measures complexity, the second spends the bits where motion and detail need them), CABAC entropy coding (context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding, the more efficient of H.264's two lossless final stages), 2 consecutive B-frames (frames predicted from both earlier and later frames), closed GOP (group of pictures: the run of frames from one keyframe to the next; closed means no frame refers outside its group) with a keyframe every half-second to 2 seconds (YouTube asks for a GOP of half the frame rate), 4:2:0 8 bit, MP4 with the moov atom (the index) at the front so playback starts before the download ends. House target bitrates, set above each platform's minimum so the file survives the platform's own re-encode: 1080p 10 to 16 Mbps (YouTube's figure is 8 Mbps at 24 to 30 fps and 12 at 48 to 60), UHD 35 to 50 Mbps (YouTube: 35 to 45, and 53 to 68 at high frame rate). H.265 (HEVC) at about 60 percent of the H.264 rate for the same quality (the published verification tests by Ohm and others in 2012 found about 50 percent savings at equal subjective quality; the house uses 60 because social encoders are conservative) where the platform accepts it (YouTube, Vimeo, Apple devices); Meta re-encodes everything, so H.264 is simpler there. UHD is uploaded to YouTube even for a 1080 film, because YouTube serves VP9 or AV1 to UHD uploads and a lower ladder to 1080 uploads. Instagram and TikTok (TikTok for UAE and GCC accounts only, section 6.5; in India the vertical set is Reels and Shorts): H.264, 1080 by 1920, 30 fps fixed (Meta's ads guide) or the native 25, AAC 128 kbps or above, under 4 GB, 10 to 16 Mbps. LinkedIn: MP4, 75 KB to 500 MB, 3 seconds to 30 minutes, under 30 fps (verified in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md). X: MP4 H.264 with AAC, with 512 MB and 140 seconds as the long-standing limits for standard accounts and longer, larger uploads for Premium subscribers (X's help pages could not be reached for this file; confirmed before any X campaign).

### 12.3 Audio in the encode

AAC-LC at 320 kbps (the ceiling of the AAC encoders in Resolve and Premiere; YouTube's recommendation is 384 kbps for stereo, so 320 is the nearest), 48 kHz, stereo, with the loudness set for the platform (section 12.1 and 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 13): about -14 LUFS integrated for YouTube and Instagram, -16 for Apple's platforms, -23 for broadcast, and true peak at -1 dBTP always, because lossy encoding adds up to 1 dB of overshoot. Mono content is delivered as dual mono (the same signal on both channels) so a phone's single speaker and a pair of earphones both get it. 5.1 only for cinema and for broadcast that asks.

### 12.4 Chroma subsampling

4:4:4 carries full colour resolution; 4:2:2 halves the chroma horizontally; 4:2:0 halves it both ways, so a 4:2:0 file has one quarter of the colour samples of its luma. Delivery is 4:2:0 because every platform and consumer device decodes it and the eye resolves colour coarsely. Masters are 4:2:2 (ProRes 422 HQ) because keying, secondaries and a later regrade need chroma resolution; graphics and alpha are 4:4:4. The visible consequence of 4:2:0: thin saturated text (a red line on white) bleeds colour at its edges, so titles are kept large and high in contrast, and the house renders titles into the 4:2:2 master before the 4:2:0 encode.

### 12.5 Colour tags and the QuickTime gamma shift

MP4 and MOV files carry colour tags (the nclc or colr atom): colour primaries, transfer characteristics and matrix coefficients, each a code. 1-1-1 means BT.709 for all three; 9-16-9 is Rec.2020 primaries with the PQ transfer and the Rec.2020 matrix; 9-18-9 is the HLG equivalent. Files exported from Resolve and Premiere as Rec.709 carry 1-1-1. The gamma shift: Apple's colour-managed path (ColorSync, used by QuickTime Player, Safari, Final Cut Pro and Preview) decodes a 1-1-1 file with the inverse of the BT.709 camera curve, close to a power of 1.96, while the grade was judged on a display decoding at 2.4 (BT.1886), so the same file looks lifted and flatter on a Mac than on the reference monitor and on most other players, which decode at the display's own gamma near 2.2. The house handling: judge the grade on the reference monitor and on the target phones; explain the Mac preview to the client in one sentence before the first review; for review files that will be watched on Macs, export from Resolve with the output colour space Rec.709-A (an Apple-specific tagging option, in Resolve since 17, that makes the Apple path display the file as graded); keep the master at Rec.709 gamma 2.4 with 1-1-1 tags; never bake a counter-gamma into a master, because then every other display is wrong. Untagged files are read as BT.709 by YouTube, which also converts full-range files to legal and wide-gamut SDR to 8 bit BT.709, so files are tagged correctly and delivered legal.

### 12.8 DCP for cinema

A Digital Cinema Package is a folder of MXF files (picture, sound, subtitles) with XML metadata (the CPL, or composition playlist; the PKL, or packing list; the ASSETMAP), as specified by Digital Cinema Initiatives in the DCI Digital Cinema System Specification (the 1.4 series; the current point version is checked at dcimovies.com) and the SMPTE ST 429 family. Values: picture as JPEG 2000 at 12 bits per component in X'Y'Z' colour (CIE XYZ encoded with a 2.6 gamma, mastered against DCI-P3 at 48 cd/m2, which is 14 foot-lamberts); containers 2048 by 1080 (2K) and 4096 by 2160 (4K), with Flat at 1998 by 1080 or 3996 by 2160 (1.85:1) and Scope at 2048 by 858 or 4096 by 1716 (2.39:1); maximum picture bitrate 250 Mbit/s (older servers are safer at about 200 to 235, so the house encodes advertisements at 200 to 230); 24 fps as the universal rate, with 25, 30, 48, 50 and 60 at 2K and 25 and 30 at 4K allowed in SMPTE DCPs and accepted by most modern servers, confirmed with the chain before the encode; audio as 24 bit 48 kHz PCM, 5.1 or 7.1 (a stereo mix is placed in left and right with the centre silent, or upmixed by the mixer), at the cinema loudness convention of 85 dB Leq(m) (the trailer level set by TASA, the Trailer Audio Standards Association, which chains commonly apply to advertisements; the chain's own figure governs); SMPTE DCP as the current flavour and Interop as the older one still accepted; no encryption, and therefore no KDM (key delivery message, the per-screen, time-limited key file that decrypts an encrypted DCP), for advertising. Naming follows the ISDCF Digital Cinema Naming Convention field order (title, content type, aspect, language and subtitle, territory, audio, resolution, date, version), for example TITLE_ADV_F_EN-XX_IN_51_2K_20261028_OV. Tools: Resolve Studio (DCP encoding built in) and DCP-o-matic (free, open source). Every DCP is ingested and played on a cinema server before the campaign, because a DCP that fails on the chain's server fails in public. Regulatory: in India every advertisement exhibited in a cinema needs a Central Board of Film Certification certificate, because advertisements are films under the Cinematograph Act, 1952; in the UAE the exhibitor (VOX, Reel, Novo, Roxy) clears content under the National Media Authority's standards. The exhibitors to spec against: PVR INOX and Cinepolis in India; VOX Cinemas, Reel Cinemas and Novo Cinemas in the UAE.

### 12.9 Broadcast specifications

India: each channel group (JioStar, Zee, Sony, Sun and the regional networks) publishes its own delivery spec; the common denominator as of October 2026 is 1920 by 1080 at 25 fps, delivered interlaced (1080i50) by many channels and progressive (1080p25) by some, as MXF OP1a with XDCAM HD422 at 50 Mbps or as ProRes 422 HQ MOV, Rec.709 legal range, 48 kHz 24 bit stereo audio, with a 10 second slate (client, product, title, duration, agency, version, date, audio layout, loudness) and 2 seconds of black before and after. Loudness is measured to ITU-R BS.1770; the reason for the limit is that a spot louder than the programme around it draws complaints, and channel specs typically ask for -24 LKFS plus or minus 2 (the ATSC A/85 figure) or -23 LUFS (EBU R128); the number of any Indian standard on this could not be verified for this file, so the channel's spec governs. Content: the Advertising Code under the Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994 (rule 7), the ASCI Code, and category rules (surrogate advertising, disclaimers in the same language and at a legible size). The Bureau of Indian Standards publishes equipment and transmission standards; no BIS document that this file could verify sets an advertisement delivery format.

UAE: the broadcasters (MBC Group, Dubai Media Incorporated, Abu Dhabi Media) take 1080i50 or 1080p25 as MXF or ProRes, loudness to EBU R128 (-23 LUFS integrated, -1 dBTP), Arabic subtitles or dubbing where the spot runs on Arabic channels, and content clearance under the Media Regulation Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 55 of 2023) and the advertising standards now administered by the National Media Authority, which took over the UAE Media Council's functions in December 2025. The channel's current spec is confirmed on every job, because a rejected master costs a day.

Common to both: progressive masters are kept and interlacing is done at the encode, never shot; the interlaced file is checked for field order (upper field first for 1080i) on a broadcast monitor; graphics stay inside title safe (section 12.7).

### 12.10 DOOH screen specifications

A digital screen's resolution is its physical size divided by its pixel pitch (the distance between LED centres): a 30.5 m by 10 m facade at 10 mm pitch is about 3,050 by 1,000 pixels; a portrait 8.64 m by 13.66 m screen at 10 mm is about 864 by 1,366. Dubai, from operators' listings read in October 2026 (vendor figures, confirmed per site before delivery): the Sheikh Zayed Road digital facade listed at 30.5 m by 10 m, 12,000 nits and 10 mm pitch; a portrait Sheikh Zayed Road screen listed at 865 by 1366 pixels; LED unipoles at 16 mm pitch, sized for viewing at 50 to 300 m. Mumbai and Delhi: the operators (Times OOH, Laqshya Media, Bright Outdoor and others, across airports, metro networks and roadside sites) publish specs per site; typical outdoor pitches are 10 to 16 mm and indoor airport and mall screens 2.5 to 4 mm, but no Mumbai or Delhi site spec was verified for this file. Delivery rules that hold everywhere: render at the screen's native pixel resolution (never a 1080 file stretched); 8 bit 4:2:0 H.264 at 20 to 50 Mbps, or an image sequence where the operator asks; 25 or 30 fps per the operator; no audio; 10 second slots in a 60 to 120 second rotation, so the design lands in the first 2 seconds and reads at 50 m; large type (a cap height of at least one fifteenth of the screen's height for roadside); black grounds where the brand allows, because an LED emits nothing for black, which makes black the deepest, cheapest and most luxurious ground on an LED and white the costliest; colour checked on site at noon and at night, because 12,000 nits in sun and at midnight are two different pictures. Scope: a site visit before delivery and a photograph of the live creative after.

### 12.11 File naming and the delivery manifest

Name: CLIENT_Project_Asset_Ratio_Duration_Language_Version_Date_Codec.ext, for example CLIENT_WinterFilm_Hero_16x9_60s_EN_v07_20261028_ProRes422HQ.mov, with no spaces, no special characters, the date as YYYYMMDD so files sort, and the version carried from the edit (section 5.8). The manifest is a sheet delivered with every batch: filename, platform or placement, codec and container, resolution and ratio, frame rate, duration to the frame, loudness and true peak, captions (burned, sidecar filename, or none), language, colour tag, checksum (xxHash or MD5, so the client's download can be verified), retouching and generative disclosures (sections 10.1 and 10.9), music licence reference, approved version and approver, delivery date. Delivered by Frame.io or a cloud link with a 30 day expiry and the checksums in the message. The manifest is the document that answers every "which file is the right one" message for the life of the campaign.

### 13.2 The QC checklist

Quality control (QC) is the check of every delivered file against its specification and its approved version before it leaves the studio. It runs in two passes. Automated file QC tools (Telestream Vidchecker, Interra Systems' Baton) catch levels, gamut, black and frozen frames, loudness and codec conformity, and are what broadcasters run on ingest, so running one first means the channel's check finds nothing. The human pass catches everything that needs eyes and ears. A rejected broadcast master costs a day; a rejected DCP costs a screening; a social file with a typo in the price costs the client relationship.

| # | Check | How | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Slate and head | Play the first 15 s | Broadcast: 10 s slate (client, product, title, duration, agency, version, date, audio layout, loudness), then 2 s black. Social and web: no slate; the first frame is a composition |
| 2 | Black frames | Automated QC, then scrub | No black anywhere except the planned head, tail and fades |
| 3 | Flash frames | Jump edit to edit and step one frame either side of each cut | No flash frame (a single stray frame of a trimmed clip left at a cut) |
| 4 | Sync | Watch every sync line at full speed on the reference monitor | Lip sync within one frame (40 ms at 25 fps); EBU R 37 sets the broadcast tolerance at sound no more than 40 ms early and 60 ms late |
| 5 | Audio pops and clicks | Headphones at every audio edit | Every audio edit faded over 2 to 4 frames or cut on a zero crossing; no clicks, dropouts or clipped peaks |
| 6 | Loudness and true peak | Integrated measurement over the whole file with a BS.1770 meter (Fairlight's loudness meter, iZotope Insight, Nugen VisLM) | On the destination target (13.1) within the tolerance in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 13; true peak at or below -1 dBTP |
| 7 | Channels and phase | Check the layout and the mono fold-down | Left and right correct, no polarity inversion, mono compatible; 5.1 in SMPTE order (L, R, C, LFE, Ls, Rs) |
| 8 | Video levels and gamut | Waveform on the encoded file; automated gamut check | Legal range (64 to 940 in 10 bit, 16 to 235 in 8 bit) within the channel's tolerance; no illegal RGB flagged |
| 9 | Colour | Wipe the encode against the approved grade still; read the tags with MediaInfo | Matches the approved grade on the reference monitor; tags 1-1-1 for SDR (9-16-9 for PQ, 9-18-9 for HLG); no counter-gamma baked in (12.5) |
| 10 | Banding and compression artefacts | View skies, studio sweeps, gradients and fades to black at 100 percent | No visible banding, blocking or ringing around edges |
| 11 | Frame rate and cadence | Step through a pan; MediaInfo | Rate matches the manifest; constant frame rate; no doubled or dropped frames from a conversion |
| 12 | Aspect and framing | View the full raster in each ratio | Correct canvas; no unintended letterbox or pillarbox; subject and product whole in every ratio |
| 13 | Safe zones | Overlay each platform's safe-zone file | Every title, caption, logo and legal line inside the tightest zone of every placement (12.7) |
| 14 | Captions | Read every cue against the script; play the sidecar in a player | Spelling, names and numerals right; on at the word, off at the cut (11.4); house type; right language, script and direction |
| 15 | Supers and legal lines | Read every super against the approved copy deck | Names, prices (INR or AED as the client writes them) and legal lines exact; size and duration meet the channel and 11.6 |
| 16 | Codec and container | MediaInfo against the manifest | Codec, profile, level, bitrate, GOP, chroma, bit depth and container as specified; moov at the front of every MP4 |
| 17 | Duration | Timeline and MediaInfo | Exact to the frame: a 30 s spot at 25 fps is 750 frames of programme, with slate and black outside them |
| 18 | Disclosures | Read the manifest | Retouching and generative uses logged (10.1, 10.9); music licence reference present |
| 19 | Names and checksums | Compare names to 12.11; compute xxHash or MD5 | Every file named and listed; checksums match after upload |
| 20 | Device test | Private upload to each platform; play on an iPhone and an Android phone; play DCP and DOOH files on the target system | It plays, it looks like the grade, and it sits at the level of the content around it |

The QC report (pass or fail per line, operator, date, tool versions) goes into the manifest. A failed line returns the file to the stage that caused it: a level fault to the grade, a sync fault to the conform, a typo to the edit, never patched in the encode.

## 14. The cheap post tells

The taste layer of cheapness is in design-taste, section 3. This is the post-production layer: what each tell looks like, why it happens, and the fix.

|---|---|---|---|
| Over-sharpening | White halos on edges, crunchy skin, fabric that shimmers | In-camera sharpening plus a sharpen in post plus the platform's re-encode | No sharpening in post; detail comes from focus and resolution (10.8) |

## Terms introduced

- 180 degree rule: keep the camera on one side of the axis of action so screen direction holds across cuts.
- 3-2-1 rule: three copies of all media, on two kinds of storage, one of them off site.
- 30 degree rule: change camera angle by at least 30 degrees between two shots of one subject, or the cut reads as a jump.
- AAF (Advanced Authoring Format): the interchange file that carries an edit's audio clips, handles, levels and fades to Pro Tools.
- ACES (Academy Color Encoding System): the Academy's open colour management pipeline from camera to display.
- ACES2065-1: the linear AP0 archive and interchange encoding of ACES.
- ACEScc: pure logarithmic ACES grading space on AP1 primaries.
- ACEScct: logarithmic ACES grading space on AP1 primaries with a toe near black, the ACES grading default.
- ACEScg: linear ACES space on AP1 primaries for VFX and compositing.
- Action safe: the inner 93 percent of the frame in which all meaningful action is kept.
- AP0: the ACES primaries that enclose the whole spectral locus, two of them imaginary.
- AP1: the smaller ACES primaries used by ACEScg, ACEScc and ACEScct.
- Assembly: the stringout cut to structure at about 1.5 times the target length.
- Average shot length (ASL): running time divided by the number of shots.
- B-frame: a compressed frame predicted from both earlier and later frames.
- Banding: visible steps where a smooth gradient has too few code values.
- Bleach bypass: skipping the bleach stage in film processing, leaving silver for high contrast and low saturation.
- Bloom: the soft spread of light around highlights from lens and emulsion scatter.
- BT.1886: the ITU recommendation setting gamma 2.4 as the reference display curve for HD.
- CABAC: context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding, the more efficient of H.264's entropy coders.
- Change list: the numbered record of what changed between versions, with timecodes.
- Chroma subsampling: storing colour at lower resolution than brightness (4:4:4, 4:2:2, 4:2:0).
- CIE chromaticity diagram: the plot of all visible colours on which display gamuts appear as triangles.
- Closed GOP: a group of pictures in which no frame refers to a frame outside the group.
- Colour boost: a saturation control that raises low-saturation areas more than high ones.
- Colour tags: the primaries, transfer and matrix codes in a file's header (1-1-1 for BT.709).
- Conform: rebuilding the locked edit from camera originals in the finishing application.
- Continuity editing: the cutting system that hides the cut through screen direction, eyelines and matched action.
- CPL (composition playlist): the XML file in a DCP that orders its picture, sound and subtitle tracks.
- CRI (colour rendering index): a 0 to 100 measure of how faithfully a light renders colour against a reference source.
- Cross cutting: alternating between two lines of action in different places.
- CST (Color Space Transform): the Resolve node that converts between colour spaces and gammas with optional tone and gamut mapping.
- Cut on action: a cut placed within a movement so the movement carries the eye across it.
- DaVinci Intermediate (DI): Blackmagic's log curve paired with DaVinci Wide Gamut.
- DaVinci Wide Gamut (DWG): Blackmagic's working gamut, larger than any camera gamut.
- DCP (Digital Cinema Package): the folder of MXF and XML files a cinema server plays.
- Diegetic sound: sound that belongs to the world of the scene.
- Display referred: values that describe what a display should emit, bounded at its white and black.
- Dither: low-level noise added before quantising so gradient steps break up.
- DME stems: separate dialogue, music and effects mixes.
- Dolby Vision: Dolby's PQ-based HDR format with dynamic per-shot metadata.
- DOOH (digital out of home): advertising on networked screens in public places.
- EDL (edit decision list): the CMX3600 text list of cuts and dissolves, one video track per file.
- EOTF (electro-optical transfer function): the curve by which a display converts code values to light.
- Eye trace: where the viewer's eye is in the frame at the moment of a cut.
- Fine cut: every cut placed to the frame, music edited, titles in as cards.
- Fitzpatrick phototype: the I to VI classification of skin by its response to sun.
- Flash frame: a single stray frame of a trimmed clip left at a cut.
- GOP (group of pictures): the run of compressed frames from one keyframe to the next.
- Grain: the visible particle structure of film, or its emulation.
- Halation: the red-orange glow around bright highlights from light reflecting back through the film base.
- HDR sheen: the tone-mapped look of flat local contrast, edge halos and evenly lit surfaces.
- HDR10+: Samsung's PQ HDR format with dynamic metadata.
- HDR10: PQ HDR with static metadata.
- HLG (hybrid log-gamma): the BBC and NHK relative HDR curve with partial SDR compatibility.
- Hold: a shot left longer than its information requires.
- HUID (hallmark unique identification): the six-character alphanumeric code on Indian hallmarked gold.
- IMF (Interoperable Master Format): SMPTE ST 2067 component master with a playlist per version.
- Input transform (IDT): the conversion from a camera's log and gamut into the working space.
- Interop and SMPTE DCP: the older and the current packaging flavours of a Digital Cinema Package.
- Invisible cut: a join hidden inside a whip, an object crossing the lens or matched movement.
- J cut: the next shot's sound starts before its picture.
- Judder: uneven motion from duplicated or dropped frames, most visible on pans.
- Jump cut: a cut between two shots of the same angle with time removed.
- KDM (key delivery message): the per-screen, time-limited key file that unlocks an encrypted DCP.
- Key: a matte of the pixels selected by a qualifier.
- Kuleshov effect: the meaning an audience reads into a shot from the shot placed beside it.
- L cut: the previous shot's sound continues under the next shot's picture.
- Legal range: video code values of 16 to 235 (8 bit) or 64 to 940 (10 bit) for luma.
- Leq(m): the M-weighted equivalent loudness measure used for cinema trailers and advertisements.
- Level (H.264): the cap a profile places on resolution, frame rate and bitrate.
- Lift, gamma, gain: the three-way corrector for shadows, midtones and highlights.
- LMT (Look Modification Transform): a systematic look applied before the ACES output rendering.
- Log encoding: a logarithmic curve that fits a sensor's dynamic range into a 10 or 12 bit file.
- Log wheels: shadow, midtone and highlight wheels with bounded ranges for log grading.
- LUFS: loudness units relative to full scale, measured per ITU-R BS.1770.
- LUT (lookup table): a table mapping input values to output values, 1D per channel or 3D across colour.
- Match cut: a cut that rhymes shape, movement or sound across two shots.
- MCP (Model Context Protocol): the open standard through which an AI assistant operates an application.
- Mezzanine: a high-quality intermediate file from which other encodes are made.
- Montage (Eisenstein): metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal and intellectual relations between shots.
- Moov atom: the index of an MP4 file, placed at the front so playback starts before download ends.
- National Media Authority (NMA): the UAE federal media regulator that replaced the UAE Media Council in December 2025.
- ODT (Output Device Transform): the ACES mapping from the rendered image to a specific display.
- OETF (opto-electronic transfer function): the curve by which a camera encodes light as code values.
- Offset: a control that shifts every code value equally, the digital printer light.
- OMF (Open Media Framework): the older audio interchange format, 2 GB limit per file.
- One-light: a single base correction applied across all footage so it can be viewed and cut.
- Online: the finishing stage at full resolution with final effects, titles and grade.
- Open and closed captions: captions burned into picture, and captions carried in a sidecar the player draws.
- Optical flow: synthesising new frames from estimated motion for slow motion or rate changes.
- OTIO (OpenTimelineIO): the open timeline interchange format hosted by the Academy Software Foundation.
- Parade: three side-by-side waveforms for red, green and blue.
- Pattern interrupt: a deliberate break in rhythm that resets attention.
- Picture lock: the point after which picture timing does not change.
- Pivot: the value around which a contrast change is centred.
- Pixel pitch: the distance in millimetres between LED centres on a screen.
- PKL (packing list): the DCP file that lists every asset with its hash.
- Power window: a geometric mask that confines a correction.
- PQ (perceptual quantizer): the SMPTE ST 2084 absolute HDR curve to 10,000 nits.
- Print film emulation: reproducing the response of a projection print stock.
- Proxy: a lightweight copy of camera media for editing, relinked to the original at conform.
- QC (quality control): the check of every delivered file against its specification and approved version.
- Qualifier: a selection of pixels by hue, saturation and luminance.
- Rec.709: ITU-R BT.709, the HD primaries, white point and camera curve.
- Relink: matching each proxy to its original by file name, reel name and timecode.
- Rough cut: structure right, length within 20 percent, temp music, no grade.
- RRT (Reference Rendering Transform): the ACES scene-to-display rendering shared by every output.
- Safe zone: the part of a vertical frame that a platform's interface does not cover.
- Scene referred: values that describe light in the scene, unbounded above white.
- Secondary correction: a correction confined by a key, a window or both.
- Selects: the 4 and 5 star clips pulled into a timeline.
- Skin tone line: the hue angle on the vectorscope along which healthy skin of every complexion falls.
- Smash cut: an abrupt cut between opposite states, quiet to loud or slow to fast.
- Speed ramp: a change of playback speed within a shot.
- Split screen: two or more frames shown at once.
- Split toning: tinting shadows and highlights with different hues.
- SRT (SubRip): a plain-text caption file of numbered, timed cues.
- Stringout: every select laid end to end in script or scene order.
- TASA (Trailer Audio Standards Association): the body that set 85 dB Leq(m) as the trailer loudness level.
- Teal and orange: the complementary grade of cyan shadows and orange skin.
- Text-based editing: cutting picture by editing its transcript.
- Textless: a master clean of every title and super, the base for localisation.
- Title safe: the inner 90 percent of the frame inside which text is kept.
- Tone mapping: compressing scene highlight range into a display's range.
- True peak (dBTP): the inter-sample peak level after oversampling.
- Turnover: hand-off of the locked edit to sound and colour.
- Vectorscope: the scope plotting chroma as hue angle and saturation radius.
- Waveform: the scope plotting brightness per column of pixels.
- WebVTT: the W3C caption format with positioning and styling.
- Wipe: a transition in which a line or shape pushes one shot off screen.
- Working space: the colour space and gamma in which the grade is performed.
- X'Y'Z': the gamma 2.6 encoded CIE XYZ colour of DCP picture.
