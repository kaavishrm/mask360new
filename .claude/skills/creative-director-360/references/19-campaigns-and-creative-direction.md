## 1. The role

### 1.1 What a creative director decides

A creative director (CD) is the person accountable for the idea and the standard of everything the agency makes for a client. Five decisions belong to the CD and to nobody else:

1. The idea. Which of the territories becomes the campaign, and the one sentence it is reduced to (section 4.6).
2. The standard. What "finished" means on this job: the reference level the craft must reach before the work leaves the building. The CD sets it by naming references (design-taste, section 4, step 1) and by refusing work that falls short.
3. The taste. Every aesthetic call that the brief does not settle: the typeface, the crop, the pace of the cut, the amount of silence. Taste is the trained prediction described in design-taste; this file is the craft beneath it.
4. The team. Who is on the job, which pair gets the hero film, which freelancer gets the retouching, which director gets the call.
5. The client relationship at the level of the work. The account lead owns the commercial relationship; the CD owns the client's trust in the creative judgement. When a client stops asking for three options, the CD has done the job.

Why these five and no more: anything the CD decides that is not one of these (the budget line for catering, the media plan, the tracking study) dilutes the attention that the idea needs. Anything on this list that the CD delegates (the standard, most commonly) decays within two rounds.

### 1.2 Titles and what they mean

| Title | Decides | Does not decide | Typical span |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chief creative officer (CCO) | Creative reputation of the whole agency or network, hiring of CDs, which pitches to take, the agency's own brand | Individual executions on most accounts | 50 to 500 creatives |
| Executive creative director (ECD) | Standard across a group of accounts, kills work before the CCO sees it, the senior client relationship | Day-to-day art direction | 3 to 8 account groups |
| Creative director (CD) | The idea and the standard on named accounts, the team, the production partners | Media, commercial terms | 2 to 6 accounts, 4 to 15 creatives |
| Associate creative director (ACD) | Runs a team of pairs on one or two accounts, first reviewer of work, often still makes work | Which idea ships (recommends, CD decides) | 1 to 2 accounts |
| Art director (AD) | The visual half of the pair: layout, image, type, casting, the look of the film | The verbal idea alone | A pair |
| Copywriter | The verbal half: headline, script, voice, naming, long copy | The visual alone | A pair |
| Designer | Form: identity, systems, layout, type, motion craft; in design-led shops the designer is the AD | The campaign idea unless they are also the AD | Project based |
| Strategist (planner) | The brief, the insight, the proposition, the research (see 08), the measurement frame | The idea (though the best planners propose territories) | 3 to 6 accounts |
| Producer | Feasibility, money, time, vendors, rights, the production bible | The idea or the aesthetic standard | Per job |

The art and copy pair is the unit of advertising creativity since Bill Bernbach put Helmut Krone and Julian Koenig together at Doyle Dane Bernbach in 1959 for Volkswagen. The reason the pair works: an image and a line that each need the other to make sense is the structural form of an advertising idea, and two people arguing produce that faster than one person alone.

### 1.3 Kinds of creative director

| Kind | Home discipline | Judges work by | Blind spot to correct |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising CD | Film, print, campaign ideas | Does it have an idea, is it famous-able | Systems, product, the identity underneath |
| Design CD | Identity, systems, typography, print, packaging | Is the system coherent, is the craft exact | The campaign idea, the joke, the human moment |
| Digital CD | Web, product, interaction, motion, social | Does it work, does it perform, does it move | Film grammar, print craft, the long copy line |
| Brand CD | Positioning, codes, tone, long-term consistency | Does it come from the brand, will it last | Speed, topicality, the stunt |
| 360 CD | All of the above | All of the above, at once | Depth in each; the fix is a bench of specialists and a reference habit |

The 360 creative director commands every craft well enough to brief, judge and correct the specialist in each, and does at least one craft at the specialist's level. The reason the role exists for Mask360: a luxury hotel in Jaipur, a jewellery house in Mumbai and a developer in Dubai each want one person who can hold the identity, the film, the website, the Instagram grid and the launch event to one standard. Separate CDs per channel produce matching luggage (section 4.3) at best and five different brands at worst.

### 1.4 A week in the life

A working CD's week at a 12 to 25 person studio, based on the rhythm used at independent shops such as Uncommon Creative Studio (D&AD Advertising Agency of the Year 2026) and Mother, adapted to Mask360's size:

| Day | Morning | Afternoon |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Work-in-progress review of every live job, 20 minutes each, standing | Writing or rewriting one brief; client call on the week's approval |
| Tuesday | Crit with the pairs on concept work (section 10.2) | Reference time: one hour reading or looking, logged to the reference board |
| Wednesday | Pre-production meeting (PPM) or shoot | Edit review or craft review |
| Thursday | Client presentation | Team one-to-ones, portfolio reviews |
| Friday | Kill or ship decisions on everything presented that week | Studio show-and-tell, 30 minutes; awards entries or case film work in season |

The rule inside the week: the CD looks at every piece of work at least twice before the client does (once at concept, once at craft) and never sees it for the first time in the client's room.

### 1.5 Decision rights, delegation and the undelegatable

| Decision | CD | ACD or AD | Strategist | Producer | Account lead | Client |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The proposition in the brief | Consulted | Informed | Decides | Informed | Consulted | Approves |
| Which territory becomes the idea | Decides | Recommends | Consulted | Informed | Informed | Approves the idea |
| Casting, location, director | Decides | Recommends | Informed | Options and cost | Informed | Approves |
| Typeface, palette, grid of the system | Decides | Executes | Informed | Informed | Informed | Informed |
| The edit (which take, the cut, the music) | Decides | Recommends | Informed | Schedule | Informed | Approves final |
| Scope, rounds, money | Consulted | Informed | Informed | Recommends | Decides | Agrees |
| Shipping | Signs off | Prepares | Informed | Delivers | Confirms | Approves |

Delegate: first drafts, layout exploration, retouching, versioning, motion polish, vendor search, research synthesis, the second and third rounds of copy.

Never delegate: the single idea sentence, the reference board, the final kill, the edit's last pass, the standards document (section 13.3), the first conversation with a new client about taste, and the apology when something ships wrong.

## 2. The creative brief

### 2.1 The one page

The brief is a one-page contract between strategy and creative. Its fields, in the order they are read:

| Field | What it must contain | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Background | The business situation in three sentences, with the number that matters (share, occupancy, average order value, launch date) | A stranger could explain why this job exists |
| Objective | One behavioural or perceptual change, with a measure | Starts with a verb; has a number and a date |
| Target audience with a portrait | A named person, their day, their money, what they already buy, what they would say about the category (see 08 for the research behind it) | The creative can picture them at breakfast |
| Insight | The tension or truth that opens the door (section 3) | It explains behaviour; it is not a fact restated |
| Single-minded proposition (SMP) | The one thing the work must leave in the audience's head, in one sentence of under 12 words | The team can say it without reading it |
| Reasons to believe (RTB) | Two or three proofs: product truth, provenance, number, name | Each is specific and checkable |
| Tone | Three adjectives at most, with a reference for each ("the composure of a Hermès window") | No adjective is "premium" or "luxurious" |
| Mandatories | Logo, legal lines, languages, formats, the thing the founder insists on | Listed, so they stop being surprises |
| Deliverables | Every asset with size, duration, language, quantity | Matches the estimate |
| Budget | Production budget and media budget, separately | A real number, with currency (INR, AED, USD) |
| Timing | Concept presentation, approval, production window, launch | Working days counted, holidays named (Diwali, Eid, the Dubai summer) |
| Success measures | The metric that will be reported, and the baseline | Agreed with the client before creative starts |

Why one page: a brief that runs to four pages has not finished the thinking. The discipline forces the strategist to choose, and the creative team to trust the choice. The famous briefs in 2.4 are all short.

### 2.2 How to write one

1. Start with the objective and the measure. Everything else serves them.
2. Write the portrait from a real interview, not a persona deck. One sentence of the person's own words beats a demographic.
3. Find the insight last, after the research (section 3.4). Writing it first produces a slogan dressed as an insight.
4. Draft the SMP ten ways. Pick the one that a creative could draw.
5. Read it aloud to someone outside the account. If they ask "so what do you want people to do", the objective is weak.
6. Attach the reference board (design-taste, section 4, step 1), the trash list of category tropes (design-taste, section 4, step 3) and the research summary (08). The brief is one page; its appendix can be twenty.

### 2.3 How to read a bad one, and the brief back

The tells of a bad brief: an objective that is a list; an audience of "affluent 25 to 45"; an insight that is a product fact; an SMP with "and" in it; a tone of "premium yet approachable"; no number anywhere; deliverables that say "social assets"; timing that starts yesterday.

The brief back is the creative team's written reply, under 200 words, restating the brief in their own words within 24 hours of receiving it. It states: what we understood the task to be, what we think the real problem is, what we are going to ignore, and what we need before we start. The reason it exists: the gap between what the strategist wrote and what the creative heard is where three wasted weeks live, and the brief back surfaces that gap for the price of an hour.

### 2.4 Famous briefs and what made them

| Brief | Agency, year | What the brief gave the creatives | Why it worked |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Economist | Abbott Mead Vickers BBDO, from 1988 | A reader who is successful because of what they know, and the fear of being caught out among such people | It briefed the reader's self-image and a status anxiety, which let David Abbott write "I never read The Economist. Management trainee. Aged 42." The white-out-of-red format became the idea |
| Guinness "Good things come to those who wait" | AMV BBDO, 1996 onward | A product truth: the pour takes 119.5 seconds and the drinker waits | The wait was reframed from a flaw to the ritual, giving "Surfer" (1999) its subject |
| Dove Campaign for Real Beauty | Ogilvy, 2004 | A research finding from the 2004 "The Real Truth About Beauty" study (3,200 women in 10 countries): about 2 percent of women described themselves as beautiful | The brief carried a cultural tension rather than a product claim, and the campaign could run for two decades |
| Nike "Just Do It" | Wieden+Kennedy, 1988 | Unify a set of disparate spots under one attitude, for everyone who moves, with the athlete as the standard | Dan Wieden's line gave the attitude a verb, and the first spot used an 80-year-old runner, Walt Stack, to prove "everyone" |

### 2.5 Three Mask360 briefs compared

| Field | Luxury hotel brand film (Rajasthan palace hotel) | Jewellery launch (bridal high jewellery, Mumbai) | Identity (new members' club, Dubai) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objective | Lift direct booking enquiries from the US and UK by a stated percent in the season after launch | Sell a numbered collection in the wedding window (October to February) at full price | Fill the founding membership at the stated fee before opening |
| Audience portrait | A couple in their 50s who have done Aman and want a place with a story; they read the FT Weekend, they book through a travel advisor | A bride's mother who buys the big piece; the bride chooses the design, the mother approves the maker | A founder in their 30s to 40s who already belongs to two clubs and wants the one that is harder to join |
| Insight | They are tired of resorts that could be anywhere; they want to be the guest of a house | The piece is bought once and worn at three weddings; the first wearing is the only one anyone photographs | Belonging is proven by who is kept out |
| SMP | A palace that is still a home | The piece you will be remembered in | The room you cannot buy your way into |
| RTB | The family still lives in one wing; 1700s frescoes; the chef's grandmother's recipes | Hand-set by named karigars; a stated carat weight; a numbered run of 25 | A founding committee of named people; a capped membership number; a building by a named architect |
| Tone | Patient, quiet, specific | Reverent, precise, unhurried | Dry, assured, few words |
| Deliverables | One 90 second film, three 15 second cuts, 20 stills, a 2 minute advisor edit | Campaign film 60 seconds, 12 product stills, 6 lookbook spreads, store window, invitation | Wordmark, monogram, membership card, stationery, signage, website, launch film, uniforms brief |
| Timing | Shoot in the cool season (November to February); 10 weeks brief to delivery | Shoot by August for an October launch; 12 weeks | 14 to 18 weeks for identity, launch film in parallel from week 10 |

The three differ in the thing the audience needs to feel: trust (hotel), permanence (jewellery), exclusion (club). The brief must name that feeling, because the film, the still and the wordmark each take their form from it.

## 3. Insight

### 3.1 Definition

An insight is a tension or a truth that explains behaviour and opens a creative door. It is held by the audience already; the work recognises it rather than announcing it. Jon Steel's definition in "Truth, Lies and Advertising" (1998): the thing that makes the consumer say "that is so true" and the creative say "I know what to do with that". Both halves are required.

### 3.2 Types

| Type | Source | Example | Door it opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human | A universal feeling | Being told "wait" is maddening until the wait is the point (Guinness) | The product's slowest attribute becomes the hero |
| Cultural | A shift in what a society argues about | Women measure themselves against images they know are false (Dove, 2004) | The brand takes a side |
| Category | What everyone in the category does, and resents | Every hotel film has a drone shot of a pool at golden hour | Refuse the trope by name |
| Product | A fact about the thing that nobody has used | Heinz ketchup is drawn the same way by everyone (Rethink, 2021) | The proof is the idea |
| Brand | Something the brand did, made or stood for in its past | Cadbury is the sweet eaten before an auspicious start (Shubh Aarambh, 2010) | A ritual the brand already owns |

### 3.3 Insight, observation, fact

A fact: Indian weddings run three to five days. An observation: the bride's jewellery is photographed most on the first day. An insight: the piece is bought for a lifetime and judged in one photograph. Only the third tells the creative what to make (a campaign built on the one photograph). The test is the second half of Steel's definition: a fact and an observation leave the creative with nothing to do.

### 3.4 How to find one

1. Research first (08): interviews, observation, the client's own sales data, the search and review language of the category.
2. Cultural observation: what the audience is actually arguing about this year (design-taste, references/currency-2026.md for the aesthetic layer; the strategist supplies the social one).
3. The obvious thing nobody said. Write down the ten most banal truths about the category and the product. One of them, said plainly for the first time, is usually the insight. Volkswagen's small car was small; Avis was second (DDB, 1962, "We try harder").
4. The complaint. What do buyers apologise for or joke about? (Oatly's buyers were embarrassed to order oat milk; the brand made the embarrassment the voice.)
5. The ritual. What do people already do with the product that the brand has never shown?

### 3.5 Testing an insight

- Say it to three members of the audience. Two of three should nod before the sentence ends.
- Hand it to a creative pair for 30 minutes. If they come back with three executions, the door is open. If they come back with questions, it is an observation.
- Check it against the competitor set. If a competitor could run it, it is a category insight, which is fine for a challenger and weak for a leader.
- Check it against the product. If the product cannot prove it, it is a wish.

## 4. The idea

### 4.1 Concept and execution

A concept is the organising thought that a stranger could describe without seeing the work: "Guinness is worth the wait". An execution is one expression of it: a surfer waiting for the wave, horses in the foam. The CD's first question in any review is which of the two is on the wall. A room full of executions with no concept is the commonest failure in a concept review; a concept with one execution is a Tuesday.

### 4.2 The campaignable idea and the platform

A campaignable idea produces its second and tenth execution as easily as its first. A platform idea is a campaignable idea that can run for years and absorb product news, seasons and cultures without changing its sentence: "Just Do It" (1988 to now), "Good things come to those who wait" (1996 to 2010s), "Fly Better" (Emirates, November 2018 to now), "Utterly Butterly" (Amul, 1966 to now). A one-off is a single execution with no second: a stunt, a topical, a launch film. One-offs are legitimate (the Burj Khalifa flight attendant for Emirates in August 2021 was a one-off inside a platform). The error is to sell a one-off as a platform, or to let a platform rot into a slogan that nobody executes.

### 4.3 Matching luggage and matching idea

Matching luggage: the same key visual, headline and colour applied to every channel, so the work looks integrated. Matching idea: each channel does what it is best at in service of one thought, so the work is integrated. The first is the 2000s definition of integration and it reads as wallpaper. The second is the standard: the film carries the emotion, the print carries the proof, the social carries the participation, the site carries the transaction, the space carries the gesture (section 6.9). Dumb Ways to Die (McCann Melbourne, 2012) is the textbook: a song, a game, a book, posters and a pledge site each doing a different job for one idea.

### 4.4 The idea test

Four questions, all must pass:

1. Can it be said in a sentence? If the sentence needs "and", there are two ideas or none.
2. Does it scale across channels? Write the social version and the spatial version before the client sees the film.
3. Does it come from the brand? Cover the logo (design-taste, section 4, step 7). If a competitor could run it, it is a category idea.
4. Is it new? Search it (Ads of the World, the D&AD archive, Lürzer's Archive, the Cannes Lions archive). Being first is checkable and the client will check.

### 4.5 The big idea and the small idea done perfectly

The big idea tradition (Bernbach, Ogilvy, Lois) holds that one large thought, famous and repeated, beats a hundred small ones. Byron Sharp's "How Brands Grow" (2010) and Les Binet and Peter Field's "The Long and the Short of It" (2013) supply the evidence that fame and consistent distinctive assets drive long-term growth, with Binet and Field's recommended split of about 60 percent brand building to 40 percent activation.

Luxury runs a second tradition: the small idea done perfectly. A Hermès window, an Aesop store, a Bottega Veneta lookbook, a Jacquemus product film each carry a modest thought (a horse, a shelf of books, a weave, a bag at the wrong scale) executed to a standard that makes it unmistakable. Kapferer and Bastien (section 8.1) explain why: luxury does not need persuasion, it needs dream maintenance, and dream maintenance is craft. The house position: for Mask360's luxury clients, the small idea done perfectly is the default; the big idea is reserved for a launch, a repositioning or a challenger in a premium category (a new hotel brand against Taj and Oberoi, a jewellery house against Tanishq).

### 4.6 Three sources of ideas

| Source | Mechanism | Examples | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product truth | Find the one fact about the thing and make the work out of it | Matthieu Blazy's Bottega Veneta (2021 to 2025): the leather made to look like denim, flannel, paper; Apple's "Shot on iPhone" (2015): the pictures are the proof | Needs a product that is actually true |
| Culture | Take a side in a live argument, or borrow a cultural object the audience loves | Nike "Dream Crazy" (2018); Loewe x Studio Ghibli (2021 to 2023); Cadbury's cricket girl (1994) in a cricket-mad country | Borrowing without permission or fluency (section 16.2) |
| Form | A formal gesture (scale, repetition, colour, material) is the idea | Jacquemus bags the size of buses (2023); Bottega's green; the Economist's red; Tiffany's blue box | Form with no thought under it becomes decoration within a year |

The single idea sentence (design-taste, section 4, step 2) is written before any execution and travels on every slide, call sheet and edit note. Example for a hotel: "The guest of a house, not a resort" fails the house copy rule on contrast constructions; rewrite it positively: "A palace that is still a home".

## 5. Ideation methods

### 5.1 Young's technique

James Webb Young, "A Technique for Producing Ideas" (lectures 1939, published 1940): an idea is a new combination of old elements, and the ability to see relationships is the whole skill. Five steps: gather raw material (specific to the product and general to life); work it over (turn every fact, write every partial thought on a card); incubate (stop, do something else); the idea arrives; shape it to the real world (show it, let it be criticised). The reason the method survives: it forces the gathering stage that impatient teams skip, and it names incubation as work. Mask360 use: the reference board and the research summary are the raw material; a 48-hour gap between briefing and the first crit is the incubation.

### 5.2 Lateral thinking

Edward de Bono, "Lateral Thinking" (1970), and "Six Thinking Hats" (1985). The useful tools: random entry (bring in an unrelated word or object and force a connection), provocation (state something absurd, "the hotel has no rooms", and move from it), reversal (the opposite of the category convention), and the hats as a way to separate critique (black hat) from generation (green hat) in a crit so that the room does not kill ideas while making them.

### 5.3 The pair, the 100 bad ideas and the territory

- The pair. Art and copy in one room, 90-minute sessions, no laptops for the first hour. The AD draws, the writer talks, the roles swap when stuck.
- The 100 bad ideas. Each pair writes 100 ideas in a day, numbered, most of them bad. The rule exists because the first 20 are the category's clichés, the next 30 are the agency's clichés, and the idea is somewhere after 50. Dave Trott's "Creative Mischief" (2009) and Paul Arden's "It's Not How Good You Are, It's How Good You Want to Be" (2003) are the short books that make the case for volume before judgement.
- Territories. Group the 100 into 3 to 5 territories: a territory is a direction with a feeling, a reference and a sample execution, short of a finished idea. A tone map plots territories on two axes the brief cares about (for a hotel: intimate to grand, quiet to witty) so the CD and client can see where the brand sits and where the competitors crowd.
- "What we are not doing". The trash list from design-taste, section 4, step 3, written for ideas as well as aesthetics: the category tropes refused by name.

### 5.4 Constraint, reference and the two tests

- Constraint as fuel. Give the pair a real limit (no people in the film, one colour, one location, a budget of 8 lakh rupees, a five-second format) and the ideas improve. Duolingo's five-second Super Bowl spot (2024) and Jacquemus' computer-generated bags (2023, no shoot) are constraint ideas.
- The reference board method. Three to five references per territory, each with the thing borrowed named (design-taste, section 4, step 1). Boards without the "what is borrowed" line are mood boards and are banned.
- The single idea sentence. Written on the wall before executions, rewritten when the executions teach something.
- The no idea test. Remove the headline and the logo. If the picture still means the same thing as every competitor's picture, there is no idea. Then remove the picture and keep the line. If the line could sit under any product in the category, there is no idea.

## 6. The idea in each channel

### 6.1 Film

- The 30-second structure. Three beats: set-up (seconds 0 to 8, the world and the tension), turn (seconds 8 to 22, the idea), resolution (seconds 22 to 30, product and line). The brand should be identifiable by second 3 on platforms where skipping begins at second 5 (YouTube skippable in-stream) and the first frame must work with the sound off for feeds. Orlando Wood's "Lemon" (IPA, 2019) gives the long-term effectiveness evidence for right-brain features: characters, dialogue, a sense of place, humour, music with melody.
- The long-form brand film (60 to 180 seconds). Cartier's "L'Odyssée de Cartier" (2012, directed by Bruno Aveillan, 3 minutes 30 seconds) is the reference for a house telling its history as spectacle; Google "Reunion" (Ogilvy India, 2013, 3 minutes 32 seconds, directed by Amit Sharma) is the reference for a product demonstrated through a story. Long form earns its length by one beat the viewer could not predict at minute one.
- The fashion film with no story. The house codes, a body, a place, a pace. Loewe's and Bottega Veneta's product films under Jonathan Anderson and Matthieu Blazy carried no plot; the idea was the object seen in a way that only that house would show it. The CD's test: freeze any frame and ask whether it is a photograph the house would print.
- Music first. For luxury film, the track is chosen or composed before the shot list, because pace in the edit is set by the music and the shoot must cover that pace. Guinness "Surfer" was cut to Leftfield's "Phat Planet" (1999). License before the shoot (section 14.4).

### 6.2 Print and out-of-home

- Headline and visual. The Bernbach form: the two halves complete each other and neither works alone. "Think Small" (1959) with a tiny car in white space; the Economist's white line on red with no visual at all.
- The visual metaphor. One object stands for the claim: the Heinz drawing (2021), the mouldy Whopper (2020), the Fevicol-strong bus (2001). The test is whether the metaphor survives without the headline.
- The one-liner. A line that is the whole ad. The Economist ran for 30 years on it. The Indian masters are Amul's topicals (daCunha Communications, weekly since 1966), where the pun is the idea and the Amul girl is the constant.
- Out-of-home reads in 3 seconds at 60 kilometres an hour on the Sheikh Zayed Road and in 1 second on a Mumbai hoarding at a signal. Seven words or fewer; the house type set at a size that reads at 50 metres; one image.

### 6.3 Social

- The drop. One asset, timed, scarce, announced and gone: the fashion model (Supreme on Thursdays, Jacquemus launch posts).
- The series. A repeatable format with a constant and a variable: Amul's topicals, Zomato's and Swiggy's push notifications and festival posts, Aesop's quotations. The format is the idea; each post is an execution.
- The format. Native to the platform's grammar: a 9:16 vertical for Reels and TikTok, sound on, text on screen, the first 1.5 seconds doing the job of a headline. A luxury house on social keeps the pace of the house (Hermès, Bottega) and refuses the platform's pace; a challenger (Duolingo, Liquid Death) adopts the platform's pace as the idea.

### 6.4 Digital

- The microsite as the campaign. Dumb Ways to Die's pledge site, Spotify Wrapped (2016 onward) as a personalised page that people post. The site is the idea when the user's own action produces the content.
- The interactive idea. A mechanism the user operates: Heinz asked people to draw ketchup; Wrapped asked Spotify to compute a year. The CD's test is whether the mechanism would be interesting if the brand's name were removed.

### 6.5 Experiential

One spatial gesture, built to a standard, photographed as the real deliverable. Jacquemus' pop-ups and the giant Le Bambino installations; Aesop's windows; a palace hotel's single long dinner table in the courtyard. The reason for one gesture: a space with five ideas photographs as a trade fair.

### 6.6 PR and earned

The stunt that is on brand: Emirates' flight attendant on the pinnacle of the Burj Khalifa (August 2021, a real person, Nicole Smith-Ludvik, with a real safety rig), Duolingo's "death" of Duo (February 2025), Liquid Death's product launches sold as horror. The test is whether the stunt is something the brand would have done with no camera present. A stunt that any brand could do is media spend in disguise.

### 6.7 Retail and packaging

Retail: the store as the brand's most complete expression; Aesop commissions a different architect for each store so that no two are alike, which is itself a campaign of several hundred executions. Packaging as campaign: Tiffany's blue box, the Hermès orange box, Bottega's green, the Amul butter pack, the Tanishq red. The pack is the medium that reaches every buyer and survives every campaign; for a jewellery client the box and the certificate are briefs of their own.

### 6.8 The integrated architecture

| Model | Structure | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Hero, hub, help (Google, 2014, also written "hero, hub, hygiene") | Hero: the one big piece, few times a year. Hub: the regular series that keeps the audience. Help: the always-on content that answers searches | Content planning for a brand's owned channels |
| Teaser, launch, sustain | Teaser: scarcity and curiosity, 1 to 3 weeks. Launch: the hero and the full channel set in one week. Sustain: 6 to 12 weeks of executions, proof, participation | Campaign sequencing for a product or property launch |
| Channel roles | Film: emotion and fame. Print and OOH: proof and presence. Social: participation and pace. Site: transaction and depth. Space: the gesture. PR: the story about the story | The one-page channel plan that goes with every idea |

Sequencing rule for a Mask360 launch: the teaser is a drop (6.3), the launch is the film and the space in the same 72 hours, the sustain is the series. The sustain is planned before the launch is approved, because the launch without a sustain is a one-off (4.2).

## 7. Headline and copy craft for creative directors

### 7.1 Structures

| Structure | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The twist | A familiar phrase with one word changed or one expectation reversed | "Think Small" (VW, 1959) |
| The proof | A fact that does the persuading | "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" (Ogilvy, 1958) |
| The challenge | The reader is dared | "Just Do It" (Nike, 1988) |
| The confession | The brand admits something | "Lemon" (VW, 1960); "We try harder" (Avis, 1962) |
| The instruction | An imperative that is also the attitude | "Fly Better" (Emirates, 2018); "Murder your thirst" (Liquid Death) |
| The list | Three things, the third turns | Most Economist posters are one line; the list form belongs to long copy |
| The wordplay used once | A pun carrying a real meaning, used once per campaign | Amul's topicals use it weekly, which only works because the pun is the format |

### 7.2 Bernbach and Abbott

Bill Bernbach's lessons, as recorded in his agency's work and Bob Levenson's "Bill Bernbach's Book" (1987): say the truth with wit; the execution is the idea; rules are what the artist breaks. David Abbott's lessons, from his AMV BBDO work (Sainsbury's, Volvo, The Economist, Yellow Pages): write to one person; keep the sentences short enough to be read on a bus; put the product in the line; the long copy must be as well written as a novel's first page; read everything aloud.

### 7.3 Rules for judging copy

- The no adjective rule. Strike every adjective, then put back only those that carry a fact ("hand-set", "numbered", "1700s"). "Luxurious", "exquisite", "bespoke", "curated" are banned in house copy.
- One line, one job. A headline that explains and sells and jokes does none.
- Specific beats general: a number, a name, a place.
- The line a confident person would say aloud (design-taste, section 5, copy).
- Say it in the audience's language, including the Hinglish or Arabic-English register of the market, and have a native speaker read it (section 16.2).
- The house system of copy rules (the 15 rules, the sentence case, the banned words, the no-contrast rule) lives in the mask360-copywriting skill; this section is the judging layer above it, and the house rules win on any conflict.

## 8. Luxury creative strategy

### 8.1 Kapferer and Bastien's anti-laws

Jean-Noël Kapferer and Vincent Bastien, "The Luxury Strategy" (2009; second edition 2012), set out 24 anti-laws of marketing: rules that reverse conventional marketing because luxury sells a dream and a distance, and conventional marketing closes distance. The full list, in their wording:

1. Forget about positioning: luxury is not comparative. 2. Does your product have enough flaws to give it soul? 3. Don't pander to your customers' wishes. 4. Keep non-enthusiasts out. 5. Don't respond to rising demand. 6. Dominate the client. 7. Make it difficult for clients to buy. 8. Protect clients from non-clients, the big from the small. 9. The role of advertising is not to sell. 10. Communicate to those whom you are not targeting. 11. The presumed price should always seem higher than the actual price. 12. Luxury sets the price; price does not set luxury. 13. Raise your prices as time goes on, in order to increase demand. 14. Keep raising the average price of the product range. 15. Do not sell. 16. Keep stars out of your advertising. 17. Cultivate closeness to the arts for initiates. 18. Do not relocate your factories. 19. Do not hire consultants. 20. Do not test. 21. Do not look for consensus. 22. Do not look after group synergies. 23. Do not look for cost reduction. 24. Do not sell openly on the Internet.

The ones a CD acts on daily: 9 and 10 (the film is for everyone, the purchase is for the few, so the work must be seen widely and feel exclusive), 16 (the ambassador logic in 8.5), 20 and 21 (no focus groups on the film, no consensus in the client's committee), 2 (the flaw with soul: the hand-stitch that is not perfect, the grain in the image). Marketing Week and others have recorded that many luxury practitioners reject the framework; the house position is that the anti-laws describe the top of the pyramid (Hermès, Patek Philippe, Aman) accurately and premium brands (a five-star chain, a bridge jewellery brand) must break several of them, chiefly 5, 7 and 24.

### 8.2 Restraint and codes

Restraint is the craft expression of anti-law 15. Codes are the brand's owned, repeated signs (Byron Sharp's distinctive brand assets, in luxury terms): Hermès orange and the horse, Tiffany's blue, Bottega's green and the intrecciato weave, Chanel's tweed and camellia, Cartier's panther, Burberry's check, the Taj dome, the Tanishq red. The CD's job across a campaign is to spend the codes with discipline: at least one code in every execution, never all of them at once, and new codes introduced only at a launch and then repeated for years.

| House | Reference films | What to learn |
|---|---|---|

### 8.4 The lookbook as idea

A lookbook is a sequence of images of a collection, in order. At the top houses it is the primary creative product: Bottega Veneta's under Blazy, The Row's, Lemaire's. The idea lives in the sequence (what is adjacent to what), the one treatment (one light, one lens, one distance) and the ending. For a jewellery or fashion client at Mask360, the lookbook is briefed as a film with no motion: a shot list with a reason for each shot and an order.

### 8.5 Celebrity, ambassador and collaboration logic

- Ambassador logic. Anti-law 16 is broken by most houses, within a rule: the ambassador is cast as a long-running character (Theron for Dior since 2004) or as a reader of the house's codes (Deepika Padukone as a Louis Vuitton house ambassador from 2022; Alia Bhatt as a Gucci global ambassador from 2023), never as a spokesperson reading claims. The test for a Mask360 jewellery client: would the house photograph this person without the product? If yes, the person's fame is doing the work and the house is renting it.
- Collaboration logic. Loewe x Studio Ghibli (Totoro 2021, Spirited Away 2022, Howl's Moving Castle 2023) borrowed a loved world and translated it into the house's craft. Tiffany x Nike (Air Force 1 1837, March 2023, US$400) put a house code on another brand's object and drew mixed reactions because the object did not change. Rule: the collaboration must produce an object neither party could make alone.
- The drop model. Scarcity by time and number: a numbered run, a date, a queue. For Indian and Gulf clients, the drop maps onto the festival calendar (Diwali, Akshaya Tritiya for gold, Eid) and the wedding season.
- Scarcity and silence. The most expensive houses advertise least per unit of revenue and post least. A luxury client who asks for daily posts is asking to look like a premium brand.
- When wit is allowed. Wit in luxury is allowed on scale (Jacquemus), on the object (Loewe's tomato heel, the Hermès animal), on culture (Loewe's spelling bee) and on the house itself. It is never on the customer, the price or the craft.
