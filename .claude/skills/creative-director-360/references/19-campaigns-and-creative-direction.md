## 1. The role

### 1.1 What a creative director decides

A creative director (CD) is the person accountable for the idea and the standard of everything the agency makes for a client. Five decisions belong to the CD and to nobody else:

1. The idea. Which of the territories becomes the campaign, and the one sentence it is reduced to (section 4.6).
2. The standard. What "finished" means on this job: the reference level the craft must reach before the work leaves the building. The CD sets it by naming references (design-taste, section 4, step 1) and by refusing work that falls short.
3. The taste. Every aesthetic call that the brief does not settle: the typeface, the crop, the pace of the cut, the amount of silence. Taste is the trained prediction described in design-taste; this file is the craft beneath it.
4. The team. Who is on the job, which pair gets the hero film, which freelancer gets the retouching, which director gets the call.
5. The client relationship at the level of the work. The account lead owns the commercial relationship; the CD owns the client's trust in the creative judgement. When a client stops asking for three options, the CD has done the job.

Why these five and no more: anything the CD decides that is not one of these (the budget line for catering, the media plan, the tracking study) dilutes the attention that the idea needs. Anything on this list that the CD delegates (the standard, most commonly) decays within two rounds.

### 1.2 Titles and what they mean

| Title | Decides | Does not decide | Typical span |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chief creative officer (CCO) | Creative reputation of the whole agency or network, hiring of CDs, which pitches to take, the agency's own brand | Individual executions on most accounts | 50 to 500 creatives |
| Executive creative director (ECD) | Standard across a group of accounts, kills work before the CCO sees it, the senior client relationship | Day-to-day art direction | 3 to 8 account groups |
| Creative director (CD) | The idea and the standard on named accounts, the team, the production partners | Media, commercial terms | 2 to 6 accounts, 4 to 15 creatives |
| Associate creative director (ACD) | Runs a team of pairs on one or two accounts, first reviewer of work, often still makes work | Which idea ships (recommends, CD decides) | 1 to 2 accounts |
| Art director (AD) | The visual half of the pair: layout, image, type, casting, the look of the film | The verbal idea alone | A pair |
| Copywriter | The verbal half: headline, script, voice, naming, long copy | The visual alone | A pair |
| Designer | Form: identity, systems, layout, type, motion craft; in design-led shops the designer is the AD | The campaign idea unless they are also the AD | Project based |
| Strategist (planner) | The brief, the insight, the proposition, the research (see 08-ux-research-and-strategy.md), the measurement frame | The idea (though the best planners propose territories) | 3 to 6 accounts |
| Producer | Feasibility, money, time, vendors, rights, the production bible | The idea or the aesthetic standard | Per job |

The art and copy pair has been the unit of advertising creativity since Bill Bernbach made the art director and the copywriter a working pair at Doyle Dane Bernbach from its founding in 1949 (Bob Gage and Phyllis Robinson were the first such team); Helmut Krone and Julian Koenig on Volkswagen (1959) is the canonical example (01-history-and-movements.md, section 9.1). The reason the pair works: an image and a line that each need the other to make sense is the structural form of an advertising idea, and two people arguing produce that faster than one person alone.

Two shifts change the table above as of October 2026. Several of the cases in section 9 (Spotify Wrapped, Duolingo, Liquid Death, Cred) came from in-house teams, where the CD reports to a chief marketing officer and owns the brand all year; the client column of the decision rights in 1.5 then belongs to that marketing officer. And the networks are consolidating: Omnicom closed its acquisition of Interpublic on 26 November 2025 and retired the DDB, FCB and MullenLowe brands into BBDO, TBWA and McCann, so the agency that made the pair its method no longer trades under its own name (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, sections 1.1 and 1.6; roles, team shapes and career ladders in sections 2 and 11).

### 1.3 Kinds of creative director

| Kind | Home discipline | Judges work by | Blind spot to correct |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising CD | Film, print, campaign ideas | Does it have an idea, is it famous-able | Systems, product, the identity underneath |
| Design CD | Identity, systems, typography, print, packaging | Is the system coherent, is the craft exact | The campaign idea, the joke, the human moment |
| Digital CD | Web, product, interaction, motion, social | Does it work, does it perform, does it move | Film grammar, print craft, the long copy line |
| Brand CD | Positioning, codes, tone, long-term consistency | Does it come from the brand, will it last | Speed, topicality, the stunt |
| 360 CD | All of the above | All of the above, at once | Depth in each; the fix is a bench of specialists and a reference habit |

The 360 creative director commands every craft well enough to brief, judge and correct the specialist in each, and does at least one craft at the specialist's level. The reason the role exists for Mask360: a luxury hotel in Jaipur, a jewellery house in Mumbai and a developer in Dubai each want one person who can hold the identity, the film, the website, the Instagram grid and the launch event to one standard. Separate CDs per channel produce matching luggage (section 4.3) at best and five different brands at worst.

### 1.4 A week in the life

A working CD's week at a 12 to 25 person studio, based on the rhythm used at independent shops such as Uncommon Creative Studio (D&AD Advertising Agency of the Year 2026) and Mother, adapted to Mask360's size:

| Day | Morning | Afternoon |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Work-in-progress review of every live job, 20 minutes each, standing | Writing or rewriting one brief; client call on the week's approval |
| Tuesday | Crit with the pairs on concept work (section 10.2) | Reference time: one hour reading or looking, logged to the reference board |
| Wednesday | Pre-production meeting (PPM) or shoot | Edit review or craft review |
| Thursday | Client presentation | Team one-to-ones, portfolio reviews |
| Friday | Kill or ship decisions on everything presented that week | Studio show-and-tell, 30 minutes; awards entries or case film work in season |

The rule inside the week: the CD looks at every piece of work at least twice before the client does (once at concept, once at craft) and never sees it for the first time in the client's room.

### 1.5 Decision rights, delegation and the undelegatable

| Decision | CD | ACD or AD | Strategist | Producer | Account lead | Client |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The proposition in the brief | Consulted | Informed | Decides | Informed | Consulted | Approves |
| Which territory becomes the idea | Decides | Recommends | Consulted | Informed | Informed | Approves the idea |
| Casting, location, director | Decides | Recommends | Informed | Options and cost | Informed | Approves |
| Typeface, palette, grid of the system | Decides | Executes | Informed | Informed | Informed | Informed |
| The edit (which take, the cut, the music) | Decides | Recommends | Informed | Schedule | Informed | Approves final |
| Scope, rounds, money | Consulted | Informed | Informed | Recommends | Decides | Agrees |
| Shipping | Signs off | Prepares | Informed | Delivers | Confirms | Approves |

Delegate: first drafts, layout exploration, retouching, versioning, motion polish, vendor search, research synthesis, the second and third rounds of copy.

Never delegate: the single idea sentence, the reference board, the final kill, the edit's last pass, the standards document (section 13.3), the first conversation with a new client about taste, and the apology when something ships wrong.

## 2. The creative brief

### 2.1 The one page

The creative brief is the one-page contract between strategy and creative. Its first line is a Get, Who, To, By summary: Get [the audience] Who [currently believe or do this] To [the new belief or action] By [the direction of the idea]. For the palace hotel in 2.5: Get well-travelled couples in their 50s, who believe luxury hotels are interchangeable, To book direct for a stay as a family's guests, By showing a palace where the family still lives. The fields beneath it, in the order they are read:

| Field | What it must contain | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Background | The business situation in three sentences, with the number that matters (share, occupancy, average order value, launch date) | A stranger could explain why this job exists |
| Problem | The one obstacle between the brand and the objective, stated as what the audience thinks or does today | The client's wish belongs in the objective; this field names the barrier |
| Objective | One behavioural or perceptual change, with a measure, tied to a category entry point (8.2) | Starts with a verb; has a number and a date |
| Target audience with a portrait | A named person, their day, their money, what they already buy, what they would say about the category (see 08-ux-research-and-strategy.md for the research behind it) | The creative can picture them at breakfast |
| Where they will meet it | The channels and the state of mind at the moment of contact (scrolling at 23:00, a travel advisor's inbox, a store visit with the family) | The creative can say which frame the person sees first |
| Insight | The tension or truth that opens the door (section 3) | It explains behaviour, and a restated fact fails it |
| Single-minded proposition (SMP) | The one thing the work must leave in the audience's head, in one sentence of under 12 words | The team can say it without reading it |
| Reasons to believe (RTB) | Two or three proofs: product truth, provenance, number, name | Each is specific and checkable |
| Tone | Three adjectives at most, with a reference for each ("the composure of a Hermès window") | No adjective is "premium" or "luxurious" |
| Mandatories | Logo, legal lines, languages, formats, the thing the founder insists on | Listed, so they stop being surprises |
| Deliverables | Every asset with size, duration, language, quantity | Matches the estimate |
| Budget | Production budget and media budget, separately | A real number, with currency (INR, AED, USD) |
| Timing | Concept presentation, approval, production window, launch | Working days counted, holidays named (Diwali, Eid, the Dubai summer) |
| Success measures | The metric that will be reported, and the baseline | Agreed with the client before creative starts |

Why one page: a brief that runs to four pages has not finished the thinking. The discipline forces the strategist to choose, and the creative team to trust the choice. The famous briefs in 2.4 are all short.

Lineage: the SMP descends from Rosser Reeves's unique selling proposition ("Reality in Advertising", 1961), which demanded one claim no competitor made. Account planning, the discipline that writes the insight half of the brief, began in 1968, when Stanley Pollitt at Boase Massimi Pollitt and Stephen King at J. Walter Thompson London each put a researcher on the account team. The Better Briefs Project's 2022 survey reported that most marketers rate their own briefs as good while few agency people agree, which is the gap the brief back in 2.3 exists to close.

### 2.2 How to write one

1. Start with the objective and the measure. Everything else serves them.
2. Write the portrait from a real interview; a persona deck produces a demographic. One sentence of the person's own words beats a demographic.
3. Find the insight last, after the research (section 3.4). Writing it first produces a slogan dressed as an insight.
4. Draft the SMP ten ways. Pick the one that a creative could draw.
5. Read it aloud to someone outside the account. If they ask "so what do you want people to do", the objective is weak.
6. Attach the reference board (design-taste, section 4, step 1), the trash list of category tropes (design-taste, section 4, step 3) and the research summary (08-ux-research-and-strategy.md). The brief is one page; its appendix can be twenty.

### 2.3 How to read a bad one, and the brief back

The tells of a bad brief: an objective that is a list; an audience of "affluent 25 to 45"; an insight that is a product fact; an SMP with "and" in it; a tone of "premium yet approachable"; no number anywhere; deliverables that say "social assets"; timing that starts yesterday.

The brief back is the creative team's written reply, under 200 words, restating the brief in their own words within 24 hours of receiving it. It states: what we understood the task to be, what we think the real problem is, what we are going to ignore, and what we need before we start. The reason it exists: the gap between what the strategist wrote and what the creative heard is where three wasted weeks live, and the brief back surfaces that gap for the price of an hour.

### 2.4 Famous briefs and what made them

| Brief | Agency, year | What the brief gave the creatives | Why it worked |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Economist | Abbott Mead Vickers BBDO, from 1988 | A reader who is successful because of what they know, and the fear of being caught out among such people | It briefed the reader's self-image and a status anxiety, which let David Abbott write "I never read The Economist. Management trainee. Aged 42." The white-out-of-red format became the idea |
| Guinness "Good things come to those who wait" | AMV BBDO, 1996 onward | A product truth: the pour takes 119.5 seconds and the drinker waits | The wait was reframed from a flaw to the ritual, giving "Surfer" (1999) its subject |
| Dove Campaign for Real Beauty | Ogilvy, 2004 | A research finding from the 2004 "The Real Truth About Beauty" study (3,200 women in 10 countries): about 2 percent of women described themselves as beautiful | The brief carried a cultural tension, which is why the campaign could run for two decades |
| Nike "Just Do It" | Wieden+Kennedy, 1988 | Unify a set of disparate spots under one attitude, for everyone who moves, with the athlete as the standard | Dan Wieden's line gave the attitude a verb, and the first spot used an 80-year-old runner, Walt Stack, to prove "everyone" |

### 2.5 Three Mask360 briefs compared

| Field | Luxury hotel brand film (Rajasthan palace hotel) | Jewellery launch (bridal high jewellery, Mumbai) | Identity (new members' club, Dubai) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objective | Lift direct booking enquiries from the US and UK by a stated percent in the season after launch | Sell a numbered collection in the wedding window (October to February) at full price | Fill the founding membership at the stated fee before opening |
| Audience portrait | A couple in their 50s who have done Aman and want a place with a story; they read the FT Weekend, they book through a travel advisor | A bride's mother who buys the big piece; the bride chooses the design, the mother approves the maker | A founder in their 30s to 40s who already belongs to two clubs and wants the one that is harder to join |
| Insight | They are tired of resorts that could be anywhere; they want to be the guest of a house | The piece is bought once and worn at three weddings; the first wearing is the only one anyone photographs | Belonging is proven by who is kept out |
| SMP | A palace that is still a home | The piece you will be remembered in | The room you cannot buy your way into |
| RTB | The family still lives in one wing; 1700s frescoes; the chef's grandmother's recipes | Hand-set by named karigars (the artisans who set the stones); a stated carat weight; a numbered run of 25 | A founding committee of named people; a capped membership number; a building by a named architect |
| Tone | Patient, quiet, specific | Reverent, precise, unhurried | Dry, assured, few words |
| Deliverables | One 90 second film, three 15 second cuts, 20 stills, a 2 minute advisor edit | Campaign film 60 seconds, 12 product stills, 6 lookbook spreads, store window, invitation | Wordmark, monogram, membership card, stationery, signage, website, launch film, uniforms brief |
| Timing | Shoot in the cool season (November to February); 10 weeks brief to delivery | Shoot by August for an October launch; 12 weeks | 14 to 18 weeks for identity, launch film in parallel from week 10 |

The three differ in the thing the audience needs to feel: trust (hotel), permanence (jewellery), exclusion (club). The brief must name that feeling, because the film, the still and the wordmark each take their form from it.

## 3. Insight

### 3.1 Definition

An insight is a tension or a truth that explains behaviour and opens a creative door. It is held by the audience already; the work recognises it. Jon Steel's definition in "Truth, Lies and Advertising" (1998): the thing that makes the consumer say "that is so true" and the creative say "I know what to do with that". Both halves are required.

### 3.2 Types

| Type | Source | Example | Door it opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human | A universal feeling | Being told "wait" is maddening until the wait is the point (Guinness) | The product's slowest attribute becomes the hero |
| Cultural | A shift in what a society argues about | Women measure themselves against images they know are false (Dove, 2004) | The brand takes a side |
| Category | What everyone in the category does, and resents | Every hotel film has a drone shot of a pool at golden hour | Refuse the trope by name |
| Product | A fact about the thing that nobody has used | Heinz ketchup is drawn the same way by everyone (Rethink, 2021) | The proof is the idea |
| Brand | Something the brand did, made or stood for in its past | Cadbury is the sweet eaten before an auspicious start (Shubh Aarambh, 2010) | A ritual the brand already owns |

### 3.3 Insight, observation, fact

A fact: Indian weddings run three to five days. An observation: the bride's jewellery is photographed most on the first day. An insight: the piece is bought for a lifetime and judged in one photograph. Only the third tells the creative what to make (a campaign built on the one photograph). The test is the second half of Steel's definition: a fact and an observation leave the creative with nothing to do.

### 3.4 How to find one

1. Research first (08-ux-research-and-strategy.md): interviews, observation, the client's own sales data, the search and review language of the category.
2. Cultural observation: what the audience is actually arguing about this year (design-taste, references/currency-2026.md for the aesthetic layer; the strategist supplies the social one).
3. The obvious thing nobody said. Write down the ten most banal truths about the category and the product. One of them, said plainly for the first time, is usually the insight. Volkswagen's small car was small; Avis was second (DDB, 1962, "We try harder").
4. The complaint. What do buyers apologise for or joke about? (Oatly's buyers were embarrassed to order oat milk; the brand made the embarrassment the voice.)
5. The ritual. What do people already do with the product that the brand has never shown?
6. The culture and its codes. Douglas Holt's cultural strategy ("How Brands Become Icons", 2004; with Douglas Cameron, "Cultural Strategy", 2010) looks for a contradiction in the culture that the brand can resolve with a new ideology. Commercial semiotics audits the category's codes as residual (fading), dominant (the current normal) and emergent (rising at the edge), the three tiers Raymond Williams set out in "Marxism and Literature" (1977) and Rachel Lawes applies in "Using Semiotics in Marketing" (2020). The CD's use: the dominant codes go on the trash list, the emergent codes are where a challenger goes, and the contradiction is where a culture idea (4.6) starts. The full method is in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, sections 1.8 and 1.9.

### 3.5 Testing an insight

- Say it to three members of the audience. Two of three should nod before the sentence ends.
- Hand it to a creative pair for 30 minutes. If they come back with three executions, the door is open. If they come back with questions, it is an observation.
- Check it against the competitor set. If a competitor could run it, it is a category insight, which is fine for a challenger and weak for a leader.
- Check it against the product. If the product cannot prove it, it is a wish.

## 4. The idea

### 4.1 Concept and execution

A concept is the organising thought that a stranger could describe without seeing the work: "Guinness is worth the wait". An execution is one expression of it: a surfer waiting for the wave, horses in the foam. The CD's first question in any review is which of the two is on the wall. A room full of executions with no concept is the commonest failure in a concept review; a concept with one execution is a Tuesday.

### 4.2 The campaignable idea and the platform

A campaignable idea produces its second and tenth execution as easily as its first. A platform idea is a campaignable idea that can run for years and absorb product news, seasons and cultures without changing its sentence: "Just Do It" (1988 to now), "Good things come to those who wait" (1996 to 2010s), "Fly Better" (Emirates, November 2018 to now), "Utterly Butterly" (Amul, 1966 to now). A one-off is a single execution with no second: a stunt, a topical, a launch film. One-offs are legitimate (the Burj Khalifa flight attendant for Emirates in August 2021 was a one-off inside a platform). The error is to sell a one-off as a platform, or to let a platform rot into a slogan that nobody executes.

### 4.3 Matching luggage and matching idea

Matching luggage: the same key visual, headline and colour applied to every channel, so the work looks integrated. Matching idea: each channel does what it is best at in service of one thought, so the work is integrated. The first is the 2000s definition of integration and it reads as wallpaper. The second is the standard: the film carries the emotion, the print carries the proof, the social carries the participation, the site carries the transaction, the space carries the gesture (section 6.5). Dumb Ways to Die (McCann Melbourne, 2012) is the textbook: a song, a game, a book, posters and a pledge site each doing a different job for one idea.

### 4.4 The idea test

Four questions, all must pass:

1. Can it be said in a sentence? If the sentence needs "and", there are two ideas or none.
2. Does it scale across channels? Write the social version and the spatial version before the client sees the film.
3. Does it come from the brand? Cover the logo (design-taste, section 4, step 7). If a competitor could run it, it is a category idea.
4. Is it new? Search it (Ads of the World, the D&AD archive, Lürzer's Archive, the Cannes Lions archive). Being first is checkable and the client will check.

### 4.5 The big idea and the small idea done perfectly

The big idea tradition (Bernbach, Ogilvy, Lois) holds that one large thought, famous and repeated, beats a hundred small ones. Byron Sharp's "How Brands Grow" (2010) and Les Binet and Peter Field's "The Long and the Short of It" (2013) supply the evidence that fame and consistent distinctive assets drive long-term growth, with Binet and Field's recommended split of about 60 percent brand building to 40 percent activation. The 60:40 figure is an average. Their "Effectiveness in Context" (Institute of Practitioners in Advertising, IPA, 2018) shows the best split moving with category, price and growth stage, and their B2B analysis for the LinkedIn B2B Institute (2019) put the B2B optimum near 46:54. The house reads it per client: a luxury house sits above 60 on brand because the dream is the product; a real estate launch with a sales window sits nearer the activation end.

Luxury runs a second tradition: the small idea done perfectly. A Hermès window, an Aesop store, a Bottega Veneta lookbook, a Jacquemus product film each carry a modest thought (a horse, a shelf of books, a weave, a bag at the wrong scale) executed to a standard that makes it unmistakable. Kapferer and Bastien (section 8.1) explain why: luxury sells by maintaining the dream, and dream maintenance is craft. The house position: for Mask360's luxury clients, the small idea done perfectly is the default; the big idea is reserved for a launch, a repositioning or a challenger in a premium category (a new hotel brand against Taj and Oberoi, a jewellery house against Tanishq).

### 4.6 Three sources of ideas

| Source | Mechanism | Examples | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product truth | Find the one fact about the thing and make the work out of it | Matthieu Blazy's Bottega Veneta (November 2021 to 2024): the leather made to look like denim, flannel, paper; Apple's "Shot on iPhone" (2015): the pictures are the proof | Needs a product that is actually true |
| Culture | Take a side in a live argument, or borrow a cultural object the audience loves | Nike "Dream Crazy" (2018); Loewe x Studio Ghibli (2021 to 2023); Cadbury's cricket girl (1994) in a cricket-mad country | Borrowing without permission or fluency (section 16.2) |
| Form | A formal gesture (scale, repetition, colour, material) is the idea | Jacquemus bags the size of buses (2023); Bottega's green; the Economist's red; Tiffany's blue box | Form with no thought under it becomes decoration within a year |

The single idea sentence (design-taste, section 4, step 2) is written before any execution and travels on every slide, call sheet and edit note. Example for a hotel: "A palace that is still a home".

### 4.7 Brand purpose: the positions

Purpose (a brand's stated reason to exist beyond profit, used as the source of its ideas) is contested.

- For: Jim Stengel, "Grow" (2011), argued from a study of 50 brands that those built on an ideal of improving people's lives outgrew their competitors; Unilever reported in 2019 that its Sustainable Living brands were growing 69 percent faster than the rest of the business and delivered 75 percent of its growth.
- Against: Terry Smith of Fundsmith, a Unilever shareholder, wrote in his January 2022 letter to investors that a company which feels it has to define the purpose of Hellmann's mayonnaise has "clearly lost the plot"; Mark Ritson has argued in his Marketing Week columns that purpose suits a minority of brands and distracts the rest; and Unilever under Hein Schumacher (chief executive from July 2023) said it would stop forcing purpose onto every brand.
- House position: in luxury, purpose shows as craft and conduct (makers credited by name, a supply chain the house will show, restraint in volume and price). A public stand is taken only when the brand has already lived it (Nike, 9.1) and the client will hold it under pressure (10.9, 16.2). Tanishq's withdrawal of "Ekatvam" in 2020 (9.3) is the cost of a stand the client could not hold.

## 5. Ideation methods

### 5.1 Young's technique

James Webb Young, "A Technique for Producing Ideas" (lectures 1939, published 1940): an idea is a new combination of old elements, and the ability to see relationships is the whole skill. Five steps: gather raw material (specific to the product and general to life); work it over (turn every fact, write every partial thought on a card); incubate (stop, do something else); the idea arrives; shape it to the real world (show it, let it be criticised). The reason the method survives: it forces the gathering stage that impatient teams skip, and it names incubation as work. Mask360 use: the reference board and the research summary are the raw material; a 48-hour gap between briefing and the first crit is the incubation.

### 5.2 Lateral thinking

Edward de Bono, "Lateral Thinking" (1970), and "Six Thinking Hats" (1985). The useful tools: random entry (bring in an unrelated word or object and force a connection), provocation (state something absurd, "the hotel has no rooms", and move from it), reversal (the opposite of the category convention), and the hats as a way to separate critique (black hat) from generation (green hat) in a crit so that the room does not kill ideas while making them.

### 5.3 The pair, the 100 bad ideas and the territory

- The pair. Art and copy in one room, 90-minute sessions, no laptops for the first hour. The AD draws, the writer talks, the roles swap when stuck.
- The 100 bad ideas. Each pair writes 100 ideas in a day, numbered, most of them bad. The rule exists because the first 20 are the category's clichés, the next 30 are the agency's clichés, and the idea is somewhere after 50. Dave Trott's "Creative Mischief" (2009) and Paul Arden's "It's Not How Good You Are, It's How Good You Want to Be" (2003) are the short books that make the case for volume before judgement.
- Territories. Group the 100 into 3 to 5 territories: a territory is a direction with a feeling, a reference and a sample execution, short of a finished idea. A tone map plots territories on two axes the brief cares about (for a hotel: intimate to grand, quiet to witty) so the CD and client can see where the brand sits and where the competitors crowd.
- "What we are not doing". The trash list from design-taste, section 4, step 3, written for ideas as well as aesthetics: the category tropes refused by name.

### 5.4 Constraint, reference and the two tests

- Constraint as fuel. Give the pair a real limit (no people in the film, one colour, one location, a budget of Rs 8 lakh (a lakh is 100,000 rupees), a five-second format) and the ideas improve. Duolingo's five-second Super Bowl spot (2024) and Jacquemus' computer-generated bags (2023, no shoot) are constraint ideas.
- The reference board method. Three to five references per territory, each with the thing borrowed named (design-taste, section 4, step 1). Boards without the "what is borrowed" line are mood boards and are banned.
- The single idea sentence. Written on the wall before executions, rewritten when the executions teach something.
- The no idea test. Remove the headline and the logo. If the picture still means the same thing as every competitor's picture, there is no idea. Then remove the picture and keep the line. If the line could sit under any product in the category, there is no idea.

### 5.5 Creativity templates and other second-pass methods

- Creativity templates. Jacob Goldenberg, David Mazursky and Sorin Solomon, "The Fundamental Templates of Quality Ads" (Marketing Science, 1999), compared award finalists with non-awarded ads in the same categories and found that most of the finalists fitted one of six templates and few of the others did: pictorial analogy (the quality shown through a symbol from another world), extreme situation (the attribute pushed into an absurd setting), consequences (what follows from using or not using the product), competition (the product beats something unexpected), interactive experiment (the viewer is made to perform a test), and dimensionality alteration (time, scale or number changed). The house runs the templates as a second pass after the 100 bad ideas (5.3), one hour per template, because each one generates from the product's own attribute. Fevicol's overloaded bus (9.3) is an extreme situation; the Heinz drawing (9.2) is an interactive experiment.
- Mario Pricken's "Creative Advertising" (2002) catalogues several hundred such provocations by mechanism, each with an awarded example; it is the reference shelf when the templates run dry.
- Random entry for a stuck room: Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's "Oblique Strategies" cards (1975), one card drawn per stalled territory.
- 6-3-5 brainwriting (Bernd Rohrbach, 1968): six people write three ideas each in five minutes, pass the sheet and build on the previous three, for six rounds: up to 108 ideas in 30 minutes, with the quiet members of a team heard as clearly as the loud.

### 5.6 AI in the process

As of October 2026 generative tools sit inside every stage of a campaign, and the CD decides where. The tools, prompting and tells are in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, sections 2, 3 and 5; its stage rules (section 6.10) govern, and this is the CD's layer above them.

| Stage | Use | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Ideation | Ask a model for the first 50 ideas any agency would have for the brief | They go straight onto the trash list (5.3): the model's average is the category's cliché |
| References | Mood and lighting studies where no photograph exists | Labelled as generated on the board; a real reference outranks a generated one |
| Presenting | AI animatics, which avoid the unlicensed-footage problem of a ripomatic (11.4) | Every AI frame shown to a client is captioned "not a shot frame", and the real version is costed before the meeting |
| Pre-production | Previsualisation of camera moves and set builds | Shared with the director and DP as a question for them to answer |
| Production and post | Versioning, localisation, dubbing, retouching clean-up, set extension | Logged in the production bible (14.8) as synthetic and disclosed (16.5) |
| The hero frame | The metal, the stone, the weave, the skin, the room stay real | A luxury object rendered by a model is a claim about the object, so the house shoots it |

The presenting rule exists because clients fall in love with frames nobody can shoot or afford, and a frame that cannot be made is a promise the studio breaks at PPM. A real person's likeness is generated only with that person's written consent (16.5).

Cases worth knowing:

- Coca-Cola's "Holidays Are Coming" remake, made with generative video (November 2024), was criticised for uncanny faces and shifting trucks: the brand's best-loved Christmas code spent on a demonstration of tooling.
- Toys"R"Us's brand film about its founder, made with OpenAI's Sora by Native Foreign and premiered at Cannes Lions in June 2024, drew the same criticism of craft.
- The AI-generated model in a Guess advertisement in the August 2025 US Vogue drew a body-image backlash, because a synthetic body sets a standard no real body meets (16.4).
- Cadbury's "Not Just a Cadbury Ad" (Ogilvy Mumbai, 2021) is the positive case: Shah Rukh Khan consented to a synthetic version of his face and voice, which let small neighbourhood shops generate an ad naming their own store during the pandemic. The synthetic element served the idea, and the mechanic was disclosed.

House position: in luxury the craft is the promise, so AI that is visible as AI reads as cost cutting. It is used where the audience never sees it, and kept away from the client's object.

## 6. The idea in each channel

### 6.1 Film

- The 30-second structure. Three beats: set-up (seconds 0 to 8, the world and the tension), turn (seconds 8 to 22, the idea), resolution (seconds 22 to 30, product and line). The brand should be identifiable by second 3 on platforms where skipping begins at second 5 (YouTube skippable in-stream) and the first frame must work with the sound off for feeds. Google's ABCD framework for YouTube (Attract, Brand, Connect, Direct) asks for early and repeated branding; its evidence and the platform rules are in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, section 8.2. Orlando Wood's "Lemon" (IPA, 2019) gives the long-term effectiveness evidence for right-brain features: characters, dialogue, a sense of place, humour, music with melody.
- The long-form brand film (60 to 180 seconds). Cartier's "L'Odyssée de Cartier" (2012, directed by Bruno Aveillan, 3 minutes 30 seconds) is the reference for a house telling its history as spectacle; Google "Reunion" (Ogilvy India, 2013, 3 minutes 32 seconds, directed by Amit Sharma) is the reference for a product demonstrated through a story. Long form earns its length by one beat the viewer could not predict at minute one.
- The fashion film with no story. The house codes, a body, a place, a pace. Loewe's and Bottega Veneta's product films under Jonathan Anderson and Matthieu Blazy carried no plot; the idea was the object seen in a way that only that house would show it. The CD's test: freeze any frame and ask whether it is a photograph the house would print.
- Music first. For luxury film, the track is chosen or composed before the shot list, because pace in the edit is set by the music and the shoot must cover that pace. Guinness "Surfer" was cut to Leftfield's "Phat Planet" (1999). License before the shoot (section 14.5; the licences in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 12.2).

### 6.2 Print and out-of-home

- Headline and visual. The Bernbach form: the two halves complete each other and neither works alone. "Think Small" (1959) with a tiny car in white space; the Economist's white line on red with no visual at all.
- The visual metaphor. One object stands for the claim: the Heinz drawing (2021), the mouldy Whopper (2020), the Fevicol-strong bus (2001). The test is whether the metaphor survives without the headline.
- The one-liner. A line that is the whole ad. The Economist ran for 30 years on it. The Indian masters are Amul's topicals (daCunha Communications, weekly since 1966), where the pun is the idea and the Amul girl is the constant.
- Out-of-home reads in 3 seconds at 60 kilometres an hour on the Sheikh Zayed Road and in 1 second on a Mumbai hoarding at a signal. Seven words or fewer; the house type set at a size that reads at 50 metres; one image. Formats, type size by viewing distance and materials are in 20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md, section 7.

### 6.3 Social

- The drop. One asset, timed, scarce, announced and gone: the fashion model (Supreme on Thursdays, Jacquemus launch posts).
- The series. A repeatable format with a constant and a variable: Amul's topicals, Zomato's and Swiggy's push notifications and festival posts, Aesop's quotations. The format is the idea; each post is an execution.
- The format. Native to the platform's grammar: a 9:16 vertical, sound on, text on screen, the first 1.5 seconds doing the job of a headline, on the platforms the market actually uses (table below). A luxury house on social keeps the pace of the house (Hermès, Bottega) and refuses the platform's pace; a challenger (Duolingo, Liquid Death) adopts the platform's pace as the idea. Platform craft is in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, sections 3, 4 and 5, and the specs in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md.

|---|---|---|

### 6.4 Digital

- The microsite as the campaign. Dumb Ways to Die's pledge site, Spotify Wrapped (2016 onward) as a personalised page that people post. The site is the idea when the user's own action produces the content.
- The interactive idea. A mechanism the user operates: Heinz asked people to draw ketchup; Wrapped asked Spotify to compute a year. The CD's test is whether the mechanism would be interesting if the brand's name were removed.

### 6.5 Experiential

One spatial gesture, built to a standard, photographed as the real deliverable. Jacquemus' pop-ups and the giant Le Bambino installations; Aesop's windows; a palace hotel's single long dinner table in the courtyard. The reason for one gesture: a space with five ideas photographs as a trade fair.

### 6.6 PR and earned

The stunt that is on brand: Emirates' flight attendant on the pinnacle of the Burj Khalifa (August 2021, a real person, Nicole Smith-Ludvik, with a real safety rig), Duolingo's "death" of Duo (February 2025), Liquid Death's product launches sold as horror. The test is whether the stunt is something the brand would have done with no camera present. A stunt that any brand could do is media spend in disguise.

### 6.7 Retail and packaging

Retail: the store as the brand's most complete expression; Aesop commissions a different architect for each store so that no two are alike, which is itself a campaign of several hundred executions. Packaging as campaign: Tiffany's blue box, the Hermès orange box, Bottega's green, the Amul butter pack, the Tanishq red. The pack is the medium that reaches every buyer and survives every campaign; for a jewellery client the box and the certificate are briefs of their own (packaging craft in 20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md, section 6, jewellery in 6.8; retail build in section 8).

Clienteling, the one-to-one selling a client advisor does with known customers, is the luxury conversion channel, and in India and the Gulf it runs through the advisor's WhatsApp. Every luxury campaign therefore ships a clienteling kit: 6 to 10 stills and one 15 second vertical cut cleared for one-to-one sending; the campaign story written in the advisor's voice as three short messages; a private-appointment invitation; and a lookbook PDF sized for a phone screen. For the top clients the experiential layer is the private viewing or trunk show (a collection shown by appointment in a suite, a home or the store after hours). Broadcasts on the WhatsApp Business Platform need each customer's opt-in under Meta's business policy (17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, section 3.12), and an advisor's customer list is personal data under India's and the UAE's data protection laws (16.11).

### 6.8 The integrated architecture

| Model | Structure | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Hero, hub, help (Google, 2014, also written "hero, hub, hygiene") | Hero: the one big piece, few times a year. Hub: the regular series that keeps the audience. Help: the always-on content that answers searches | Content planning for a brand's owned channels |
| Teaser, launch, sustain | Teaser: scarcity and curiosity, 1 to 3 weeks. Launch: the hero and the full channel set in one week. Sustain: 6 to 12 weeks of executions, proof, participation | Campaign sequencing for a product or property launch |

Sequencing rule for a Mask360 launch: the teaser is a drop (6.3), the launch is the film and the space in the same 72 hours, the sustain is the series. The sustain is planned before the launch is approved, because the launch without a sustain is a one-off (4.2).

### 6.9 Paid social and the creative system

Each week the client's media or performance team asks the CD for variants. The answer is a system designed at PPM and built to how the buying platforms now work.

- Concepts before variants. Meta's Andromeda ad retrieval system (announced 2 December 2024) and its Advantage+ automation match distinct creatives to distinct people, so the differences between creatives act as a targeting signal. The house briefs 3 to 5 genuinely different concepts per ad set (a different angle, person, setting or format); 30 recolours of one key visual count as one concept. Platform detail and the testing method are in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, sections 8.1 and 8.7.
- The modular asset system, planned at PPM and shot as cover (10.6): a hero master; 6, 15 and 30 second cuts; 9:16, 4:5 and 1:1 masters framed on set; text-free plates; end cards as separate files; the hook (the first 3 seconds) shot three ways. Planned at PPM, the system costs a day of cover; built in post from a 16:9 master, it costs the frame.
- Fatigue. Creative fatigue (the fall in performance as the same audience sees an ad repeatedly) is read from frequency, with the house refresh trigger at 3.5 average impressions per person (17, section 8.1), and from click-through rate decaying week on week. The refresh is a new concept every 3 to 4 weeks and new hooks on winning concepts every 1 to 2.
- The performance standard: one page per luxury client, signed with the media team. No price stickers or discount bursts on hero assets; at least one code (8.2) in every variant; offers live only in the activation layer of the brand and activation split (4.5); Meta's automatic creative enhancements switched off on graded images; the end frame designed by the house.

### 6.10 Creators and talent

Creator partnerships are a main channel for luxury in Dubai and India. Direction, rights, tiers and vetting are in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, sections 5.8 and 5.9. The CD's rules:

- The creator brief is one page: the idea sentence; two or three moments the product must appear in (in hand, on skin, the place named); the mandatories; the "what we are not doing" list (5.3); and freedom over format and voice. Creator work is valuable because it reads as the creator's, so the house allows one concept approval and one draft approval and never line-edits the creator's voice.
- Usage is priced separately from the post: reposting, paid usage as partnership ads or whitelisting (running the creator's post from their handle as an ad), each with a term (30, 90 or 365 days), a territory and an exclusivity window (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7).
- Disclosure. India: ASCI's Guidelines for Influencer Advertising in Digital Media (launched May 2021, in force from June 2021) accept labels such as Advertisement, Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration, Partnership, Employee and Free gift, placed upfront; on video without text the label stays for at least 3 seconds on videos of 15 seconds or less, a third of the length up to 2 minutes, and the whole brand section beyond that (17, section 5.8). The Department of Consumer Affairs' "Endorsements Know-hows!" (January 2023) adds the statutory layer. UAE: the Advertiser Permit (16.6). Saudi Arabia: the Mawthooq licence for influencers who advertise, issued by the General Authority for Media Regulation; confirm the current fee and term before contracting.
- The luxury test: book only a creator the house would photograph without the product (8.5). A creator whose audience follows for discounts teaches that audience the brand discounts.

### 6.11 Sound, scent and immersive

- The sonic code: a mnemonic of two to four seconds plus a house palette of instruments, treated as a brand code (8.2). Titan's adaptation of Mozart's Symphony No. 25 (9.3) is the Indian model of a sonic code held for decades; the brief and craft are in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 15.
- Scent: a hotel's signature scent in the lobby, the room and the invitation envelope is a campaign code the guest carries home; it is briefed to a perfumer with the same reference board as the film.
- Lenses: Snapchat AR (augmented reality) lenses are a primary format in Saudi Arabia (6.3). A lens earns its place when it does what the brand does (a try-on, a reveal); a face filter with a logo is media spend.
- AR try-on for jewellery (CaratLane among others in India) turns the product page into a fitting. It collects faces, so the consent rule in 16.11 applies before build.
- Gaming: Gucci Garden on Roblox (May 2021) and Balenciaga's Fortnite collaboration (September 2021) translated a house's codes into a game's own economy of skins and spaces.

Each of these passes the idea test (4.4) or is cut, because a gesture in a new medium with no idea under it is a demonstration of the medium.

### 6.13 Ramadan as a season

Ramadan is the peak season for viewing and advertising in the Arab world. Brands launch the main film of their year in the week before it, against the Ramadan drama series families watch after iftar (the meal that breaks the fast at sunset).

- Tone: generosity, family, the iftar table, reflection, the hour before sunset. Little or no hard selling in the first nights; offers belong to the last ten days and Eid. Wit is allowed in the Kuwaiti and Saudi tradition: Zain, the Kuwaiti telecoms group, releases a Ramadan film each year, and its 2017 film confronted terrorism directly.
- Craft reference: the Dubai Lynx 2026 Film Craft Grand Prix to Shareet Studios for "WABA Ramadhan", Kuwait's first Grand Prix (15.1).
- The trash list: lanterns, crescents, dates and gold on navy are every brand's Ramadan (04-colour.md); they appear only when the idea earns them.
- Timing: Gulf social use shifts after iftar and late into the night (17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md, sections 3.13 and 5.5), so premieres and posts are scheduled for the evening.
- Production: all footage is shot before Ramadan begins. Showing people eating, drinking or smoking in public in daylight is a sensitivity issue in the UAE and stricter in Saudi Arabia, and fasting crews work shorter days. Approvals slow under the reduced private-sector hours in 12.9, so the approval calendar is planned backward from the first night.

## 7. Headline and copy craft for creative directors

### 7.1 Structures

| Structure | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The twist | A familiar phrase with one word changed or one expectation reversed | "Think Small" (VW, 1959) |
| The proof | A fact that does the persuading | "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" (Ogilvy, 1958) |
| The challenge | The reader is dared | "Just Do It" (Nike, 1988) |
| The confession | The brand admits something | "Lemon" (VW, 1960); "We try harder" (Avis, 1962) |
| The instruction | An imperative that is also the attitude | "Fly Better" (Emirates, 2018); "Murder your thirst" (Liquid Death) |
| The list | A run of like items, then the turn | "Here's to the crazy ones. The misfits. The rebels. The troublemakers. The round pegs in the square holes." (Apple, 1997): the list builds a type of person, and the script turns on "because the people who are crazy enough to think they can change the world are the ones who do" |
| The wordplay used once | A pun carrying a real meaning, used once per campaign | Amul's topicals use it weekly, which only works because the pun is the format |

### 7.2 Bernbach and Abbott

Bill Bernbach's lessons, as recorded in his agency's work and Bob Levenson's "Bill Bernbach's Book" (1987): say the truth with wit; the execution is the idea; rules are what the artist breaks. David Abbott's lessons, from his AMV BBDO work (Sainsbury's, Volvo, The Economist, Yellow Pages): write to one person; keep the sentences short enough to be read on a bus; put the product in the line; the long copy must be as well written as a novel's first page; read everything aloud. Both men's places in the history are in 01-history-and-movements.md, section 9.

### 7.3 Rules for judging copy

- The no adjective rule. Strike every adjective, then put back only those that carry a fact ("hand-set", "numbered", "1700s"). "Luxurious", "exquisite", "bespoke", "curated" are banned in house copy.
- One line, one job. A headline that explains and sells and jokes does none.
- Specific beats general: a number, a name, a place.
- The line a confident person would say aloud (design-taste, section 5, copy).
- Say it in the audience's language, including the Hinglish (Hindi and English mixed in one sentence) or Arabic-English register of the market, and have a native speaker read it (section 16.2).
- The house system of copy rules (the 15 rules, the sentence case, the banned words, the no-contrast rule) lives in the mask360-copywriting skill; this section is the judging layer above it, and the house rules win on any conflict.

### 7.4 Transcreation and bilingual lines

Transcreation is rewriting a line natively in the target language from the idea sentence, as a native writer would have written it in the first place; translation carries the words across, and transcreation carries the idea. The process:

1. Brief the native writer with the idea sentence, the trash list and the references, and hold the English line back until they have written their own.
2. Ask for two or three native options.
3. Back-translate each literally for the client, with a note on what the line does in its own language (rhyme, register, the proverb it echoes).
4. Replace every pun; a pun is never translated.
5. A second native speaker reads the final in layout (16.2).

Arabic register: Modern Standard Arabic for government, luxury and formal work; Gulf (Khaleeji) dialect for warmth and social; Saudi dialect increasingly for KSA-only campaigns; Egyptian Arabic for pan-Arab entertainment. Arabic reads right to left, so the gaze and the product move right to left and directional graphics mirror; the script needs more vertical space, and the line is typeset by a native designer (right-to-left craft in 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md, section 6.2; bilingual lockups in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 3.4).

India: the choice is between Devanagari Hindi, Roman-script Hinglish and the regional languages, market by market (10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md, sections 6.7 and 6.8). Piyush Pandey's lesson, told in "Pandeymonium" (2015): write in the language the audience thinks in. The reference lines are "Daag Achhe Hain" ("stains are good", Surf Excel, Lowe Lintas), "Thanda Matlab Coca-Cola" ("cold means Coca-Cola", McCann) and the Fevicol and Cadbury work in 9.3.

## 8. Luxury creative strategy

### 8.1 Kapferer and Bastien's anti-laws

Jean-Noël Kapferer and Vincent Bastien, "The Luxury Strategy" (2009; second edition 2012), set out 24 anti-laws of marketing: rules that reverse conventional marketing because luxury sells a dream and a distance, and conventional marketing closes distance. The full list, in their wording:

1. Forget about positioning: luxury is not comparative. 2. Does your product have enough flaws to give it soul? 3. Don't pander to your customers' wishes. 4. Keep non-enthusiasts out. 5. Don't respond to rising demand. 6. Dominate the client. 7. Make it difficult for clients to buy. 8. Protect clients from non-clients, the big from the small. 9. The role of advertising is not to sell. 10. Communicate to those whom you are not targeting. 11. The presumed price should always seem higher than the actual price. 12. Luxury sets the price; price does not set luxury. 13. Raise your prices as time goes on, in order to increase demand. 14. Keep raising the average price of the product range. 15. Do not sell. 16. Keep stars out of your advertising. 17. Cultivate closeness to the arts for initiates. 18. Do not relocate your factories. 19. Do not hire consultants. 20. Do not test. 21. Do not look for consensus. 22. Do not look after group synergies. 23. Do not look for cost reduction. 24. Do not sell openly on the Internet.

The ones a CD acts on daily: 9 and 10 (the film is for everyone, the purchase is for the few, so the work must be seen widely and feel exclusive), 16 (the ambassador logic in 8.5), 20 and 21 (no focus groups on the film, no consensus in the client's committee), 2 (the flaw with soul: the hand-stitch that is not perfect, the grain in the image). The house position: the anti-laws describe the top of the pyramid (Hermès, Patek Philippe, Aman) accurately and premium brands (a five-star chain, a bridge jewellery brand) must break several of them, chiefly 5, 7 and 24.

### 8.2 Restraint and codes

Restraint is the craft expression of anti-law 15. Codes are the brand's owned, repeated signs (Byron Sharp's distinctive brand assets, in luxury terms): Hermès orange and the horse, Tiffany's blue, Bottega's green and the intrecciato weave, Chanel's tweed and camellia, Cartier's panther, Burberry's check, the Taj dome, the Tanishq red. The CD's job across a campaign is to spend the codes with discipline: at least one code in every execution and fewer than all of them at once, and new codes introduced only at a launch and then repeated for years.

A code is measured before it is spent or dropped. Jenni Romaniuk's "Building Distinctive Brand Assets" (2018) scores each code for fame (the share of category buyers who link it to the brand) and uniqueness (how few link it to a competitor) and places it on a grid: use or lose, investment potential, avoid solo use, ignore or test (the survey method is in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 1.11). The house runs the survey before any rebrand drops a code, because a code with fame represents years of media spend. Her "Better Brand Health" (2022) adds category entry points (CEPs): the buying situations ("a gift for my mother's 60th", "a weekend away from Mumbai in the monsoon") the brand must be linked to in memory. The brief's objective (2.1) names the CEP the campaign is built to own.

### 8.4 The lookbook as idea

A lookbook is a sequence of images of a collection, in order. At the top houses it is the primary creative product: Bottega Veneta's under Blazy, The Row's, Lemaire's. The idea lives in the sequence (what is adjacent to what), the one treatment (one light, one lens, one distance) and the ending. For a jewellery or fashion client at Mask360, the lookbook is briefed as a film with no motion: a shot list with a reason for each shot and an order.

### 8.5 Celebrity, ambassador and collaboration logic

- Ambassador logic. Anti-law 16 is broken by most houses, within a rule: the ambassador is cast as a long-running character (Theron for Dior since 2004) or as a reader of the house's codes (Deepika Padukone as a Louis Vuitton house ambassador from 2022; Alia Bhatt as a Gucci global ambassador from 2023); a spokesperson reading claims is the failure case. The test for a Mask360 jewellery client: would the house photograph this person without the product? If yes, the person's fame is doing the work and the house is renting it.
- Collaboration logic. Loewe x Studio Ghibli (Totoro 2021, Spirited Away 2022, Howl's Moving Castle 2023) borrowed a loved world and translated it into the house's craft. Tiffany x Nike (Air Force 1 1837, March 2023, US$400) put a house code on another brand's object and drew mixed reactions because the object did not change. Rule: the collaboration must produce an object neither party could make alone.
- The drop model. Scarcity by time and number: a numbered run, a date, a queue. For Indian and Gulf clients, the drop maps onto the festival calendar (6.12) and the wedding season. Every number and deadline in a drop is a scarcity claim and needs evidence (16.10).
- Scarcity and silence. In the house's observation (unquantified), the most expensive houses post least and buy least paid media relative to their revenue. A luxury client who asks for daily posts is asking to look like a premium brand.
- When wit is allowed. Tension and wit are design-taste principles (design-taste, section 1, principles 5 and 8). The campaign limit: wit lands on scale, the object, culture or the house itself, and never on the customer, the price or the craft.

## 10. Running the creative process

### 10.1 The stage gates

A campaign for a Mask360 client runs through eleven stage gates (review points, each with an owner and one yes-or-no question that funds the next stage). Each gate has an input and an output. The durations assume a 60 second film with stills and a social series, 10 to 14 weeks from brief to launch; an identity runs on the schedule in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 5.1.

| Gate | Week | Who is in the room | Input | Output | The gate question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kickoff | 1 | CD, strategist, account lead, producer, the client's decision maker | Signed brief (section 2), budget, timing | Brief back (2.3), reference board, trash list, the week-by-week plan | Does everyone describe the task in the same sentence? |
| Immersion | 1 to 2 | Creative team, strategist | Research summary (08-ux-research-and-strategy.md), site visits, the product in hand | A one-page "what we learned" with the insight candidates | Have the creatives touched the product and met the buyer? |
| Territory review | 3 | CD, pairs, strategist | 100 bad ideas (5.3), 3 to 5 territories with references | Three territories chosen, with the idea sentence drafted for each | Could each territory produce ten executions? |
| Tissue session | End of 3 | CD, strategist, account lead, the client's decision maker | Three territories as rough tissues: a sentence, a reference board and one sample execution each | The client's directional choice, in writing | Which territory does the client want to see made? |
| Concept review | 4 to 5 | CD, pairs, strategist, account lead; the client at the end of the week | One recommended concept, presented whole, with one reserve held back (11.3): a film outline, a key visual, a social format, a spatial gesture | One concept approved by the client, in writing, with the idea sentence | Does the concept pass the idea test (4.4)? |
| Craft review | 6 | CD, pairs, producer, director or photographer shortlisted | Script to draft 3, key visual to layout, type and colour tokens, the director's treatment (the director's written and visual approach to the script; 14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, section 3.1) | Locked script, locked key visual, chosen director | Would the house print any frame of this? |
| Pre-production meeting (PPM) approval | 7 to 8 | CD, producer, director, production company, client | The PPM book: treatment, boards, cast, locations, wardrobe, props, schedule, budget | Client signature on the PPM book; changes after it are charged | Is there anything on the shoot day that has not been decided? |
| Production | 8 to 9 | CD, producer, director, crew; client at video village (the monitor position where agency and client watch takes) | PPM book | Rushes (the raw footage) and the stills selects | Did we shoot the idea, and did we shoot the cover (the alternatives that protect the edit)? |
| Post review | 10 to 12 | CD, editor, colourist, sound designer, producer | Assembly (every usable take in script order), rough cut, fine cut, grade, mix (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 5) | Picture lock, then the final masters per 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md | Is the idea visible in the first 3 seconds and the last frame? |
| Launch | 13 to 14 | CD, account lead, client, media | Masters, the channel plan (6.8), the sustain series, the baseline (10.8) | Live, measured against the brief's success measures | Is the sustain ready before the hero goes out? |
| Post-launch review | 4 and 12 weeks after launch | CD, strategist, account lead, client, media | Delivery data, brand lift results, the baseline (10.8) | A one-page learning note, filed with the kill list and the case library | Did the work move the brief's measure against the baseline? |

Why gates and signatures: creative work decays through unmade decisions. A client who has signed a PPM book cannot change the cast on the shoot day without paying, and a team that knows the gate question works towards it. The tissue session (a rough, low-cost showing of territories to get the client's directional choice) is the only point where the client is offered a choice (11.3). The client chooses a direction there and signs at concept and at PPM, and nowhere in between: feedback at craft review is the CD's job, and a client in the craft stage judges the execution of an idea they have not yet seen whole.

### 10.2 The critique method

The crit (critique) is a structured review of work in progress. The full method, with the vocabulary and the written format, is in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md, section 4, and in design-taste, section 8. A shared scale keeps reviews comparable across the year: the house scores ideas on the rubric in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md, section 13.1; Leo Burnett's 10-point creative scale, running from destructive work at 1 to the best in the world at 10, is the agency precedent. The rules a CD holds in every crit:

1. Idea before craft. The first ten minutes are only about whether the idea is there and whether it is the brief's idea. Craft notes on a dead idea waste the room.
2. Describe before judge. The reviewer says what they see ("the line and the image are saying the same thing twice") before saying what they feel.
3. One owner of the decision. The crit informs; the CD decides, in the room, and says the decision aloud so nobody leaves with a different version.
4. Notes go to the problem. "The ending does not land" is a note; "end on the logo" is a solution, and solutions from the room produce committee work.
5. The reference is the argument. Disagreements are settled by putting a reference next to the work (design-taste, section 4, step 1), because taste argued in words is opinion and taste argued in references is evidence.

### 10.3 The braintrust

Ed Catmull's braintrust at Pixar (described in "How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity", Harvard Business Review, September 2008, and in "Creativity, Inc.", 2014, with Amy Wallace) is a group of experienced makers who watch a film in progress every few months and give candid notes, with three rules: the braintrust has no authority, so the director decides what to do with the notes; the notes address the film, and the person is out of scope; candour is the point, so politeness that hides a problem is a failure of the group. Pixar's claim is that every one of its films was bad at some point and the braintrust is the mechanism that made them good.

Mask360's adaptation for a 12 to 25 person studio: a monthly two-hour braintrust on the two biggest live jobs, attended by the CD, one senior from a different discipline (a designer on a film, a writer on an identity), one outside practitioner under a non-disclosure agreement, and the producer. The job's own team presents and takes notes; nobody on the job argues back in the room. The reason for the outsider: a small studio's taste converges within a year, and the outsider is the only person who sees the house style as a style.

### 10.4 When to kill work

Kill criteria, any one of which ends the route:

- It fails the idea test (4.4) after two rounds of development. Two rounds is the limit because the third round is where the team starts to love the execution and stops seeing the idea.
- The client's decision maker has said no twice to the same objection. The third presentation of the same route is a request to be fired.
- It can be made only by breaking the budget by more than 20 percent or the timing by more than two weeks, and the client has refused both.
- It has been done. The archive search (4.4, test 4) found it.
- The CD cannot say the single idea sentence without reading it.

The sunk cost rule: the hours already spent are gone whether the route lives or dies, so they count for nothing in the decision (23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md, section 8). Work that touches religion, gender, caste, nation or community also passes the cultural risk review in 10.9 before it ships. The kill list is a running document of killed routes with one line on why, kept because killed ideas return on other briefs (Young's raw material, 5.1) and because a client who asks "what about doing X" should hear "we did, on 14 March, and here is why it died".

### 10.5 Protecting the idea through production

Ideas die in production by a hundred reasonable decisions: the location that was cheaper, the cast that was available, the line that legal softened, the edit that the client's nephew preferred. The defences:

- The idea sentence travels. It is printed on the call sheet (the daily schedule issued to cast and crew), on the first page of the PPM book, on the edit's whiteboard and on every review deck.
- The departure log. Every change from the approved PPM book is written down with who asked and why, and the CD signs each one. A change that nobody will sign is a change that should not happen.
- The "what would make this generic" list. Written at concept approval: the five things that, if they crept in, would turn this film into the category's film (the drone over the pool, the slow-motion smile, the piano). The CD reads it before each shoot day and each edit review.
- The reference stays in the room. The reference board is on the wall at the shoot and open on the edit's second monitor.

### 10.6 The CD on set and in the edit

On set, the director directs and the first assistant director (first AD) runs the floor (set roles: 14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, section 1). The CD's station is video village (the monitor position where agency and client watch takes), and the CD's job there is four things: check each set-up against the approved board and the idea sentence; confirm the cover (alternative takes, a clean plate (the set shot empty, for compositing), a version without dialogue, a vertical framing for social) before the crew moves on; speak to the director between takes, through the agency producer where the production company's etiquette requires it, and leave the crew to the first AD; and keep the client at video village informed and away from the director. The reason for the chain: a set with two directors produces neither director's film. One rule for luxury shoots: the CD watches the monitor with the sound off for one take of every set-up, because the still frame is what the house will print (6.1).

In the edit, the sequence runs assembly (every usable take in script order), rough cut, fine cut, picture lock (after which no frame changes), grade, sound mix and mastering (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, sections 4 and 5). The CD's three passes: the idea pass on the assembly (is the idea on screen, is the ending the ending), the rhythm pass on the rough cut (the music, the breath before the line, the length), and the polish pass on the fine cut (every transition, every title, the logo's frame count). The CD never notes polish on an assembly, because a polish note signals to the editor that the structure is settled before it is.

### 10.7 QA gates and sign-off

Each deliverable has a technical QA (the specs in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, the legal lines, the languages, the safe areas) run by the producer, and a creative QA run by the CD against the sign-off checklist in section 17. Nothing ships on the CD's verbal approval; the sign-off is a dated line in the job's record. Why the record: when a client asks in month six why the Arabic subtitle was wrong, the record shows who approved which version. The quality system behind it is in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 10.

### 10.8 Measurement, pre-testing and the post-launch review

- The baseline is captured before launch: the brief's success measure (2.1) at its current level, plus awareness and consideration where a tracker exists. Brand lift studies (Meta Brand Lift and YouTube Brand Lift: a platform's randomised test-and-control survey of ad recall and consideration) are booked before the media is bought, because the holdout groups are set at launch and each platform sets a minimum spend; the media team confirms the current minimum with the platform.
- The post-launch gate (10.1) runs twice: at 4 weeks on delivery and early signals, at 12 weeks on effects against the baseline. Its output is a one-page learning note, filed with the kill list (10.4) and the case library, written as what we would repeat and what we would change.
- Pre-testing is contested. System1's Test Your Ad predicts long-term potential as a Star rating from 1 to 5.9 and short-term sales potential as a Spike rating, both from the viewer's emotional response, and Orlando Wood ("Lemon", 6.1) argues for testing emotion; Kantar's LINK tests diagnostics (comprehension, branding, persuasion) for the client's committee; Kapferer and Bastien's anti-law 20 (8.1) says do not test luxury at all. House position: test comprehension and attention; taste is judged by the CD (11.6), and a luxury film is never scored by a panel.

### 10.9 Cultural risk review

Work touching religion, gender, caste, nation or community gets a pre-mortem (Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007) before PPM and again before launch. The team imagines the work has failed publicly a week after launch and writes down every way it failed: the reading a critic will give, the frame that will be screenshotted, the politician who will object. Each failure gets a fix in the work or a line in the response plan.

Agreed with the client in writing before launch: a holding statement (the first public response, drafted in advance); a named spokesperson; the conditions under which the work is withdrawn; protection for store staff and the client's people on the ground; and 72 hours of social listening after launch. The sensitivity review and the backlash decision tree are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 8.11.

The Indian precedents, all withdrawn after political or online pressure: Tanishq's "Ekatvam" and its Diwali film asking for no firecrackers (October and November 2020); Fabindia's "Jashn-e-Riwaaz" (October 2021); Dabur Fem's Karva Chauth film with a same-sex couple (October 2021); Sabyasachi's mangalsutra campaign (October 2021). The lesson is the one in 4.7: a stand the client will not hold under pressure is a stand the work should not take.

## 11. Presenting work

### 11.1 Bierut and the Pentagram method

Michael Bierut (Pentagram partner since 1990) has argued across "How to use graphic design to sell things, explain things, make things look better, make people laugh, make people cry, and (every once in a while) change the world" (2015) and his Design Observer essays, including "On (Design) Bullshit" (2005), for three things a CD can use directly: the presentation is a narrative that makes the solution feel inevitable, so the problem is told before the answer is shown; the rationale must be true, because a client can smell a reason invented after the design; and the designer's job in the room is to help the client see what the work will do for them, which means showing it in use before showing it in isolation. Pentagram partners differ on how many directions to show, which is the question in 11.3. Bierut's method in full is in 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md, section 2.10.

### 11.2 The setup and the reveal

Order of a creative presentation, with time for a 60 minute meeting:

| Minutes | Part | Content | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | The task | The brief in two sentences and the one number that matters | Read from the brief the client signed; this is the contract being honoured |
| 5 to 12 | The insight | What we learned and the tension we found (section 3) | The client should nod before the idea appears |
| 12 to 15 | The idea sentence | One slide, one sentence, nothing else on it | Silence for three seconds after reading it |
| 15 to 40 | The idea in the world | Film (animatic or ripomatic, 11.4), key visual, the social format, the space, the site, in the order the audience will meet them | One idea per slide; the why on the slide before the what |
| 40 to 45 | The sustain and the plan | How it runs for six months, the channel roles (6.8) | Shows the idea is a platform (4.2) |
| 45 to 50 | What it costs and when | Production estimate, timing, the decisions needed today | Money comes after the idea |
| 50 to 60 | Questions and the decision | The one question: "may we take this to PPM?" | Leave with a decision or a dated decision meeting |

One idea per slide is a rule about attention: a slide with the film frame, the headline and the social post asks the client to judge three things and they judge the weakest. The why before the what is a rule about ownership: a client who agrees with the reason has already half-agreed with the work.

### 11.3 One direction or three

The positions: the classic agency method shows three routes (safe, recommended, wild) because it lets the client choose and protects the agency when the choice fails; the design-studio method, which Paula Scher (28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md, section 2.9) and several Pentagram partners are associated with, shows one because the studio is paid for judgement and three routes invite the client to assemble a fourth from the parts. The house position: one recommended route presented whole, with one alternative held in reserve and shown only if the recommendation is refused on a reason the CD accepts. Three routes are shown only at the tissue session (10.1), where choice is the point. The reason: Mask360 sells taste, and a client who is handed three answers has been told the studio does not know which one is right.

### 11.4 Prototypes and films over boards

Boards (printed layouts and storyboards) ask the client to imagine; a prototype removes the imagining. For film, present an animatic (the storyboard frames cut to the chosen music with a scratch voice) or a ripomatic (existing footage from references cut to the script to show pace and tone, kept inside the room because the footage is unlicensed). For a site, present a clickable prototype in Figma or a built page (21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md). For a space, present a 3D walk-through or a scale model. For an identity, present the mark on the five surfaces that matter (18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 4.15) before showing it alone. The reason: every argument about "I cannot picture it" is an argument the CD loses, and a prototype costs less than a lost round.

### 11.5 Reading the room

Who speaks first after the reveal tells the CD who decides. If the decision maker looks at a subordinate before reacting, the subordinate is the real approver. A client who asks about cost before the idea has finished is signalling a budget problem the account lead did not surface. Silence after the idea sentence is good; silence after the film is bad. The CD watches the room during the film; the screen has already been seen a hundred times. When a founder's family member is in the room (12.9), their reaction is weighed as the founder's.

### 11.6 Handling objections

| Objection | What it usually means | Response |
|---|---|---|
| "Make the logo bigger" | The client does not feel ownership of the work | Show the work covered with the logo removed (design-taste, section 4, step 7): if it is unmistakably theirs, the logo is big enough; then show a competitor's ad with a large logo and ask which looks more expensive |
| "Can we see it in blue" | They want to participate in the making | Show it in blue in the meeting if the tool allows, side by side, and let them see; the comparison usually settles it. If it does not, ask what the blue was meant to fix |
| "My wife does not like it" | A trusted outsider has been shown work without the setup | Offer to present to the wife, with the setup. Work seen without its reason is judged as decoration |
| "Can we have the film from route A with the line from route B" | Three routes were shown (11.3) | Explain why the parts belong to different ideas, and offer to develop the preferred idea fully instead |
| "It is too simple" | They expected visible effort | Show the reference board and the four hundred hours test (design-taste, section 4, step 7); show the category's cluttered norm next to it |
| "It needs to pop" | The work is not loud enough for the person who will judge them internally | Ask who that person is and what they will measure, and answer that measure with the plan |
| "Our competitor did something like this" | Fear of looking second | Show the archive search (4.4); if it is true, the route is dead and the CD says so first |
| "Add a tagline" | The idea sentence has not reached them | Say the idea sentence again; if it needs a tagline to be understood, the work has failed and the CD says so |
| "Can we test it" | Risk transfer | Agree to test the comprehension (do people get it) and refuse to test the taste (do people like it); explain anti-law 20 (8.1) for luxury clients (10.8) |
| "Put me in the film" (the founder) | The founder is the brand in their own mind, and often in the market's | Do it when the founder is the proof (the maker, the family that still lives in the palace); otherwise offer a founder's letter or a founder film for owned channels |
| "Show all 25 pieces" | Every piece has an internal champion | One hero piece per film; the collection lives in the lookbook and on the site (8.4) |
| "Add the offer, the EMI, the price" | Sales pressure on the quarter (an EMI is an equated monthly instalment) | It belongs in the activation layer (4.5, 6.9) and stays out of the hero |
| "Make it viral" | A wish for reach without the media budget | Ask what measure "viral" stands for, then answer that measure with the plan (6.8) |
| "The astrologer has changed the date" | The muhurat moved (12.9) | Rebuild the schedule backward from the new date and say in writing what will be cut to meet it |
| "Can we use the competitor's ambassador" | Fame by association | Run the test in 8.5: would the house photograph this person without the product, and what does the audience already link them to? |

### 11.7 The client chair exercise and the written rationale

The client chair exercise: the day before a presentation, the CD sits in the client's seat in the meeting room and writes the five questions the client's own boss, board or spouse will ask them after the meeting. The deck is then re-ordered so each question is answered before it is asked. The exercise exists because the client is the person who has to re-present the work to someone who was not in the room.

The written rationale: one page, sent within 24 hours of the meeting, in four parts: the task as briefed, the insight, the idea sentence, why this execution and no other. It is written in the house copy system (mask360-copywriting). Its purpose is to be forwarded: it is the document the client's boss reads instead of the deck.

### 11.8 Presenting remotely

Rules for a video call: the films are downloaded locally and shared as a full-screen window, because a film streamed through the call's screen share is at the mercy of the connection; cameras on for everyone who will give an opinion; the CD presents from a second device showing the clients' faces, because the room (11.5) is read in the faces; nothing is sent in advance except the agenda, because work sent ahead is judged without its setup; 20 minutes per route, then a break; the decision question is asked on the call and the written rationale follows within the hour. Record with permission so the absent decision maker sees the setup and the reveal in order.

### 11.9 Pitch theatre and the house deck

Agency pitch theatre (its cost and economics are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 5.4): the branded room, the reveal behind a curtain, the mock-ups on every wall, the chemistry video, the surprise gift with the client's name. It works on procurement-led pitches where the client is choosing an agency's energy. The house declines it. Mask360 decks follow the house deck grammar in design-taste, section 7. The reason: a luxury client judges the agency by the agency's own restraint, and a pitch that shouts has told them what the work will do.

## 12. Client management for creative directors

The commercial side of client management (onboarding, stakeholder maps, rounds in the contract, churn) is in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 8; this section is the CD's side.

### 12.1 Expectation setting

The first meeting with a new client sets five expectations in writing, in a one-page "how we work" note sent after it: who decides on each side (12.4); how many rounds the fee includes (12.2); that feedback arrives consolidated and in writing (12.3); that the studio shows one recommended route (11.3); and that the studio will say no, with reasons, when a request would damage the work. Clients who refuse the note in the first week are clients who will refuse it in week ten at a higher cost.

### 12.2 Rounds and scope

| Stage | Rounds included | Working days for client feedback | What a round is | Beyond the rounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Territories | 1 | 5 | One consolidated written response after the tissue session (10.1) | A new territory round is a new fee |
| Concept | 2 | 5 | Changes within the approved concept | A new concept is a new brief and a new fee |
| Craft (script, layout, key visual) | 2 | 5 | Line edits, crops, type, colour within the concept | Day rate per 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3 |
| Edit | 2 after rough cut, 1 after fine cut | 3 | Notes on structure then on polish | Re-opening picture lock is charged at the post house's rate plus the studio's |
| Final files | 1 (technical corrections) | 2 | Typos, legal lines, specs | Creative change after sign-off restarts the craft stage |

Five working days is the contract standard (rounds policy: 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, sections 3.4 and 8.3); the edit and final-file windows are shorter because the post house is booked by the day. A round is counted when a consolidated response arrives, whatever its length. The reason for counting: unlimited rounds price the studio's judgement at zero and teach the client to postpone deciding.

### 12.3 Feedback consolidation

One voice: the client appoints one person who collects internal opinions, resolves conflicts among them and sends one written response per round within the window. The studio refuses to arbitrate between the marketing head and the founder's daughter; that is the client's job and doing it for them makes the studio the enemy of whoever lost. Feedback received on WhatsApp, by phone or in corridors is acknowledged and then requested in the consolidated form. The feedback format the house sends with every presentation has four headings: what works, what does not work and why, what must change (mandatory), what we would like changed (optional). The "why" column exists because "we do not like the blue" is useless and "the blue reads as a bank" can be acted on.

### 12.4 Decision makers and approvers

At kickoff the studio asks one question and writes the answer into the brief: who signs the concept, who signs the PPM book, who signs the final, and who can veto any of them. A client who answers "the committee" is told that committees do not sign films and asked for the name. Late joiners (the board member who appears at the craft review) are briefed from the signed documents and asked to accept the decisions already signed; if they cannot, the clock restarts at the stage they contest and the fee follows (18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 5.1 applies the same rule to identity work).

### 12.5 Educating taste without condescension

Taste is taught by comparison. The methods that work: the reference walk (an hour in the client's own category looking at what the best houses do, with the client, before any work is shown); pairs (the house's reference next to the category trope, no commentary, let them see); the field trip (lunch at the hotel whose restraint the studio is proposing; the jewellery house whose windows say nothing); and the delayed verdict (show the work, ask them to live with it for 48 hours before responding, because first reactions to restraint are fear). Words that condescend and are banned in client rooms: "trust us", "this is what luxury looks like", "your customers will not notice". The CD explains the reason behind every choice as if to an intelligent person from another profession, which the client is.

### 12.6 When to push back and how

The ladder, climbed one rung at a time:

1. Ask for the reason behind the request. Half of the requests dissolve when the reason is stated aloud.
2. Offer the test. "Let us put both on the wall and look." Comparison beats argument.
3. State the cost. "We can do that; it will cost the idea, and here is the reason." Say it once, plainly.
4. Record the disagreement. In the written rationale: "The studio recommended X; the client chose Y." The record exists for the post-mortem and for the next brief.
5. Commit. Once recorded, the studio makes Y as well as it would have made X. Sulking in the execution is unprofessional and visible.

The CD refuses outright, and walks away if necessary, on three things: work that is unlawful (section 16), work that would put the house's name on something the house would not show, and work that misrepresents the product. Everything else is a negotiation.

### 12.7 The long relationship

A client kept for five years is worth more than five clients kept for a year, and the creative work improves with every year because the brief gets shorter. The rituals: a quarterly review of everything shipped against the brand's codes (8.2); an annual taste review where the CD shows the client what has changed in the category and in the culture (design-taste, references/currency-2026.md) and what the house will do about it; a yearly brand film or lookbook that is the studio's own standard-setting piece for that client; and the honesty to tell a client when the studio is no longer the right one for a task.

### 12.8 The difficult client

| Type | Behaviour | Response |
|---|---|---|
| The committee | Feedback from six people, contradictory | 12.3; refuse to proceed without one voice |
| The ghost | Silent for weeks, then urgent | The feedback window (12.2) with the timeline shifting day for day; written notice when the window closes |
| The late joiner | A senior arrives at craft and reopens concept | 12.4; brief from signed documents; restart the clock and the fee if needed |
| The designer | Sends their own layouts | Treat them as a brief; extract the intent, show the house answer to it |
| The price shopper | Every round is a negotiation | Pricing in writing before each stage; no work starts on an unsigned estimate |
| The scope creeper | "While you are at it" | The change request form (a one-page record of an addition's scope, price and date, signed before work starts): every addition priced and dated, even at zero |
| The abuser | Shouting, late-night demands, humiliating staff | One warning in writing from the account lead, then termination; no fee is worth the team |

### 12.9 The founder client in India and the Gulf

Patterns the house has met and plans for, stated as patterns with no claim about any individual:

- The family in the room. A founder's spouse, son or daughter often holds a veto that is not on the organisation chart. Ask at kickoff who else will see the work at home, and offer to present to them with the setup (11.6).
- The returning generation. The child who studied in London or New York is often the modernising voice and the studio's ally; they are also the person most likely to send references from brands with ten times the budget. Translate the reference to the budget early.
- Feedback by WhatsApp at 23:00. Acknowledge, then consolidate (12.3). Set the expectation in the first week that the studio reads WhatsApp in office hours.
- The auspicious date. Launches in India are set by the muhurat (the astrologically chosen time) and the festival calendar (Diwali, Akshaya Tritiya for gold, Dussehra, the wedding season from October to February, Eid for Muslim clients). Build the production schedule backward from the fixed date and refuse to move the shoot into the monsoon for a date set in June.
- The Gulf decision pattern. Decisions can be fast at the top (a single yes from the owner) and slow below it (procurement, the government-linked client's protocol office, the sheikh's office for a royal or semi-government client). Get the top yes in writing and plan three weeks for the paperwork beneath it. Ramadan reduces working hours by law in the UAE private sector (two hours a day) and slows every approval (6.13); the Dubai summer (June to September) empties decision makers' offices.
- The price conversation. In both markets the founder negotiates personally and sees negotiation as respect. Price the scope in stages (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3) so the negotiation is about which stages are bought.
- Language and script. Confirm at kickoff whether the work is English only, English with Hindi or Arabic, or script-first; a founder who approves an English film and then asks for a Hindi version in week 13 has asked for a second film (10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md covers the bilingual craft).

## 13. Team leadership

### 13.1 Hiring for taste

The portfolio review method, scored out of 100 and written down so hiring decisions can be compared across the year (the 15-minute read, questions and red flags are in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md, section 11):

| Criterion | Weight | What the reviewer looks for |
|---|---|---|
| The idea | 35 | Can each piece be described in one sentence by the reviewer without the candidate's help? |
| The craft | 25 | Type, crop, pace, finish at the level of the house references (design-taste, references/canon.md) |
| The choice | 20 | What the candidate chose to show and leave out; a portfolio of six is a stronger signal than one of thirty |
| The references | 10 | Asked "what do you look at", the candidate names specific people and works, with reasons |
| The account of the process | 10 | The candidate can say what was killed and why; "the client changed it" with no further detail is a red flag |

The test: a paid half-day brief (a real past brief with the client's name removed), three hours, one idea sentence and one execution, presented in ten minutes. The test measures the idea and the presentation; the portfolio has already measured the craft. The trial: two to four weeks paid, on live work, with one named reviewer, a standards document (13.3) on day one, and a written review at the end against the same five criteria. Hiring on an interview alone is banned because an interview measures confidence, and the job is judged on ideas and craft, which the test and the trial measure.

### 13.2 Developing designers and writers

- The crit (10.2), weekly, with the junior presenting their own work and taking notes themselves.
- The reference habit. One hour a week, logged, of looking at or reading things outside the current job, with three references a week posted to the studio board, each with the line "what is borrowed" (design-taste, section 4, step 1). The CD reviews the board monthly; a junior whose references are all from Instagram is sent to the library, the cinema and the street.
- Copywork. Writers retype great copy by hand (David Abbott's Economist lines, Bill Bernbach's Volkswagen pages, the Hermès annual theme copy) to feel the sentence lengths; designers redraw great layouts (Helmut Krone's "Think Small" page, a Bottega lookbook spread, an Aesop store window) to feel the proportions. The method is the painter's copy in the museum and it works for the same reason: the hand learns what the eye skims.
- The one craft at specialist level. Every creative is expected to hold one craft (type, image, film, writing, motion) at the level where a specialist would hire them, and to be fluent in the rest. The 360 CD (1.3) is the person who has done this twice.
- Rotation. Every creative spends two weeks a year on a production (a shoot, an edit, a print run) in a role below their title, because the craft of direction is learned by watching the craft being executed.

### 13.3 Delegation without dilution

The standards document is the instrument that lets a CD delegate without the standard falling. One per job, two pages, written by the CD before any execution starts:

1. The brief's objective and the idea sentence.
2. The reference board with "what is borrowed" per reference.
3. The trash list: what we are not doing (design-taste, section 4, step 3).
4. The tokens: typeface and setting, palette, margin, the one motion, the image treatment (design-taste, section 4, step 4; 03-typography.md and 04-colour.md for the craft).
5. The tests the work must pass before the CD sees it (design-taste, section 4, step 7, plus the job's own).
6. Three examples of "done" from the house's own past work or the canon.

The first 10 percent and the last 10 percent are the CD's own hands: the first board, the first sketch of the key visual, the first draft of the idea sentence, and then the last pass on the edit, the final crop, the last read of every line. The middle 80 percent is delegated against the standards document. The reason for the split: the first 10 percent sets the direction nobody else can set, and the last 10 percent is where the difference between good and unmistakable lives.

### 13.4 Feedback vocabulary

| Say | Instead of | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The idea is on the third frame; move it to the first" | "It is slow" | Locates the problem |
| "This line could sit under any hotel; give me the one fact only this house has" | "Make it more premium" | Names the test that failed |
| "Which reference is this borrowing from, and what did you borrow" | "I do not like it" | Returns to evidence |
| "Remove until it breaks, then put one thing back" | "Simplify" | Gives the method (design-taste, section 4, step 6) |
| "Show me the sound-off version" | "Is it clear" | Tests the frame on its own |
| "What did you kill to get here" | "Explore more" | Makes volume visible and respects the work done |
| "This is finished; ship it" | "Fine" | Finished must be said, because juniors cannot tell |

The banned feedback: "make it pop", "more premium", "jazz it up", "I will know it when I see it". Each names a feeling and leaves the task unstated (feedback by discipline: 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md, section 6).

### 13.5 Freelancers and production partners

Rules: the standards document (13.3) and the brief in writing before the day rate is agreed; the rate, the usage (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7) and the credit agreed in one email before work starts; a named studio owner for every freelancer who reviews at 25 percent and 75 percent of the time booked; source files and fonts licensed to the studio or the client, because a licence held by the freelancer alone leaves the client unable to use their own work; and a vendor record after every job with a quality score and a reliability note, because the memory of who was good is the studio's real asset. Retouchers, 3D artists and editors are briefed with reference frames and the trash list.

### 13.6 A studio culture of craft

Rituals that keep the standard without a speech about standards: Friday show-and-tell, 30 minutes, everyone shows one thing made this week; "the best thing I saw", one slide per person per month; the reference wall, physical, in the room where work is reviewed; the library (books on paper, lookbooks, annuals, the D&AD and Lürzer's archives) with a budget line; the house canon (design-taste, references/canon.md) read by every joiner in week one; and the rule that the CD's own work is critiqued in the same crit as the juniors'.

### 13.7 Burnout and pace

House rules, stated because a studio that ships luxury work on exhausted people ships the exhaustion: no shoot day over 12 hours and at least 11 hours between wrap and the next call; no more than two concept presentations per pair per week; a presentation day is followed by a day with no deadlines for the team that presented; the CD's own week (1.4) includes reference time, and a CD with no reference time within a month is a CD whose taste is running on memory. Crunch is permitted for a launch and is paid back in time within the month. Why these numbers: the eleventh hour of a shoot day produces the take that is reshot; the second concept presentation in a week is the one that recycles the first.

### 13.8 The CD as editor

The editing discipline is in design-taste, section 5, and the remove-until-it-breaks test in design-taste, section 4, step 6. A campaign adds two rules. The team's favourite shot is cut when it does not serve the idea, and the CD cuts it, because nobody else on the job has the standing. The strongest execution goes first or last in every sequence (the deck, the lookbook, the launch week), because the middle is where things are forgotten.

### 14.2 Estimates

A production estimate is built bottom-up by line and read top-down by percentage. The lines: pre-production (treatment, casting, location scouting, art department prep), shoot (crew, equipment, studio or location, catering, transport), talent (fees and usage), post (edit, grade, sound, VFX, versions), music (sync and master, or composition), travel, insurance and permits, contingency, the production company's fee. The reading:

- Contingency: 10 percent of direct costs, held by the producer, released only by the CD and the client together. Below 10 percent a weather day or a reshoot comes out of the fee.
- Production company fee: in bids the house has seen in India and the Gulf, a fee of about 15 to 25 percent on direct costs is the usual structure; treat as market knowledge as of October 2026 and verify on each bid.
- Talent usage: priced separately from the shoot fee, always, on the five factors in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7 (term, territory, media, exclusivity, prominence).
- The three-bid rule for any film over the studio's stated threshold: three production companies bid on the same treatment and the same specification sheet, so the bids are comparable. A bid that is 30 percent below the others has misread the treatment or will recover the difference in overages.
- Overages (costs beyond the approved estimate) need the client's written approval before they are incurred, except for safety.

### 14.4 Producers

Three producers are on any film: the agency producer (the studio's own, who owns the estimate, the schedule, the bids, the rights and the production bible); the production company's line producer (who runs the shoot's money and crew day by day); and the executive producer (EP) at the production company, who owns the bid and the relationship with the director. The CD briefs the agency producer and talks to the director; the agency producer talks to the line producer and the EP. Money crosses the line only through producers.

### 14.5 Usage rights and buyouts

The numbers for talent, photography, film, voice, music and illustration in India and the GCC (Gulf Cooperation Council) states are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7, which this file does not repeat. The CD's rules, in order of the trouble they prevent:

1. No frame is shot without the release (the signed consent to be photographed and the usage terms) in the producer's hand. A release signed after the shoot is a negotiation.
2. Music needs two licences: the synchronisation licence for the composition (from the publisher) and the master licence for the recording (from the label); a cover version needs only the first (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 12.2). The famous-track trap: a reference cut to a well-known song that the client falls in love with before the price is known. Price the track at the ripomatic stage or use a composer.
3. Term, territory and media are written on the call sheet for every piece of talent, so that the producer can answer "can we use this in the Gulf in year two" in a minute.
4. In India, the Copyright Act 1957 governs; moral rights (section 57) stay with the author even after assignment, and performers' rights are separate. In the UAE, Federal Decree-Law No. 38 of 2021 on Copyrights and Neighbouring Rights governs. Both markets: an assignment of copyright must be in writing.
5. Generative material: the model's terms, the training-data risk and the disclosure duty (section 16.5; 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md) are checked before an AI-made element enters a deliverable, and the client is told which elements are synthetic.
6. Fonts are licensed to the entity that uses them (the studio for its work, the client for theirs); a desktop licence does not cover a website or an app (03-typography.md).
7. Stock and library assets are checked for editorial-only restrictions; a luxury client's campaign on an editorial-only image is a claim waiting to happen.

### 14.6 Insurance and permits

Production insurance covers equipment, the recorded media, public liability, weather (for exterior shoots with a fixed date), non-appearance of a named talent, and, for broadcast, errors and omissions (E&O, which covers claims arising from the content itself). The producer confirms the production company's cover and the studio's own before the first call sheet. Permits as of October 2026, to be confirmed on each job: in Dubai, filming in public spaces runs through the Dubai Film and TV Commission's permit system; in India, filming at protected monuments needs the Archaeological Survey of India's permission, state film cells and police permissions cover streets, and foreign crews use the Film Facilitation Office under the National Film Development Corporation (NFDC); drones need registration with the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) on the Digital Sky platform under the Drone Rules 2021 in India, and in the UAE registration with the General Civil Aviation Authority plus the emirate's permit (the Dubai Civil Aviation Authority in Dubai). Lead times for permits run from days to six weeks; the producer files on the day the location is chosen (detail and drone rules: 14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, sections 4.2 to 4.6).

Heat: the UAE's midday break bans outdoor work from 12:30 to 15:00 between 15 June and 15 September (Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation), and Saudi Arabia applies a similar summer midday ban; exterior shoots in those months run at dawn and in the evening, which also suits the light. Incentives: Abu Dhabi's Creative Media Authority advertises a cash rebate of 35 percent on qualifying production spend and the Saudi Film Commission up to 40 percent; check whether a commercial qualifies before the budget assumes it (14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, section 4.4). Carbon: the AdGreen carbon calculator measures a shoot's footprint and AdNetZero sets the industry's reduction framework; the house runs the calculator on every shoot with flights.

### 14.7 Approvals and the PPM book

The PPM book is the document the client signs at the PPM approval gate (10.1): treatment (14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, section 3.1), storyboards or shot list, cast with photographs, locations with photographs, wardrobe, props, art direction references, the schedule, the budget, the usage terms, and the delivery list. The approval chain for every page: director proposes, agency producer checks against the estimate, CD approves against the idea, client signs. A change after signature is logged in the departure log (10.5) with its cost. For stills and print the equivalent is the shot list and the paper dummy; for a space it is the drawing set and the sample board.

### 14.8 The production bible

One folder, maintained by the agency producer, that a stranger could use to deliver the job: the signed brief and brief back; the idea sentence and the standards document (13.3); the PPM book and the departure log; every release, licence and usage term with its expiry date in a single table; the call sheets; the delivery specification per asset; the colour reference frames and the sound reference; the vendor contacts and rates; the approvals record with dates; the asset register with file names per the house convention. The expiry table is the part that earns its keep: it is the reason a client is told in month 11 that the hero image's usage ends in month 12.

### 15.2 What juries reward

Across shows, the patterns that win: an idea that can be told in the case film's first 20 seconds; a cultural or product truth that the viewer did not know; craft that is visible at a glance (a jury sees 200 entries a day and stops on the one frame that looks like four hundred hours); a result stated as a number with a baseline; and work that is on brand, because juries of CDs penalise ideas that any brand could have run. The patterns that lose: a case film longer than its idea, a stunt with no brand reason, a claim of results without a baseline, and craft categories entered with work whose only strength is the idea.

### 15.3 The case film: how to read one and how to make one

Reading one: watch the first 20 seconds with the sound off and ask whether the idea is visible; listen for the baseline whenever a percentage appears; look for the date and the duration of the campaign; note whether the results come from the brand, the agency or a third party (Nielsen, a platform's own report, a research firm); and check whether the thing shown ran at the scale claimed (the one billboard filmed from five angles is a known tell).

Making one (the case film budget and the house case format are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 12.4), in the structure most juries have learned to expect, about two minutes long (Cannes Lions and D&AD both cap case films at around two minutes in recent years; confirm against the year's entry kit):

| Seconds | Part | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 15 | The problem, with one number | No logo animation, no agency name |
| 15 to 30 | The insight | One sentence on screen |
| 30 to 50 | The idea | Said in one sentence, then shown |
| 50 to 90 | The execution across channels | Real footage of the work in the world, dated |
| 90 to 115 | The results | Numbers with baselines and sources; earned media as reach with a source; "impressions" alone fails the jury |
| 115 to 120 | The end | The idea sentence again; the brand; no claims |

Rules for honesty: results come with sources on screen; nothing is shown that did not run; the case film is written by the strategist and cut by the editor, and the CD approves it against the same standard as the work, because juries are CDs and read a cheap case film as a cheap campaign.

### 15.4 Awards against effectiveness

The tension: Peter Field's "The Crisis in Creative Effectiveness" (IPA, 2019) found, across about 600 IPA Databank cases from 1996 to 2018, that creatively awarded campaigns had lost the effectiveness advantage they once held, and attributed it chiefly to short-termism and a misunderstanding of how brands grow. The IPA's and Binet and Field's broader finding stands (4.5): creativity that builds fame and distinctive assets over the long term is the most efficient driver of growth. James Hurman and Peter Field's "The Effectiveness Code" (Cannes Lions and WARC, 2020) sets out the Creative Effectiveness Ladder, six levels a CD can use to rate any case: influential idea, behaviour breakthrough, sales spike, brand builder, commercial triumph, enduring icon. The house position: enter awards with work that ran, at scale, for a client, with results the client will sign; refuse scam ads (work made for awards and run once or never); and read the Effie's results weighting and the Creative Effectiveness Lion as the standard the house's case films are written to.

## 16. Ethics and responsibility

### 16.1 Representation and casting

Cast for the audience that exists and the audience the brand wants, with the range of skin tones, ages, bodies and faces that the market holds. In Indian beauty and fashion the default has been fair, slim and young, which is why the Advertising Standards Council of India's (ASCI) guidelines on skin-lightening advertising (2014) prohibit depicting people with darker skin as unattractive, unsuccessful or inferior, and why Unilever renamed Fair & Lovely to Glow & Lovely in 2020. ASCI's Guidelines on Harmful Gender Stereotypes (June 2022), which followed its GenderNext study with Futurebrands (2021), bar portrayals that tie a person's worth or role to their gender, such as the woman who exists only to serve the family or the man mocked for caring; every casting brief and script is read against them. For the Gulf, casting for a campaign that will run across the UAE and Saudi Arabia is checked against each market's standards on dress and depiction before the shoot, because the edit cannot fix casting. The CD's casting rule: the person in the frame is someone the brand's buyer would recognise as themselves at their best, and the casting brief states the range explicitly so the agency cannot drift to the default.

### 16.2 Cultural borrowing

The line between borrowing and taking: permission, fluency and credit. Loewe's Studio Ghibli collaborations (2021 to 2023) were licensed, made with the studio and credited. Dior's 2019 Sauvage film with Native American imagery was withdrawn after criticism; Gucci's 2019 turban headpiece drew the same. For India and the Gulf the house works with craft and cultural partners in the frame and in the credits: Dior's Pre-Fall 2023 show at the Gateway of India with the Chanakya School of Craft in Mumbai is the model of a house crediting the hands. Rules: a native speaker reads every line in Hindi, Arabic or any regional language; a religious symbol, a sacred site or a ritual is used only with the community's own advisers; and the craft shown is credited by name of the maker or the workshop.

### 16.3 Greenwashing

Greenwashing is an environmental claim made without verifiable evidence. A claim about environmental benefit needs the same substantiation as a claim about price. In India, ASCI's guidelines for advertisements making environmental or green claims took effect on 15 February 2024, and the Central Consumer Protection Authority's Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Greenwashing or Misleading Environmental Claims were notified in October 2024; both require that terms such as "sustainable", "eco-friendly" and "carbon neutral" be backed by verifiable evidence, with qualifiers and disclosures. The UK Competition and Markets Authority's Green Claims Code (2021) is the reference text the house uses for the six principles (truthful, clear, no omissions, fair comparisons, whole life cycle, substantiated). Rule: no green claim appears in a Mask360 deliverable without the evidence file in the production bible (14.8).

### 16.4 Body image in beauty

Retouching that changes body shape or skin colour is refused; retouching that removes a temporary blemish is allowed and logged. The disclosure norms in some markets (France has required a "photographie retouchée" label on commercial images with retouched bodies since 2017) are followed as house policy in India and the Gulf even where the law does not require them, because the beauty and jewellery clients the house serves sell to the people most affected. Dove's 2004 research (section 9.1) is the reason this is a commercial question as well as an ethical one.

### 16.5 AI disclosure

ASCI issued its Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising on 29 September 2026 (position as of October 2026), after a draft in May 2026 that set out a three-tier, risk-based model: high-risk uses prohibited (fabricated endorsements, exaggerated product performance, deepfakes without consent), medium-risk uses requiring a label (such as "Audio/Video created using AI" or "enhanced using AI") where the synthetic content materially influences the consumer's decision, and low-risk uses requiring none. The draft's comment window closed on 13 June 2026; the final text renamed "AI-generated" to "synthetically generated content", added a three-question test for whether a label is needed, and added a separate category for accessibility uses such as captions and translations (per Fox Mandal). The guidelines apply three months after publication: Khaitan & Co reads that as 29 December 2026, exchange4media reports 1 January 2027; confirm on ascionline.in. Disclosure is no safe harbour: a labelled synthetic film that makes a false claim still breaches the Code. The craft side (which elements may be synthetic, the tells, provenance marks such as Google's SynthID) is in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, with the law in its section 7. House rule: the client is told in writing which elements are synthetic, and no synthetic likeness of a real person is used without that person's written consent.

### 16.6 The ASCI Code and UAE advertising standards

The ASCI Code (Advertising Standards Council of India, self-regulatory, with complaints handled by its Consumer Complaints Council) has four chapters: I, truthful and honest representation (claims, substantiation, disclaimers, social media disclosure labels; chapter dated 10 July 2025); II, non-offensive to the public (decency, the depiction of women); III, against harmful products, services and situations (children, dangerous practices, banned products, circumvention of restrictions; last updated 29 April 2024); IV, fair in competition (comparative advertising, imitation). The Consumer Protection Act 2019 and the CCPA's Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022, give the statutory layer beneath it, with penalties for misleading advertisements and for endorsers.

In the UAE, advertising is regulated under Federal Decree-Law No. 55 of 2023 on Media Regulation, with penalties set by Cabinet Resolution No. 42 of 2025 (as cited by Gulf News). The UAE Media Council administered it until its functions passed to the National Media Authority in December 2025 (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 14.7). The Advertiser Permit, announced on 30 July 2025 with a three-month grace period that was then extended to 31 January 2026 (Gulf News gives 1 February 2026), applies to any individual publishing promotional content on social media, paid or unpaid. It is free for the first three years; people promoting their own business from a personal account are exempt, as are minors under 18 in educational, cultural, sports or awareness activities; visiting creators need a visitor permit valid for three months and renewable once, obtained through a council-licensed advertising or talent agency; Gulf News reports AED 10,000 for a first offence of advertising without a permit and AED 40,000 for a repeat, with content breaches fined AED 5,000 to AED 1,000,000. Applications run through uaemc.gov.ae. Content standards require respect for religion, the state and public morals, and prohibit advertising of alcohol and tobacco to the public. The house checks every influencer and creator engagement in the UAE for a valid permit before the brief is sent, and every Saudi engagement against the rules of the General Authority for Media Regulation, including the Mawthooq licence (6.10). The contract and legal summary is in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 14.

### 16.7 Children

ASCI defines children as under 12 (Code definition 7) and Chapter III sets the rules: no advertisement may show children in dangerous situations, encourage them to pester, or exploit their credulity. The CCPA's 2022 guidelines add statutory rules on advertisements targeting children, including on claims about health and on endorsements. For a hospitality or F&B client, the practical rules: no child is cast without a guardian's written consent and presence on set; shoot days for children are shorter and scheduled around school; nothing that implies a product makes a child more popular or a parent better.

### 16.8 Alcohol and surrogate advertising in India

Direct advertising of alcohol and tobacco on cable television is prohibited under the Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994; the 2009 amendment permits advertising of a genuine brand extension only if the advertisement shows only the extension product, makes no reference to the restricted product and avoids its packaging, colours and visuals. The Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act, 2003 (COTPA) nearly bans tobacco advertising. Surrogate advertising (an advertisement that appears to promote a permitted product in order to promote a restricted one) received its first legal definition in the CCPA's 2022 guidelines. ASCI's test for a genuine brand extension has used commercial thresholds (reported as at least 10 percent in-store availability of the category leader, or annual turnover above Rs 5 crore nationally or Rs 1 crore in the state of distribution (a crore is 10 million rupees); verify the current figures with ASCI). In 2024 the CCPA sought three years of brand-extension and sales data from liquor marketers, and Reuters reported a draft tightening with fines of up to Rs 50 lakh and endorsement bans of one to three years; as of October 2026 no finalised rule was found. The Delhi High Court's TV Today Network v. Union of India (2021) on the "All Seasons" club soda trade dress is the case to read. House rule: Mask360 does not make surrogate advertising; a premium spirits client is served through legal channels (on-premise, events over 25 years of age, packaging, the brand's own channels where the state permits) and the extension product must exist at the scale of the campaign.

### 16.9 Property advertising

- India: section 11(2) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 requires every advertisement or prospectus for a registered project to show prominently the state RERA authority's website address and the project's registration number; an unregistered project may not be advertised. MahaRERA in Maharashtra has also required a QR code on project advertisements since 2023 (20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md, section 7.2; confirm the current format before artwork).
- Dubai: every property advertisement, social posts included, needs an advertising permit from the Dubai Land Department's Trakheesi system, and the permit number appears on the ad. In Abu Dhabi the regulator is ADREC, the Abu Dhabi Real Estate Centre.
- Both markets: CGI is captioned as an artist's impression (9.7); stated areas follow the market's convention (carpet area in India); yield, return and payment-plan claims carry written substantiation in the production bible (14.8).

### 16.10 Dark patterns and scarcity claims

The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) notified the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 on 30 November 2023. They name 13 patterns: false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing and rogue malware. The drop model in 8.5 lives next to the first of them. Rules: every scarcity or urgency claim ("25 pieces", "ends Sunday", a countdown timer, "only 3 left") has an evidence file in the production bible (14.8) showing it is true while the ad runs; a microsite shows the full price, taxes and charges included, at the first mention, because drip pricing is on the list; and a paid creator post carries its label (6.10), because disguised advertisement is too. The interface craft is in 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md.

### 16.11 Data and personalisation

Personalised and interactive mechanics (a Wrapped-style page, a drawing mechanic like Heinz's, AR try-on, a microsite that takes a name, user-generated content) collect personal data.

- India: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, with the DPDP Rules 2025 notified in November 2025 and coming into force in phases to about May 2027; notice and consent before collection, and verifiable parental consent for anyone under 18.
- UAE: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the Protection of Personal Data (PDPL), in force since 2 January 2022.
- Saudi Arabia: the Personal Data Protection Law, in force from 14 September 2023 and enforced from 14 September 2024.

Rule: any mechanic that collects faces, voices, user-generated content or contact data has a consent notice and a data map (what is collected, where it is stored, who sees it, when it is deleted) approved by the client's counsel before build. Contracts and the processor role are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 14.8.

### 16.12 National symbols, maps and rulers' images

- India: the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act 1950 bars commercial use of the names and emblems in its schedule, including the national flag; the State Emblem of India (Prohibition of Improper Use) Act 2005 protects the state emblem; the Flag Code of India 2002 governs how the flag is shown. Maps of India show the official boundaries published by the Survey of India; a map that alters them is withdrawn and exposes the publisher to prosecution.
- UAE: the flag, the national emblem and any image of the rulers or members of the ruling families are used only with permission and within the media content standards (16.6); National Day work is checked for this before the shoot.
- Saudi Arabia: the flag carries the shahada (the Islamic declaration of faith) and is kept off products and anything that may be discarded; the flag and images of the leadership fall under the General Authority for Media Regulation's content rules.

These are among the commonest reasons festive and national-day work is pulled after launch, so the cultural risk review (10.9) checks them.

## 17. The creative director's checklist before anything ships

Idea

1. The single idea sentence is on the first page of the file and the work still matches it.
2. The idea test (4.4) passes all four questions today, with the archive search dated.
3. Cover the logo: it is unmistakably this client's (design-taste, section 4, step 7).
4. The social version and the spatial version exist and carry the same idea (4.3).
5. The sustain is planned and funded before the hero goes live (6.8).
6. The baseline is captured and brand lift is booked before launch (10.8).
7. For work touching religion, gender, caste, nation or community: the pre-mortem is done and the response plan is signed by the client (10.9).

Craft

8. Sound off: the first frame and the last frame say the idea.
9. Every frame the house would print (6.1); every line a confident person would say (7.3).
10. The reference board was open during the last pass and the work does not copy any reference whole.
11. The "what would make this generic" list (10.5) has been read against the final.
12. Type, colour and layout conform to the tokens in the standards document (13.3) and to 03-typography.md and 04-colour.md.
13. Removed until it broke, one thing put back (design-taste, section 4, step 6).
14. Captions burned in for sound-off viewing; audio description on the broadcast hero film (11-accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md); OOH contrast readable at distance (20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md, section 7.5).
15. Arabic layouts mirrored and typeset by a native designer; every bilingual line transcreated and read by a second native speaker (7.4).

Brand

16. At least one brand code present and fewer than all of them (8.2).
17. The tone is the brief's three adjectives, checked by someone outside the job.
18. The copy passes mask360-copywriting and design-taste, section 7; the bilingual version is read by a native speaker.
19. Nothing in the work would embarrass the client's founder in front of their family or their board.
20. Paid variants follow the client's performance standard (6.9).

Legal

21. Every release, licence and usage term is in the production bible with an expiry date that covers the planned run (14.5, 14.8).
22. Music has both sync and master licences, or a composer's assignment.
23. Claims are substantiated in writing; green claims have the evidence file (16.3); comparative claims pass ASCI Chapter IV.
24. Every scarcity and urgency claim has its evidence file (16.10).
25. Synthetic elements are listed, labelled where required and disclosed to the client (16.5); CGI is captioned as an artist's impression (9.7).
26. Every property ad shows the RERA registration number and authority website, or the Trakheesi permit number (16.9).
27. Every interactive or personalised mechanic has its consent notice and an approved data map (16.11).
28. Creators hold the UAE Advertiser Permit or the Saudi Mawthooq licence where applicable, and Indian creator posts carry the ASCI label (6.10, 16.6).
29. Children, alcohol, health and financial content checked against 16.7, 16.8 and the Code; national symbols, maps and rulers' images cleared (16.12).

Delivery

30. Every asset matches the spec in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md: size, duration, bitrate, safe areas, subtitles, script and language.
31. The versions are named per the house convention and the asset register is complete.
32. The approvals record shows a dated client signature on the version being shipped.
33. The departure log is closed with every change signed.
34. The team that made it has seen the final and knows where it runs; the credit list is correct.
35. The CD's dated sign-off is in the job's record (10.7).

## Terms introduced

- 360 creative director: a creative director who commands every craft (identity, film, web, social, space, print) well enough to brief, judge and correct the specialist in each, and does at least one at the specialist's level.
- Advertiser Permit: the UAE Media Council's licence, free for its first three years, required of any individual who publishes paid or unpaid promotional content on social media in the UAE.
- Agency producer: the studio's own producer, who owns the estimate, schedule, bids, rights and the production bible.
- Animatic: storyboard frames cut to the chosen music with a scratch voice, used to present a film before it is shot.
- Anti-laws of marketing: Kapferer and Bastien's rules that reverse conventional marketing for luxury brands, 24 in the second edition of "The Luxury Strategy".
- Art and copy pair: the two-person creative unit of an art director and a copywriter, the structural form of advertising ideas since Bernbach's DDB.
- Big idea: one large, famous, repeated thought that organises a campaign, the Bernbach and Ogilvy tradition.
- Braintrust: Pixar's group of experienced makers who give candid notes on work in progress without authority to mandate changes.
- Brief back: the creative team's written restatement of the brief, under 200 words, within 24 hours of receiving it.
- Call sheet: the daily schedule issued to cast and crew for a shoot.
- Campaignable idea: an idea that produces its second and tenth execution as easily as its first.
- Case film: the two-minute film that presents a campaign's problem, insight, idea, execution and results to an awards jury.
- Client chair exercise: writing the five questions the client's own boss will ask them, the day before a presentation, and re-ordering the deck to answer them.
- Codes: a brand's owned, repeated signs (a colour, a weave, an animal, a box), the luxury form of distinctive brand assets.
- Concept: the organising thought a stranger could describe without seeing the work; an execution is one expression of it.
- Cover: alternative takes, framings and versions shot to protect the edit.
- Creative effectiveness ladder: Hurman and Field's six levels for rating a campaign's effect, from influential idea to enduring icon.
- Crit: a structured critique of work in progress.
- Departure log: the record of every change from the approved PPM book, with who asked, why, and the CD's signature.
- Drop: a scarce, timed, announced release of one asset or product.
- E&O insurance: errors and omissions cover for claims arising from the content of a broadcast work.
- Execution: one expression of a concept.
- First AD: the first assistant director, who runs the floor on a shoot.
- First and last 10 percent: the parts of a job the CD does personally (the first board and sketch; the final pass), with the middle delegated against the standards document.
- FOOH: fake out-of-home, computer-generated placements composited into real streets and released as film.
- Genuine brand extension: a product sharing a restricted brand's name that exists in the market at scale and is advertised without reference to the restricted product.
- Hero, hub, help: Google's model of a brand's content as a few big pieces, a regular series and always-on answers.
- Intrecciato: Bottega Veneta's hand-woven leather technique, the house's principal code, 50 years old in 2025.
- Insight: a tension or truth that explains behaviour and opens a creative door; held by the audience already.
- Kill list: the running record of killed routes with one line on why each died.
- Lion: the Cannes Lions award, given as Grand Prix, Gold, Silver and Bronze in each of 31 Lions across 10 tracks in 2026; Titanium is the top track.
- Lookbook: the ordered sequence of images of a collection, briefed as a film with no motion.
- Master licence: the licence to use a specific recording of a composition, obtained from the label or owner.
- Muhurat: the astrologically chosen auspicious date and time that fixes many Indian launches, weddings and openings.
- Matching idea: integration in which each channel does what it is best at in service of one thought; matching luggage is the same key visual applied everywhere.
- No idea test: remove the headline and logo, then the picture, to see whether anything distinctive remains.
- One voice: the client's single appointed person who consolidates internal opinions into one written response per round.
- One-off: a single execution with no second, such as a stunt or a launch film.
- Overage: a cost beyond the approved estimate, requiring written approval before it is incurred.
- Pencil: the D&AD award, in Black, Yellow (or White for Impact), Graphite and Wood, with shortlist below.
- Picture lock: the point in post-production after which no frame changes.
- Pitch theatre: the agency ritual of branded rooms, reveals and chemistry films in a pitch, which the house declines in favour of the lean deck.
- Platform idea: a campaignable idea that runs for years and absorbs product news, seasons and cultures without changing its sentence.
- PPM book: the document signed at the pre-production meeting: treatment, boards, cast, locations, wardrobe, props, schedule, budget, usage and delivery list.
- Production bible: the single folder from which a stranger could deliver the job: brief, approvals, releases, licences with expiry dates, call sheets, specs, references, vendors.
- Reasons to believe: the two or three specific, checkable proofs that support the proposition.
- Release: the signed consent of a person to be photographed or filmed, with the usage terms.
- Ripomatic: existing footage from references cut to a script to show pace and tone, for internal use only.
- Rushes: the raw footage from a shoot.
- Scam ad: work made for awards and run once or never, which the house refuses to enter.
- Series: a repeatable social format with a constant and a variable, where the format is the idea and each post an execution.
- Single-minded proposition: the one thing the work must leave in the audience's head, in one sentence of under 12 words.
- Small idea done perfectly: the luxury alternative to the big idea: a modest thought executed to an unmistakable standard.
- Standards document: the two-page per-job instrument (objective, idea sentence, references, trash list, tokens, tests, examples of done) that lets a CD delegate without the standard falling.
- Surrogate advertising: an advertisement that appears to promote a permitted product in order to promote a restricted one, defined in the CCPA's 2022 guidelines.
- Sync licence: the synchronisation licence to use a composition with moving images, obtained from the publisher.
- Synthetically generated content: ASCI's term for AI-generated or AI-altered material in advertising, subject to its 2026 labelling guidelines.
- Teaser, launch, sustain: the three-phase sequencing of a campaign: scarcity, the hero and full channel set, then the series.
- Territory: a creative direction with a feeling, a reference and a sample execution, short of a finished idea.
- Three-bid rule: three production companies bid on the same treatment and specification so the bids are comparable.
- Tone map: territories plotted on two axes the brief cares about, to show where the brand and competitors sit.
- Trash list: the category tropes refused by name before work begins.
- Treatment: the director's written and visual response to a script, with the approach, references and shot intentions, submitted with the bid.
- Usage right: permission to use a created asset for a defined term, territory and set of media; a buyout is a lump-sum purchase of broad or unlimited usage.
- Video village: the monitor position on a set where the agency and client watch takes.
- Wet proof: a printed proof made on the job's actual paper and press conditions, used to approve colour on a luxury print job.
- Written rationale: the one-page note sent within 24 hours of a presentation: task, insight, idea sentence, why this execution.
