## 1. How to use this corpus

### 1.1 What the corpus is

The corpus is 27 reference files under `creative-director-360/references/`, numbered 00 to 26. Each file is the craft and technical layer for one discipline: the measurements, standards, tool settings, named methods and named practitioners a working creative director needs when the taste call has already been made. The taste call itself lives in the design-taste skill (its SKILL.md and `references/canon.md`, `worlds.md`, `type.md`, `currency-2026.md`). This corpus never restates it. When a file here says "see design-taste, references/type.md", read that file for the judgement and come back here for the execution.

A 360 creative director (CD) is the person accountable for every creative output of a brand or campaign across identity, print, web, product interface, motion, film, social and events, and for the people who make them. The "360" is literal: the CD signs off a hotel's wayfinding and its Instagram reel on the same afternoon and both must read as one brand. Mask360's clients (luxury hospitality, fashion, beauty, jewellery, real estate, F&B, events, and the agency's own showcase) across Mumbai, Jaipur, Delhi, Goa, Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Riyadh need exactly that breadth, which is why the corpus covers film grading and dirham pricing tables in the same set.

### 1.2 The stack: how the skills layer

The four house skills and this corpus form one stack. Each owns a question; the one that owns the question wins when two seem to answer it.

| Layer | Skill | Owns | Wins when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Judgement | design-taste | What reads expensive, current and specific; the canon; the cheap tells; the house layer (Space Grotesk, flame #F53901, white ground, 70 to 30 visual to text) | Any question of "should we" or "does this look good" |
| Direction | art-direction | The shoot brief format, mood board rules, photographer and videographer direction, talent briefing, feedback on visual work | A camera or an image-maker has to be briefed |
| Words | mask360-copywriting | Every line of copy: hooks, captions, overlays, decks, outreach; the 15 copy rules | Any text that a reader will see |
| Generation | higgsfield-prompting | The three-layer prompt system (image, identity, motion), model choice on Higgsfield, camera keywords | A video or image is being generated by a model |
| Craft | creative-director-360 (this corpus) | Standards, specs, measurements, methods, tools, rubrics, the reading and exercise programme | The judgement is made and the work has to be executed, checked or taught |

Order of precedence: Kaavish's instruction in the session, then the design-taste house layer, then this corpus, then any generic rule. The reason: the house layer encodes decisions already taken (one typeface, one accent, no em dashes, no contrast constructions); a craft file that contradicts them is a defect in the craft file.

### 1.4 Conventions used across the files

- As-of dates. Any time-sensitive claim (a tool version, a platform spec, a price, a standard's status, a person in a role) carries "as of <month> <year>". A claim without one is timeless by assertion and may be challenged in critique.
- Cross-references. Sibling files are cited by filename and section: "see 04-colour.md, section 6". The design-taste skill is cited by name and reference file.
- Term definitions. Every technical term is defined in the sentence where it first appears and collected in that file's "Terms introduced" section. The glossary (section 10) merges them.
- Contested subjects. Where practitioners disagree (for example, whether an 8-point spacing scale or a 4-point scale is the right base for interface spacing), the file names the positions and their proponents and states which one the house follows and why.
- Numbers. Approximate values are marked "about" and sourced. Unknown values are marked unknown. A fabricated number is the worst defect a file can carry, because the corpus exists to be trusted when there is no time to check.

## 2. The competency map of a 360 creative director

### 2.1 How to read the levels

The four levels below collapse the five-stage Dreyfus model of skill acquisition (Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus, 1980: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert) into the four that matter for hiring and self-assessment: novice, competent, expert, master. The Dreyfus insight that survives the collapse is that progress is a change in how a person perceives the situation, from rules applied to features to a holistic read with intuitive response. So every level is written as observable behaviour: what the person does when handed a brief, a file or a crit, which a reviewer can see in an hour. Self-report ("I am strong at typography") is not evidence; the behaviours are.

Novice: applies rules without context, needs the rule stated. Competent: chooses between rules, delivers reliable work on a known brief, needs review on anything new. Expert: perceives the whole, works from internalised references, is right without being able to say why at first, and can say why on reflection. Master: sets the rules others apply, teaches by naming the invisible, and produces work that changes what the category expects.

The CD does not need master level in every row. The working target for a 360 CD at Mask360 is master in critique, strategy and brand identity; expert in typography, layout, colour, motion, campaigns, leadership and business; competent or better in everything else, with the judgement to know which specialist to hire for the rest. A CD who is novice in any row cannot brief or critique that row and will be bluffed by vendors in it.

### 2.2 Strategy and briefing

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Restates the client's request as the brief; confuses objective with deliverable; cannot say who the audience is beyond a demographic |
| Competent | Writes a one-page brief with objective, audience, single-minded proposition, mandatories and success measure; identifies the category tropes to refuse |
| Expert | Finds the tension in the brief (the thing the brand wants to be and the thing the audience currently believes) and writes the proposition as that tension resolved; predicts which three ideas the room will produce and briefs past them |
| Master | Reframes the business problem so the creative problem becomes solvable; the brief is one sentence a junior can build from for a year; clients adopt the language of the brief in their own board decks |

### 2.3 Brand identity

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Designs a logo as a drawing; applies it at one size; "guidelines" are a logo and two colours |
| Competent | Builds a system (mark, wordmark, lockups, clear space, minimum sizes, colour, type, grid, image treatment) and tests it on 12 touchpoints; produces a usable guidelines document |
| Expert | Designs the behaviour before the mark (how it moves, speaks, scales, degrades); the system flexes across print, screen, motion, Devanagari and Arabic without losing itself; the identity is recognisable with the logo covered |
| Master | The identity changes what the category looks like; other agencies' pitch decks start citing it as a reference within 18 months; the system is still coherent five years and three CMOs later |

### 2.4 Typography

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Picks a typeface by name recognition; sets body at 16px with 1.5 line height because a blog said so; cannot name the difference between tracking and kerning |
| Competent | Builds a type scale with named steps, sets measure between 45 and 75 characters, pairs two families with a stated reason, kerns display headlines by hand, knows hanging punctuation and optical sizes exist |
| Expert | Reads a typeface's construction (stroke contrast, aperture, x-height, spacing) and predicts how it will behave at 11px and 160px; sets Devanagari and Arabic with the correct vertical metrics and script-specific line heights; detects a 2px baseline error across a spread by eye |
| Master | Commissions or modifies a typeface for a brand with a foundry (Commercial Type, Dinamo, Klim, Indian Type Foundry, Boharat) and can brief the designer in their own vocabulary; the type system is the identity |

### 2.5 Colour

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Chooses colours from a palette generator; cannot explain why a screen green prints dull; uses pure black (#000000) for text on white |
| Competent | Builds a palette of two colours plus one accent from the subject's material world; checks contrast ratios against WCAG 2.2 (4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text); specifies Pantone, CMYK and sRGB values for each colour; knows sRGB from Display P3 |
| Expert | Works in a perceptual colour space (OKLCH or CIELAB) so tints and shades keep their hue; predicts metamerism (two colours that match under one light and differ under another) across paper stocks; matches a printed proof to a screen under D50 viewing light |
| Master | Owns one colour for a brand the way Tiffany owns its blue (Pantone 1837) and Hermès its orange; the colour decision is the brand decision; specifies lighting for a store and a film grade from the same palette logic |

### 2.6 Layout and grids

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Centres everything; margins vary by page; uses a 12-column grid because the framework shipped it |
| Competent | Chooses columns, gutters, margins and baseline for the format and the content; aligns to the baseline grid; builds hierarchy from space and weight before size; breaks the grid once with a reason |
| Expert | Reads a Gentlewoman or Apartamento spread and reconstructs its grid, measure and rhythm in ten minutes; designs the page as a sequence (pacing across spreads or scroll) rather than a set of single pages; uses modular grids (Müller-Brockmann) and the van de Graaf canon where they serve |
| Master | Invents a grid logic that becomes the brand's recognisable structure (Vignelli's Unigrid for the US National Park Service is the reference case); the grid carries the identity when type and colour are absent |

### 2.7 UI design

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Copies a component library's defaults; every corner the same radius; every element the same weight; cannot name states (hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, error, empty) |
| Competent | Builds components with all states; uses a spacing scale and a type scale as design tokens (named values for colour, space, type and radius shared between design and code); hands off with specs a developer can build from without a call |
| Expert | Designs the system (tokens, components, patterns, documentation) and the governance that keeps it alive; reproduces a Linear, Vercel, Stripe or Ssense screen to within 2px and can explain every deviation they would make; density and hierarchy decisions are deliberate |
| Master | Defines a product's visual language so that a new feature designed by a junior six months later still looks like the product; the interface is a brand asset (Linear's interface is the brand) |

### 2.8 UX research

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Asks users what they want; runs a survey with leading questions; treats five opinions as data |
| Competent | Chooses the method for the question (the NN/g cheat sheet: field study, diary study, user interview, card sorting, qualitative usability testing, benchmark testing, survey, analytics review); writes a discussion guide; synthesises into findings with evidence |
| Expert | Runs continuous discovery (Teresa Torres: weekly customer touchpoints, opportunity solution trees); triangulates qualitative and quantitative; knows that five users find about 85 percent of usability problems in a given design (Nielsen and Landauer, 1993) and when that model does not apply |
| Master | Research changes the brief before the brief is written; builds the organisation's research practice (repository, cadence, recruitment, ethics); can tell a client their product has no market with evidence they accept |

### 2.9 Interaction design

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Designs screens, not flows; every action needs a modal; does not know what a system status is |
| Competent | Maps flows before screens; applies Norman's principles (affordance, signifier, feedback, mapping, constraint, conceptual model); designs microinteractions (trigger, rules, feedback, loops and modes, Saffer 2013) for the key actions |
| Expert | Designs for error (undo over confirm), for latency (optimistic UI, skeletons under 300ms, progress above 1 second), and for edge states; prototypes at the fidelity the question needs (paper to code); uses Fitts's law and Hick's law as working tools |
| Master | Invents an interaction pattern that other products adopt (pull to refresh, the swipe-to-dismiss card); the product feels inevitable; the pattern language is documented and taught |

### 2.10 Content design

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Writes labels last; "Submit"; error messages blame the user; "Learn more" everywhere |
| Competent | Writes microcopy with the house copy rules; buttons name the outcome; error messages say what happened and what to do; empty states teach; reading level checked (about grade 8 for public interfaces) |
| Expert | Designs the information architecture and the content model (types, fields, relationships) before the pages; writes bilingual systems where Hindi, Arabic and English copy carry equal craft and the layout accommodates text expansion (Arabic runs about 25 percent longer than English, German about 30 percent, in interface strings) |
| Master | The voice is so specific it is recognisable without the logo; content strategy drives the product roadmap; style guide is used by engineering without prompting |

### 2.11 Accessibility

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Adds alt text at the end; thinks accessibility is a contrast checker; has never used a screen reader |
| Competent | Designs to WCAG 2.2 level AA (W3C Recommendation, first published 5 October 2023, current edition dated 12 December 2024); tests with VoiceOver and NVDA; keyboard-only pass; focus order and visible focus; 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum target size (success criterion 2.5.8) |
| Expert | Tracks WCAG 3.0 (W3C Working Draft dated 10 September 2026) and the legal landscape (European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, applying from 28 June 2025; India's Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016); designs reduced-motion variants (prefers-reduced-motion) as a first-class state; audits a luxury site and finds the 3:1 non-text contrast failures the brand team never noticed |
| Master | Accessibility is invisible in the work because it was designed in from the first sketch; the motion system, colour system and type system all pass by construction; trains the team so no one else needs to audit |

### 2.12 Motion design

| Level | Observable behaviours |
|---|---|
| Novice | Fade-up on everything; linear easing; durations chosen by feel; motion as decoration |
| Competent | Applies the 12 principles of animation (Thomas and Johnston, 1981) and the interface subset (easing, duration, choreography); durations in the 150 to 400ms band for interface, longer for narrative; one considered move per page; ease-out for entries, ease-in for exits |
| Expert | Reproduces a Buck or Ordinary Folk shot frame by frame and names the easing curve, the offset and the overshoot; designs motion as a system (tokens for duration and easing); uses springs (stiffness, damping, mass) with intent; delivers Lottie or Rive for product and ProRes 4444 with alpha for broadcast |
| Master | Motion is the identity (Studio Dumbar's kinetic type work is the cited reference in design-taste currency-2026.md); the system is documented so an in-house team keeps it alive; the CD can direct a motion studio in its own vocabulary |

### 2.13 Video

Video is seven competencies, because a CD who can direct a shoot and cannot read a waveform will be overcharged by a colourist and under-served by an editor.

| Sub-discipline | Novice | Competent | Expert | Master |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-production | Shoots from a mood board | Treatment, script, storyboard, shot list, schedule, call sheet, budget, crew list, location release, talent release | Treatment sells the idea in two pages; shot list is designed for the edit (coverage planned per cut, not per scene); contingency for weather and Gulf heat built in | Pre-production is so complete the shoot is boring; the director can be replaced and the film survives |
| Cinematography | Does not know focal length from f-stop | Chooses lens (focal length, aperture), sensor and frame rate for the look; reads a histogram; knows the exposure triangle; can brief a DP in shot sizes and movements (Mascelli's five Cs: camera angles, continuity, cutting, close-ups, composition) | Reads a Deakins or Lubezki frame and names the light source, ratio, lens and why; chooses a camera (ARRI Alexa 35, Sony Venice 2, RED V-Raptor) for the delivery, not the brochure; specifies LUTs and monitoring on set | Directs the DP's instinct, not their hands; the frames would be recognised as the brand's without a logo |
| Lighting | "Natural light" as a plan | Three-point setup, motivated light, key-to-fill ratios (2:1 soft, 8:1 dramatic), colour temperature in kelvin (3200K tungsten, 5600K daylight), diffusion and negative fill | Lights for the grade (protects highlights, keeps shadow detail at the sensor's floor); designs the light of a hotel film so the architecture, not the lamp, appears to light the room; works with mixed sources without colour casts | Light is the signature; the look is taught to other DPs by name |
| Sound | Camera audio | Lavalier and boom, 48kHz 24-bit, room tone, wild lines, sync (timecode or slate), levels at minus 12 to minus 18 dBFS for dialogue | Sound design (Sonnenschein's layers: dialogue, ambience, effects, music), loudness delivery (minus 14 LUFS integrated for streaming platforms, minus 23 LUFS for EBU R128 broadcast), music licensing and composer briefing | Sound carries the brand; silence is used; the mix is referenced by other agencies |
| Editing | Cuts to music | Assembly, rough cut, fine cut, picture lock; cuts on action; the J-cut and L-cut; Murch's rule of six (emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4); knows the NLE (Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, Final Cut Pro, Avid Media Composer) | Edits for the platform (hook inside 1 second for feed, 3 seconds for pre-roll); shapes rhythm with pauses; a one-minute edit from stock each week that passes crit | The edit is invisible; pacing is a signature; can save a badly shot film in the cut and knows when it cannot be saved |
| Grading | Applies a LUT | Primary correction (exposure, white balance, contrast) then secondary (qualifiers, power windows); reads waveform, vectorscope and parade; works in a colour-managed pipeline (ACES or DaVinci Wide Gamut); knows Rec. 709 from Rec. 2020 and SDR from HDR (PQ, HLG) | Matches a reference grade shot for shot; builds a show LUT for the brand; grades for the delivery display (phone in sunlight in Dubai, OLED in a boardroom) | The grade is the look of the brand across years and DPs; colourists reference it |
| Delivery | Exports "high quality" | Codec and container per platform (H.264 or H.265 in MP4 for social, ProRes 422 HQ for masters, ProRes 4444 for alpha); bitrate, frame rate, resolution and aspect ratio per spec; safe areas for 9:16 (UI overlays at top and bottom); captions burned or sidecar; archive with checksums | Delivery matrix for a campaign of 40 assets in 6 ratios and 4 languages built in an afternoon; QC pass (black frames, audio peaks, caption sync) before the client sees it | Delivery is a system the whole agency uses; nothing is re-exported twice |

## 3. How taste and judgement are built

Taste, as design-taste defines it, is a trained prediction of what a sophisticated audience will find desirable, current and inevitable. This section is the training method: how the prediction gets trained, measured and kept sharp. The three components are exposure (what has been seen), discrimination (the ability to tell better from worse in a pair) and articulation (the ability to say why). Each is built separately and each has a number attached.

### 3.1 Exposure volume

The visual memory a CD draws on is built by volume, and the volume is larger than most designers believe. A working rule for this corpus: 10,000 references seen with attention, filed and tagged, before judgement becomes reliable; about 200 a week sustained for a year. "With attention" means each reference is looked at for at least 30 seconds and one thing is named about it (the margin, the light, the pairing). Scrolling Pinterest at two images per second is exposure without encoding and builds nothing.

Where the 10,000 comes from: Fonts In Use (launched December 2010 as a blog, full site July 2012) indexes thousands of real-world typographic uses; Brand New (Armin Vit and Bryony Gomez-Palacio, UnderConsideration) reviews several identities a week and has done so since 2006; Siteinspire and Godly curate sites; Mobbin indexes about 620,000 product screens as of May 2026 by its own count (quoted by third-party comparison pages; verify on mobbin.com); a single issue of The Gentlewoman carries about 40 spreads worth measuring. A year of these at 200 a week is 10,400.

Exposure must be curated against the trash lists in design-taste references/worlds.md. A thousand Dribbble shots teach the Dribbble look; the cure is to weight the diet towards the canon (Pentagram, Studio Dumbar, Bureau Borsche, the fashion houses, the foundries) and towards primary artefacts (a printed Gentlewoman, a real Aesop bottle) over screenshots of them.

### 3.2 Discrimination training

Discrimination is the skill of telling better from worse in a pair and is trained with pairs. The method, borrowed from perceptual learning research and from wine and coffee grading, is forced-choice comparison with feedback: two spreads, two logos, two cuts; choose the better one in two seconds; then check against a reference judgement (a senior's call, an award jury's result, a Brand New verdict) and record hit or miss. The two-second limit matters because taste operates at the speed of System 1 (Kahneman's fast, intuitive mode) and the slow mode can rationalise anything.

Targets: 100 pairs a week; a hit rate above 80 percent against the reference judgement in a discipline before trusting the gut in that discipline; log kept in a sheet with date, pair, call, reference, hit or miss. Where no reference judgement exists, the pair is still worth doing: name the better one and write one line of why, then revisit in a month.

A second drill is the blind reconstruction: look at a reference for 10 seconds, close it, redraw the layout in wireframe, reopen and compare. The gap between memory and reference is a map of what the eye has not yet learned to see (most people first miss the margin ratios and the type size relationships, then the alignment of secondary elements).

### 3.3 Articulation

Articulation is the vocabulary to say what the eye sees, and it is what turns private taste into direction a team can execute. A CD who can only say "it feels off" cannot brief a fix. The drill: every reference filed gets one sentence in technical vocabulary ("Söhne Breit at 11px tracked plus 80, hairline rule, 24-column grid, 1:1.618 margin ratio"), and every crit uses the vocabulary of the discipline being critiqued (lens and ratio for a frame, easing and offset for a motion, measure and leading for type). The glossary pointed to in section 10 is the shared vocabulary; a term absent from it gets added when it is first used.

### 3.4 The Ira Glass gap

Ira Glass (host of This American Life) described, in part 3 of the "Ira Glass on Storytelling" video series produced with Current TV and PRI, the condition of every beginner: the taste that pulled them into creative work is ahead of their ability to make work that meets it, so their own early work disappoints them, and most quit in that phase. His prescription is volume with deadlines (a story a week, a story a month) because only a volume of finished work closes the gap. The corpus adopts this directly: section 6's ladder is counted in finished pieces, and a piece abandoned at 80 percent counts as zero. The reason: the last 20 percent (the ending, the kerning, the export) is where the craft lives and the only part the audience sees.

### 3.5 Deliberate practice

Anders Ericsson's research programme on expertise is the evidence base for the ladder. In Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993, Psychological Review, volume 100, number 3, pages 363 to 406), the best violin students at the Berlin Hochschule der Künste had accumulated an estimated 7,410 hours of solitary practice by age 18, the good students 5,301 hours and the future music teachers 3,420 hours. Malcolm Gladwell's Outliers (2008) rounded the best group's total by age 20 into the "10,000-hour rule"; Ericsson and Pool's Peak (2016) rejects the rounding and restates the finding: the hours only count when they are deliberate practice, which has four conditions.

1. A specific, measurable goal for the session (a kerning pass on one headline, one easing curve matched), set just beyond current ability.
2. Full concentration; sessions of 60 to 90 minutes with rest, because concentration degrades and sloppy practice trains sloppiness.
3. Immediate, informative feedback: a reference to overlay, a scope to read, a senior to ask, a measurement to take.
4. Repetition with refinement: the same drill repeated with the error from the last attempt corrected.

The product of deliberate practice, in Ericsson's account, is mental representations: detailed internal models that let the expert see a finished page before it exists and detect deviation from it at a glance. Every drill in section 6 is written to those four conditions, with the feedback source named.

### 3.6 Copywork of masters

Copywork is reproduction of a master's work by hand to learn its decisions from the inside; it is how painters trained for 500 years and how copywriters still train (hand-copying Ogilvy and Bernbach ads). For design the rule is reproduction to measurement: the reproduction is overlaid on the original at 50 percent opacity and the deviation is measured in pixels or millimetres. A reproduction that is "close" teaches the look; a reproduction measured to within 2px teaches the decisions, because the last 2px is where the designer had to choose.

What to copy, by discipline: type specimens (Bringhurst's own pages, Hochuli's, foundry specimens from Commercial Type and Klim); Müller-Brockmann posters; Gentlewoman and Apartamento spreads; Linear, Vercel, Stripe and Ssense screens; Buck and Ordinary Folk shots; Deakins and Lubezki frames (lit and shot with a phone and one lamp, then graded to match); Aesop and Hermès packaging (dielines rebuilt from the object). The reproduction is never shipped; the muscle it builds is.

### 3.8 Crit culture

A crit (critique session) is the fastest taste-building instrument an agency has, because it is discrimination training with articulation and feedback in one hour. The format is design-taste section 8 (verdict, what works, what does not and why, the fix with a reference). The rules that make it work:

- The work is on the wall or the screen, full size, before anyone speaks; two minutes of silent looking first, because the two-second verdict needs to form before the explanations start.
- The maker states the brief in one sentence and then stays silent until asked.
- Idea problem first, craft problem second; a crit that opens with kerning when the idea is wrong wastes the room.
- One reference per fix, opened on screen, with the specific thing to take from it named.
- Verdict recorded (expensive, competent, cheap) and revisited at the next round; the trend across rounds is the maker's growth curve.
- Weekly cadence (Friday, 90 minutes, whole studio); a studio that crits monthly is a studio that ships its first idea.

Running 10 crits is on the ladder in section 6 because leading a crit is a different skill from attending one: the leader must hold the room to the idea problem, protect the maker from pile-on, and end with a decision.

### 3.9 The 10,000 decisions view

A finished identity, site or film is the sum of about 10,000 small decisions (the figure is an order of magnitude, used as a working model: a 60-page brand guideline has about 2,000 typographic decisions alone, counting each size, weight, tracking, leading and alignment choice; a 90-second film has about 1,500 across shot, cut, grade and mix). The view has three consequences for the CD.

1. Taste is the default setting for the decisions nobody reviews; the 9,900 decisions no client will ever see are made by the maker's defaults, so training the defaults (sections 3.1 to 3.6) is training the work.
2. Craft is visible in aggregate; no single decision reads as expensive, and the sum does, which is why "it looks like four hundred hours" (design-taste section 1) is a real test.
3. A system (tokens, grids, type scales, motion durations, a delivery matrix) is a way of making a thousand decisions once; the corpus's craft files are mostly systems for that reason.

### 3.10 The reference-first habit

The single habit that distinguishes experts from competents in the competency map is reference-first: before any build, three to five references from the canon with the borrowed thing named for each (design-taste section 4, step 1). The corpus treats skipping it as a process defect in critique. The reason is cognitive: without a reference the maker builds from their most recent exposure, which is whatever the feed showed them this morning, and the feed is the peaking and dead layer of the wave.
