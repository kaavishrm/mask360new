## 1. How to run this file

### 1.1 Where this file sits

The design-taste skill owns judgement: what reads expensive, the canon, the cheap tells, the house layer. 00-curriculum-and-method.md owns the syllabus and the exercise ladder. This file owns the mind: the operations a master performs between receiving a brief and signing off a piece, the evidence that those operations work, and the daily and yearly rhythm that keeps them working. When a session asks "how would a great designer approach this", run section 8 as a procedure and consult sections 2 to 7 for the reason behind each step.

### 1.2 The thesis in four claims

1. Mastery is a repeatable structure of about sixteen named moves (section 4), each with a documented cognitive basis (sections 2 and 3). The moves are the same across disciplines; the materials differ.
2. The structure is trained by a daily rhythm (section 5) and a principle-making cycle (section 7), both of which can be scheduled and audited.
3. The best evidence says the structure is learnable and the ceiling varies by person. Deliberate practice explains between under 1 percent and 26 percent of performance variance depending on domain (Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, 2014), so the house trains the structure and refuses to promise a ceiling.
4. Under commercial conditions the structure survives only when it is protected by ritual: the single-idea presentation, the crit, the brief as instrument (section 10).

### 1.3 Reading rules

Every move in section 4 cites at least three named practitioners and the source where the habit is documented. Where a habit is reported second-hand or the attribution is unverified, the text says so. Nothing in this file overrides the house layer in design-taste section 7.

## 2. The science of creative cognition

Each entry gives the researcher, the finding as measured, and the design consequence the house draws from it. The consolidated table is in section 12.2.

### 2.1 Divergent and convergent thinking (Guilford)

J. P. Guilford's 1950 presidential address to the American Psychological Association and his Structure of Intellect model (1967) separated divergent thinking (producing many candidate answers to an open problem) from convergent thinking (narrowing to the one correct answer). His divergent tests score four properties: fluency (count of ideas), flexibility (count of categories), originality (statistical rarity) and elaboration (detail added). Consequence: generation and judgement are separate operations and must be separated in time. A sketch session that judges as it goes produces fewer and more conventional candidates. The house generate step (section 8.3) bans evaluation until a fixed count is reached; the count is the fluency score, and the category spread is the flexibility score.

### 2.2 The Geneplore model (Finke, Ward and Smith)

Ronald Finke, Thomas Ward and Steven Smith's Creative Cognition (1992) describes a two-phase loop: a generative phase producing preinventive structures (mental forms, images or part-combinations that have no assigned meaning yet), and an exploratory phase that interprets those forms, tests them against constraints and sends them back for regeneration. Their experiments asked participants to combine three simple parts (a sphere, a hook, a tube) before being told the product category, and the forms produced before the category was assigned were judged more creative. Consequence: make forms before meanings. Styleframes precede storyboards at Buck; type experiments precede the brand rationale at Studio Dumbar; the house asks for ten compositions before the one-sentence idea is finalised, then interprets.

### 2.3 Conceptual blending (Fauconnier and Turner)

Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner's The Way We Think (2002) models creative meaning as blending: two input mental spaces are mapped through a generic space into a blended space that carries emergent structure present in neither input. The desktop metaphor blends an office and a file system; Jacquemus's 2023 Paris stunt blended a Bambino bag and a city bus. Consequence: a blend needs two specific inputs. "Luxury" plus "hospitality" produces nothing because neither is a space with structure; "a Jaipur haveli courtyard at 6am" plus "a Swiss timetable" produces a layout with a thermal rhythm and a strict grid. Name both inputs in the brief.

### 2.4 Bisociation (Koestler)

Arthur Koestler's The Act of Creation (1964) called the creative act bisociation: the collision of two self-consistent frames of reference (he called them matrices) that are habitually kept apart. He argued that humour, scientific discovery and art share this one mechanism and differ in emotional tone. Consequence: the "tension" rule in design-taste section 1 is bisociation applied: one collision per piece, two matrices named, and the collision placed where the eye lands first. Two collisions cancel; none reads as corporate.

### 2.5 Analogical reasoning and structure mapping (Gentner)

Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory (1983) showed that strong analogies map systems of relations between domains while weak ones map surface attributes. "The atom is like the solar system" maps orbits and attraction; "the logo is like a sunset" maps a colour. Consequence: when design-taste's reference-board rule asks for "the exact thing borrowed", the borrowed thing should be a relation (the ratio of silence to event in a Deakins frame, the pacing of a Jonathan Glazer cut, the way Aesop's product grid subordinates the bottle to the label) and never a surface (the beige). Surface borrowing is what makes work read as copied.

### 2.6 Incubation, the default mode network and insight

Roger Beaty and colleagues (PNAS, 2018, 115(5), pages 1087 to 1092) scanned 163 people generating alternative uses for objects and found that individual creative ability was predicted by connectivity between three brain systems that normally work in opposition: the default mode network (active in memory, imagination and unfocused thought), the executive control network (sustained attention and evaluation) and the salience network (which switches between them). The same connectivity pattern predicted creativity in two further independent samples. John Kounios and Mark Beeman's insight studies (Jung-Beeman et al., PLoS Biology, 2004; The Eureka Factor, 2015) recorded a burst of alpha activity over the right occipital cortex roughly 1.5 seconds before a reported insight (the brain reducing visual input) followed by a gamma burst in the right anterior superior temporal gyrus about 300 milliseconds before the answer. Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod's 2009 meta-analysis of incubation (Psychological Bulletin) found real incubation effects, larger for divergent tasks and larger when the interval is filled with a low-demand task rather than another hard task. Consequence: schedule gaps on purpose; fill them with walking, washing up or administrative work, never with a second design problem; and treat the moment of looking away from the screen as part of the method.

### 2.7 The Wallas four stages and the evidence on each

Graham Wallas's The Art of Thought (1926) named preparation, incubation, illumination and verification. The evidence a century later: preparation is the best supported stage, because every expertise study (section 2.11) shows that what arrives during incubation is drawn from what was loaded during preparation; incubation is supported with the conditions in 2.6; illumination exists as a measurable neural event but it is the end of a process and arrives only to a prepared mind; verification is where the professional's hours actually go and is the stage the eureka myth deletes. Consequence: the house operating system (section 8) assigns about 60 percent of elapsed time to preparation and verification combined.

### 2.8 Flow (Csikszentmihalyi)

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's Flow (1990) identified the conditions of absorbed performance: clear goals, immediate feedback, a challenge slightly above current skill, and the resulting merging of action and awareness with loss of self-consciousness and distorted time sense. Consequence: three operational rules. Use tools that give feedback in under a second (the Figma canvas, a live browser, a scrub bar), which is also Bret Victor's principle (section 7.2). Protect blocks of 90 minutes or more, because entry takes about 15 minutes and every interruption restarts it. Calibrate challenge: boredom means add a constraint; anxiety means cut scope.

### 2.9 The adjacent possible (Kauffman, Johnson)

Stuart Kauffman's Investigations (2000) defined the adjacent possible as the set of states reachable in one step from what exists now; Steven Johnson's Where Good Ideas Come From (2010) applied it to innovation, arguing that ideas arrive when their components become available. Consequence: a studio's reference library sets the edge of what it can reach. A library of only design work makes the adjacent possible a set of other design work; a library that includes architecture, film, science and craft widens the edge. Section 5.4 sets the ratio.

### 2.10 Constraints as creative fuel (Stokes)

Patricia Stokes's Creativity from Constraints (2006) argued from case studies (Monet's series paintings, Motherwell's single motif) that constraints work by precluding the habitual response and so promoting the novel one, and that masters impose constraints on themselves before anyone else does. Catrinel Haught-Tromp (Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 2017) found that people asked to write greeting-card rhymes containing a required noun produced rhymes rated more creative than those written without the requirement. Consequence: the house constraint set in design-taste section 7 (one typeface, two colours plus one accent, no fade-ups) is a creativity device and a taste device at once. When a piece is flat, the first fix is to add a constraint, never to add freedom.

### 2.11 Expertise, chunking and deliberate practice (Chase and Simon, Ericsson, Macnamara)

William Chase and Herbert Simon (1973) showed that chess masters recall real board positions far better than novices because they encode them as a few familiar chunks, and estimated a master's vocabulary at roughly 50,000 chunks; on random positions masters were no better than novices. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer (1993) defined deliberate practice as effortful activity designed to improve performance, at the edge of current ability, with feedback; the best violin students in their Berlin sample had accumulated about 10,000 hours by age 20, which Malcolm Gladwell (Outliers, 2008) turned into a rule the original authors never stated. Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald's meta-analysis (Psychological Science, 2014; 157 effect sizes, more than 11,000 participants) found deliberate practice explained 26 percent of performance variance in games, 21 percent in music, 18 percent in sports, 4 percent in education and under 1 percent in professions. Ericsson disputed their coding; the dispute is unresolved. Consequence: design is a profession with delayed, noisy feedback, which is exactly the condition under which hours convert worst. The fix is to make practice game-like: short defined tasks, a right answer or a crit within the hour, and a record. That is the design of the exercise ladder in 00-curriculum-and-method.md section 6.

### 2.12 Perceptual learning and the trained eye (Kellman)

Philip Kellman's perceptual learning modules (Kellman and Kaiser, 1997, for aviation instrument reading; Kellman and Garrigan, Physics of Life Reviews, 2009) train discrimination through hundreds of short trials with immediate feedback, and show that expert-level pattern recognition can be built in hours rather than years when the trials are structured. Consequence: the two-second "expensive or cheap" read in design-taste section 1 is a perceptual skill and is trained the same way: paired comparisons (which of these two kerning settings, which of these two margins, which of these two cuts) with an answer, 50 to 100 per session. Section 11.2 schedules them.

### 2.13 Remote associates and semantic networks (Mednick)

Sarnoff Mednick (1962) proposed that creative people have flatter associative hierarchies: a cue activates many associations at similar strength, so remote ones are reachable. His Remote Associates Test gives three words (cottage, Swiss, cake) and asks for the fourth (cheese). Consequence: the network is fed by what is consumed. A designer who consumes only design has a steep hierarchy in which "luxury hotel" activates "serif, cream, terracotta" and nothing else, which is the AI-default convergence in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md. Section 5.4 is the counter-diet.

### 2.14 The quantity to quality relation (Simonton)

Dean Keith Simonton's equal odds rule (1997) states that across a career a creator's number of hits is a positive, linear, stochastic function of total output, and that the hit ratio stays roughly constant: more work, more hits, same ratio. Evidence at the level of single sessions is mixed: Jung et al. (Frontiers in Psychology, 2015) found the number of responses on a divergent task correlated 0.73 with judged creativity, while a 2019 review in the same journal reported studies finding no relation or a negative one at very high volumes. Consequence: produce many and select hard. The house asks for ten compositions, three directions, one presented. Beeple's everydays (section 4.9) are the extreme case.

### 2.15 Blind variation and selective retention (Campbell)

Donald Campbell (1960) proposed that creative thought works like natural selection: variations produced without foreknowledge of which will succeed ("blind"), then selected and retained. Consequence: the selection mechanism matters as much as the generation. A studio with no crit ritual, no test battery and no written principles generates variation and retains noise. Sections 7 and 11 are the retention mechanism.

### 2.16 Mood, sleep and walking

Matthijs Baas, Carsten De Dreu and Bernard Nijstad's meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin, 2008) found that activating positive moods (happiness, elation) raise creative output, deactivating moods (relaxation, calm) do not, and activating negative moods (anger, anxiety) raise it under some conditions. Ullrich Wagner and colleagues (Nature, 2004, "Sleep inspires insight") trained people on a number task with a hidden shortcut; about 59 percent of those who slept eight hours before retesting found the shortcut, against about 23 percent of those kept awake. Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz (Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014) ran four experiments with 176 participants: walkers produced more novel alternative uses in 81 to 100 percent of cases depending on the experiment, about twice as many in one experiment, Stanford's summary put the average increase at about 60 percent, the benefit held indoors on a treadmill facing a wall, persisted briefly after sitting down, and reversed slightly for single-answer convergent tasks. Consequence: walk before generating and sit before converging; never make the final sign-off call at the end of a late night; and treat a flat studio mood as a production problem.

### 2.17 The myths, corrected

| Myth | Evidence against | Source | House position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eureka: ideas arrive from nowhere | Insight has a measurable neural run-up and draws only on loaded material | Kounios and Beeman 2015; Wallas 1926 | Load first, then wait; the wait is scheduled |
| Innate genius: you have it or you do not | Output and practice structure predict success; heritable factors also matter, so the ceiling varies | Simonton 1997; Macnamara et al. 2014; Ericsson 1993 | Train the structure; promise no ceiling |
| Group brainstorming produces more ideas | Nominal groups (people working alone, ideas pooled) produced about twice as many ideas as interacting groups across 22 experiments; production blocking (waiting to speak) was the main cause | Osborn 1953 made the claim; Diehl and Stroebe, JPSP 1987, 53, 497 to 509, refuted it; Mullen, Johnson and Salas 1991 meta-analysis | Generate alone in writing, converge together |

## 3. Design cognition and the designerly way of knowing

### 3.1 Schön: reflection in action

Donald Schön's The Reflective Practitioner (1983) observed architects and found that the practitioner thinks by acting: a move is made in the material, the situation "talks back", the practitioner reads the talk-back and moves again. He called this reflection in action and the whole loop a conversation with the materials of the situation. Framing is the act of naming what kind of problem this is; reframing is changing that name when the talk-back resists. Consequence: Claude designs by making a draft early and reading it, never by planning the whole piece in text first. A layout that resists is a frame that is wrong, so the first response to resistance is to reframe, never to force.

### 3.2 Cross: designerly ways of knowing

Nigel Cross ("Designerly ways of knowing", Design Studies, 1982; the 2006 book; Design Thinking, 2011) summarised four decades of protocol studies: designers are solution-focused (they learn about the problem by proposing solutions), they reason abductively (abduction is inference to the best explanation: given a desired value and no known form, propose a form that would deliver it), and the problem and the solution co-evolve (Dorst and Cross, Design Studies, 2001): a partial solution reveals a new aspect of the problem, which reshapes the solution. Consequence: the brief is a draft until the second round. Writing a complete brief before any making produces a brief for the wrong problem.

### 3.3 Lawson: primary generators and breadth-first versus depth-first

Bryan Lawson's How Designers Think (1980; fourth edition 2006) reported Jane Darke's 1979 interviews with architects, who began from a primary generator: a single strong idea (a view, a courtyard, a material) that narrows the problem enough to start, chosen before analysis is complete. Lawson's own experiment set architecture and science postgraduates a coloured-block arrangement task with a hidden rule: scientists worked problem-first, testing combinations to find the rule; architects worked solution-first, proposing arrangements and discarding those that failed. Consequence: a master starts from one generator and tests it hard, which is the single-idea discipline in section 4.3. The risk is fixation (3.13), so the generator is held loosely for the first round.

### 3.4 Dorst: frame innovation

Kees Dorst's Frame Innovation (2015) makes the frame the creative unit: "if we look at the situation as X, then Y follows". His worked cases (Sydney's Kings Cross nightlife, reframed from policing to hosting a festival) show that the most valuable design move is often a new frame and the artefacts follow from it. His nine-step method runs archaeology (how did the problem come to be), paradox, context, field, themes, frames, futures, transformation, integration. Consequence: the house demands three frames before choosing one (section 8.2), and writes each in Dorst's form. "If we look at this Dubai residence launch as a private members' invitation, then the site has no public navigation."

### 3.5 Rittel and Webber: wicked problems

Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber ("Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning", Policy Sciences, 1973) listed ten properties of wicked problems: no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, solutions good or bad rather than true or false, no immediate test, every attempt counts (one-shot), no enumerable solution set, every problem unique, every problem a symptom of another, the explanation chosen determines the solution, and the planner has no right to be wrong. Consequence: brand and campaign problems are wicked; UI problems are often tame. Treat a brand problem as tame (one brief, one answer, one test) and the work will be correct and dead. The stopping rule is supplied by the test battery (design-taste section 4, and the file numbered 23 in this corpus), because the problem will not supply one.

### 3.6 Simon: satisficing and ill-structured problems

Herbert Simon's The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) treated design as the science of the artificial: devising courses of action to change existing situations into preferred ones. Satisficing (1956) is accepting the first option that meets a threshold because the full search is impossible; ill-structured problems are those whose goal, constraints and operators are not all given. Consequence: set the threshold explicitly and high (the test battery), so that satisficing stops at expensive rather than at adequate.

### 3.7 Goldschmidt: seeing as and seeing that

Gabriela Goldschmidt ("The dialectics of sketching", Creativity Research Journal, 1991) analysed architects' sketch protocols and found a rapid alternation between seeing as (reading a figural, metaphoric possibility in the marks: this blob could be a canopy) and seeing that (reading a non-figural fact: that span is too long). Consequence: sketching is cognition, which is why hand sketching survives among masters (section 5.11); and a crit should name which mode a comment is in, because "this feels heavy" (seeing as) and "the margin is 12px" (seeing that) need different replies.

### 3.8 Goel: lateral and vertical transformations

Vinod Goel's Sketches of Thought (1995) distinguished lateral transformations (moving from one idea to a different idea) from vertical transformations (refining the same idea), and showed that ambiguous, freehand media support lateral moves while precise media (CAD, and today a pixel-exact Figma frame) push designers into vertical refinement too early. Consequence: generate in a loose medium (pencil, grey boxes, a styleframe at 25 percent zoom) and refine in a precise one. A pixel-perfect first draft is a vertical move disguised as a start.

### 3.9 Buxton: sketching versus prototyping

Bill Buxton's Sketching User Experiences (2007) separates sketches (quick, timely, cheap, disposable, plentiful, ambiguous, suggestive, exploring) from prototypes (specific, resolved, testing a known question). The error he documents is prototyping before sketching: building one thing well instead of considering many things lightly. Consequence: ten sketches before one prototype; a prototype answers a question written down in advance.

### 3.10 Kolko: abductive synthesis and sensemaking

Jon Kolko ("Abductive Thinking and Sensemaking", Design Issues, 2010) described synthesis as the externalised, abductive act of organising research into a frame that implies a design, using affinity diagrams, concept maps and insight statements. Sensemaking is the social process of giving data meaning. Consequence: research without a wall of post-its that the team reorganises is data, and data does not imply a design. The synthesis session is scheduled, not assumed.

### 3.11 Brown and Katz, and the Jen critique

Tim Brown with Barry Katz (Change by Design, 2009) codified design thinking as a repeatable process (inspiration, ideation, implementation) usable by non-designers. Natasha Jen, a Pentagram partner, argued in 2017 ("Design Thinking is Bullshit", 99U) that the codified version replaced craft and critique with post-its and produced work that no one reviewed against a standard. House position: Brown is right that the moves are teachable; Jen is right that without a crit against a standard the moves produce nothing. This corpus follows Jen on the standard (the test battery) and Brown on the teachability.

### 3.12 Nelson and Stolterman, Krippendorff, Alexander

Harold Nelson and Erik Stolterman's The Design Way (2003; second edition 2012) argued design is a distinct tradition of enquiry alongside science and art, with its own judgement types (framing, appreciative, compositional) and the designer's "guarantor" being the desiderata of the client rather than truth. Klaus Krippendorff's The Semantic Turn (2006) holds that artefacts are designed for meaning: people act on what things mean to them, so the designer's object is meaning and the form is the carrier. Christopher Alexander's Notes on the Synthesis of Form (1964) decomposed a problem into a tree of misfit variables and solved subtrees independently; A Pattern Language (1977, with Ishikawa and Silverstein) published 253 patterns, each in the form context, problem, solution, with links to larger and smaller patterns. Consequence: a pattern is a thinking tool because it packages a frame and a solution with the evidence that they belong together; a design system (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md) that lacks the "problem" field for each component is a parts list.

### 3.13 Fixation (Jansson and Smith) and the library in the head

David Jansson and Steven Smith ("Design fixation", Design Studies, 1991, 12(1), pages 3 to 11) gave engineering students briefs (a car-mounted bicycle rack, a measuring cup for the blind, a spill-proof disposable coffee cup); those shown an example solution reproduced its features, including its flaws, even when instructed to avoid them. Lawson's later work describes the precedent library: experienced designers carry a large stock of prior solutions and recognise which applies. The two findings pull against each other: the library makes a master fast; the example makes a novice fixated. Masters avoid fixation by loading many precedents (so none dominates), by naming the borrowed relation (2.5) rather than copying the form, by deliberately generating a direction that contradicts the example, and by looking at the reference board once at the start and then covering it. Consequence: Claude reads the references, writes the borrowed relation in one line each, then designs with the references closed.

## 4. The moves shared by masters

Each move is named, given its cognitive basis, and illustrated with at least three named practitioners and the source where the habit is documented.

### 4.1 Immersion

Basis: preparation (2.7), the library (3.13). Roger Deakins has said in interviews and on the Team Deakins podcast (from 2020) that he reads a script repeatedly, without notes at first, until images start to arrive, and only then begins lists. Jony Ive's immersion is in material and process: Walter Isaacson's Steve Jobs (2011) records his months with aluminium suppliers and the unibody machining process, and Ive's own account of caring about the parts no one sees comes from the same source. Matthieu Blazy's Bottega Veneta debut (February 2022) was built in the atelier: the "denim" trousers and "flannel" shirts were printed and worked nubuck leather, trompe l'oeil that only an immersion in the leather workshop produces. Walter Murch (The Conversations, with Michael Ondaatje, 2002) describes judging a cut with the sound turned off to see whether the pictures hold by themselves, and separately with the picture off. Consequence: the immersion stage (8.1) reads the brief, the subject, the place, the material and the audience before a reference board is opened.

### 4.2 The single question

Basis: framing (3.1, 3.4). Dieter Rams's standing question was whether a thing is necessary; his "Weniger, aber besser" (less, but better) is the answer. Ive's recurring question in interviews is what the quietest version of the object is: what remains when the design stops announcing itself. Kenya Hara (Designing Design, 2007) asks what the object would be if it were honest to its use, which is why Muji products carry no promise. Consequence: before generating, write one question in under twelve words. The house defaults: "What is the quietest version?", "What would this be if it were honest?", "What is unnecessary?"

### 4.3 The single idea discipline

Basis: primary generator (3.3), bisociation (2.4). Bill Bernbach's Doyle Dane Bernbach built the Volkswagen "Think small" campaign (1959, art director Helmut Krone, copywriter Julian Koenig) on one idea per advertisement. Michael Bierut (How To, 2015) presents one idea to a client and explains why; the alternatives he shows are the ones he does not recommend, labelled as such. The house rule in design-taste section 4 is the same: one sentence, as a feeling, or there is no design yet. Consequence: the frame stage (8.2) ends when one sentence exists; everything that does not serve it is cut at 8.7.

### 4.4 Inversion and the what if

Basis: lateral transformation (3.8), blending (2.3). Virgil Abloh's method, laid out in his Harvard Graduate School of Design lecture (2017, published as Insert Complicated Title Here, 2018), is a set of "cheat codes": quotation marks around a word on a garment turn the object into a comment on itself; the "3 percent approach" (4.8) is the inversion of originality. Simon Porte Jacquemus inverts scale: the Chiquito bag (2018) too small to hold a phone, the 2023 CGI stunt of bus-sized Bambino bags driving through Paris (made with Ian Padgham). Jonathan Anderson at Loewe placed surreal objects inside classical tailoring (the balloon heels of 2022, the pixelated hoodie and dress of 2022). Consequence: at 8.3 every direction is run through "what if the opposite", "what if ten times the size", "what if it were the only thing on the page".

### 4.5 Subtraction as the primary move

Basis: constraints (2.10), satisficing threshold (3.6). Rams's tenth principle: good design is as little design as possible. The Row (Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen, 2006) removed the logo, then the show invitation's phones (the February 2024 Paris show asked guests to put phones away). Hara's concept of emptiness (White, 2010) is a receptacle the user fills with meaning, which is why a Muji label says nothing. Massimo Vignelli's canon (2010) subtracts typefaces to a handful. Consequence: the subtract stage (8.7) runs "remove until it breaks, put one back".

### 4.6 Constraint setting

Basis: 2.10. Vignelli listed in The Vignelli Canon the few typefaces he used (Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Futura, Times and Helvetica) and argued the discipline was the point. Peter Saville's Unknown Pleasures sleeve (Joy Division, Factory Records, 1979) carries no band name and no title on the front, only the pulsar plot; Factory's willingness to leave it off was the constraint that made the image an icon. Muji's name (mujirushi ryohin, no-brand quality goods, 1980) is a constraint written as a company. David Carson broke rules he knew: setting a Bryan Ferry interview in Ray Gun (1994) entirely in Zapf Dingbats was legible only as a decision about an unreadable interview, and Carson has said the readable version appeared later in the issue. Consequence: constraints are written at 8.4 before making, in the form "we will not use", and the list is shown to the client as a feature.

### 4.7 Cross-domain transfer

Basis: structure mapping (2.5), the adjacent possible (2.9). Fashion from sculpture and architecture: Rei Kawakubo's "Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body" (Comme des Garçons, spring 1997) padded the body into sculpture; Iris van Herpen's collaborations with architect Philip Beesley brought structural systems into couture. UI from physical objects at Apple: Bas Ording's inertial scrolling and rubber-band bounce (iPhone, 2007) map the physics of a thrown object onto a list; the iOS 7 reset (2013) kept the physics and removed the leather. Motion from physics: spring-based animation (the Apple springs, and the spring model in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md section 3) replaced hand-drawn curves. Editing from music: Thelma Schoonmaker cuts Scorsese's sequences to songs chosen before shooting (Goodfellas, 1990, the "Layla" coda); Paul Machliss cut Baby Driver (2017) to the pre-cleared soundtrack on set with Edgar Wright. Consequence: at 8.3 at least one direction borrows its structure from a domain outside design, and the borrowed relation is written down.

### 4.8 Reference as raw material and the three percent change

Basis: 2.5, 3.13. Abloh's "3 percent approach" (Harvard GSD lecture, 2017): take an existing object and change it by about 3 percent, enough to make it a new idea, and declare the source. Duchamp's Fountain (1917) is the readymade: an existing object repositioned as art by context and signature. The line "good artists copy, great artists steal" is routinely attributed to Picasso; the attribution is unverified, and Steve Jobs quoted it in 1996 (Triumph of the Nerds). Consequence: the reference is named, the borrowed relation is named, and the change is deliberate and visible. Unnamed borrowing is the reference regurgitation of 9.2.

### 4.9 Quantity then selection

Basis: 2.14, 2.15. Beeple (Mike Winkelmann) has made and posted one finished image every day since 1 May 2007; his site counter read 7,086 consecutive days when checked for this file, and the unbroken count on 28 October 2026 would be about 7,121. Stefan Sagmeister's studio produces by iteration and discards most of it; his sabbatical (4.12) exists to refill. Paula Scher sketches identities in pencil first (the Citibank mark, 1998, began as a napkin sketch in a first meeting, and she has said it took a few seconds and decades of practice). Bierut's numbered notebooks (kept since 1982, more than 100 volumes) are a quantity record. Jonathan Hoefler and Tobias Frere-Jones drew typefaces glyph by glyph: a text family like Gotham (2000) grew to 66 styles, each with hundreds of glyphs, and every glyph was drawn and respaced by hand. Consequence: at 8.3 the fluency target is ten compositions or thirty thumbnails; at 8.6 three survive; one ships.

### 4.10 Making as thinking

Basis: Schön (3.1), Geneplore (2.2). Apple's industrial design studio under Ive built CNC-machined models of nearly every variant (Isaacson, 2011); Pixar storyboards and cuts every film into story reels with scratch dialogue before animation begins, and reworks the reels until they play (Ed Catmull, Creativity, Inc., 2014). Figma is the sketchbook of product teams because a frame is faster than a sentence. Rauno Freiberg ("Invisible Details of Interaction Design", July 2023, and his craft archive of Vercel work) builds the interaction to find the feeling, then writes the rule. Buck's production method puts styleframes (single still frames that fix the final look of a shot) before any animation. Consequence: the make stage (8.5) starts within the first 20 percent of elapsed time, with the real material (real copy, real photography, real font) and never with lorem ipsum.

### 4.11 The tool mastered to invisibility, then pushed

Basis: chunking (2.11), flow (2.8). John Lasseter's formulation at Pixar: "The art challenges the technology, and the technology inspires the art." Andrew Kramer (Video Copilot, tutorials since 2005, Element 3D 2012) built the After Effects skill of a generation by making the tool disappear and then building plugins to push it. Bruno Simon's 2019 portfolio (a car driven across a WebGL landscape) and his Three.js Journey course (2020) do the same for WebGL. Consequence: a master does not learn a tool during a client job; the tool is learned in practice time (section 11), and on the job the tool is pushed one step past what it did last time.

### 4.12 The critique loop

Basis: selective retention (2.15), reflection (3.1). Pixar's Braintrust (Creativity, Inc., 2014) began on Toy Story 2 (1999): a room of directors and story leads watches the reels, speaks with candour, and has no authority to mandate changes, which leaves the director responsible. Apple's design reviews under Jobs were near-daily visits to the studio (Isaacson, 2011). Pentagram's partners (about two dozen, equal partners, each running a team) present work to each other at partner meetings and critique it. The Bauhaus Vorkurs (Itten, then Albers) built teaching on the crit of made work. Consequence: 8.6 runs a crit with the design-taste tests, in the braintrust form: candour, no mandate, the designer decides.

### 4.13 Rhythm and time

Basis: incubation (2.6), sleep (2.16). Sagmeister closes his studio for one year every seven (first in 2000, then Bali, then 2016 in Mexico City, Tokyo and Schwarzenberg), and his TED talk "The power of time off" (TEDGlobal, Oxford, 2009) argues that the sabbatical's experiments supply the next seven years of work. Paul Graham's "Maker's schedule, manager's schedule" (2009) explains why one meeting destroys an afternoon. Cal Newport's Deep Work (2016) sets the block. Consequence: section 5.3 sets the house rhythm.

### 4.14 The long game

Basis: 2.14 over a career. Rams designed for Braun from 1955 to 1995 and the Vitsoe 606 shelving system (1960) is still sold and still compatible with its first pieces. Hermès (1837) plans in generations. The Row grew without logos or discounting for nearly two decades before its 2024 valuation round. Consequence: a brand decision is tested against a ten-year horizon ("does it still hold in 2036"), which is the long form of design-taste's "will it still hold in 2029" test.

### 4.15 Intuition trained by analysis

Basis: 2.11, 2.12, 3.1. Schön's practitioners act fast because they have reflected slowly. Adriaan de Groot's chess studies (1946; English edition 1965) found that masters consider few moves because recognition has already pruned the tree. Murch (In the Blink of an Eye, 1992; second edition 2001) counted cuts and blinks, derived the rule of six with its weights (emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4), and then cuts by feel. Consequence: analysis is done in practice time and in the reflect stage (8.9), never during the make stage, so that the make stage can be fast.

### 4.16 Play and seriousness, and the readiness to be plain

Basis: 2.16 (activating positive mood). Paul Rand's essay "Design and the play instinct" (A Designer's Art, 1985) argues that play within rules produces form. Scher's TED talk (2008) distinguishes serious play, which produces her best work, from solemnity, which produces her worst. Naoto Fukasawa's "without thought" workshops (from 1998) and his wall-mounted CD player for Muji (1999) design for the action the body already performs. The readiness to be plain is the other face: Miuccia Prada's black nylon (1984) and "ugly chic", Phoebe Philo's Céline (2008 to 2018) and her 2023 return, Muji's entire catalogue. Consequence: the work is allowed to be plain when plainness is the idea, and the crit asks "is this plain or is this empty" (design-taste's "is there one thing you will remember tomorrow" test).

## 5. How masters function day to day

Verified from interviews, books and talks; the habit is given with its source and the house setting derived from it.

### 5.1 The notebook habit

Michael Bierut has kept numbered notebooks since 1982, when he joined Vignelli Associates, and had filled more than 100 by the mid 2010s; each is the same format, which removes the choice of where to write. Leonardo da Vinci's surviving notebook pages number about 7,200 (the Codex Leicester alone, 72 pages, sold to Bill Gates in 1994 for about 30.8 million dollars). Paula Scher's identities begin in pencil, and the Citibank mark was first drawn on a napkin in the first client meeting (1998). House setting: one notebook per designer, same format, dated pages, carried to every meeting; the sketch goes in the notebook before it goes in Figma.

### 5.2 The reference wall and the library

Are.na (founded 2011 by Charles Broskoski and collaborators) is the digital reference board most studios use because it has no algorithmic feed and blocks are connected by hand. Physical boards persist at Buck, at Pentagram and in fashion ateliers because a wall is read peripherally all day. Peter Saville's method is the library: the Power, Corruption and Lies sleeve (New Order, 1983) is Henri Fantin-Latour's A Basket of Roses from the National Gallery, taken from the gallery postcard; the colour-code alphabet beside it is Saville's own system. David Carson collects found type and photographs signage. House setting: one Are.na channel per client with three to five references, each annotated with the borrowed relation in one line (2.5, 4.8); the channel is read at the start of the frame stage and closed during the make stage (3.13).

### 5.3 Morning, deep work and review cadence

Graham's maker schedule (2009) and Newport's Deep Work (2016) converge on blocks of three to four hours with no meeting inside them. Pixar's "dailies" review animation every morning in front of the director; Apple's design studio under Jobs was reviewed nearly daily; Linear runs two-week cycles with one owner per issue. House setting: designers hold one protected block of at least three hours before 13:00 with notifications off; the studio crit is fixed at the same hour on the same two days each week; client reviews are never scheduled inside the block. A creative director's own calendar follows the same rule on at least three days a week or the director stops designing within a quarter.

### 5.4 How they consume outside design

Steve Jobs and Jony Ive held long walking conversations (Isaacson, 2011). Kenya Hara's thinking is built on Japan itself: Muji's "Horizon" campaign (2003) photographed in the Uyuni salt flat and Mongolian grassland came from his reading of emptiness in landscape. Walter Murch worked for years on the Titius-Bode relation in astronomy and translated Curzio Malaparte (The Bird That Swallowed Its Cage, 2013). Roger Deakins trained in graphic design and photography at Bath before film. Kris Sowersby's Klim essays trace each typeface to printing history. House setting: the ratio of looking to making (5.12) assigns at least a third of the looking to art, architecture, film, literature, science, nature and travel. For Mask360 that means Mumbai's Art Deco, Jaipur's city grid and jantar mantar instruments, Goa's Portuguese houses, Dubai's 1970s modernism (the World Trade Centre, 1979) and Abu Dhabi's Louvre (2017) are in the library before any luxury-brand reference.

### 5.5 Fear and taste anxiety

Ira Glass's "the gap" (a 2009 interview widely transcribed) names the condition: taste outruns ability for years, and most people quit in the gap. Steven Pressfield's The War of Art (2002) names the daily avoidance as resistance and prescribes turning up at the same hour regardless. Sagmeister's first sabbatical (2000) was triggered by the fear that the work was repeating itself. House setting: fear is handled by procedure. The first move of the day is making, never checking; the crit separates the work from the person by naming the element and the fix (design-taste section 8); and the "plain or empty" question (4.16) is asked aloud so plainness is a decision rather than a retreat.

### 5.6 How they decide when something is done

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's rule (Terre des hommes, 1939) that a thing is finished when nothing more can be removed is the subtraction test. Rams's tenth principle is the same. Murch stops when the cut plays for an audience without his noticing the cuts. The house uses written tests so that "done" is a verdict rather than a feeling: the seven tests in design-taste section 4, the discipline batteries in the file numbered 23 in this corpus, and the subtract rule (8.7). A piece is done when every test passes and one more removal breaks it.

### 5.7 How they recover from bad work

Ed Catmull's rule at Pixar is to fail early and fail fast, because every film is bad in its first reels and the Braintrust exists to say so while fixing is cheap. David Fincher reshoots and repeats takes until the scene is right, and treats the repetition as the method rather than the failure. Bierut's How To includes projects that were killed or rejected, presented as part of the practice. House setting: a bad piece is diagnosed in the crit format (idea problem first, craft problem second), the diagnosis is written into the reflect stage (8.9), and the designer starts the next piece the same day.

### 5.8 How much they produce and discard

Beeple: one finished image a day for more than 7,000 days. Zach Lieberman: a daily coding sketch posted since 2016. Hoefler and Frere-Jones: hundreds of glyphs drawn for each of dozens of styles, with whole styles discarded. Pixar: every film is storyboarded and cut into reels several times over before animation. House ratio: ten compositions or thirty thumbnails to three directions to one presented, and the discards are kept in a dated archive, because Simonton's rule (2.14) says the next hit is in the next ten.

### 5.9 How they teach

Paul Rand taught at Yale from 1956 and returned in the 1970s; Josef Albers built the Yale programme on exercises with one variable at a time (Interaction of Color, 1963). Bierut and Scher have taught at Yale and the School of Visual Arts for decades, and both teach by crit of made work. Sowersby teaches through essays that show the drawing decisions of each typeface. Deakins teaches through a forum and a podcast where he answers specific lighting questions. Murch teaches through two books that give the rule and the counts. House setting: a creative director teaches thinking by asking the question (4.2) before giving the answer, by running the crit in the braintrust form, and by publishing the principle set with the reason for each rule (section 7).

### 5.10 Their relationship with clients and briefs

Paul Rand charged Steve Jobs 100,000 dollars for the NeXT logo (1986), delivered one solution in a bound booklet that walked Jobs through the reasoning, and told him that if he wanted options he should talk to other people (Isaacson, 2011). Bierut presents one recommendation and shows the alternatives as the reasons it was chosen. Saville's relationship with Factory Records gave him total freedom and no commercial control, which produced the sleeves and the lateness (the FAC 1 poster of 1978 arrived after the event it advertised). House setting: one idea is presented; the brief is rewritten by the studio and sent back for agreement before work starts (section 10.2); the client sees the "what we are not doing" list as a feature of the proposal.

### 5.11 The studio environment and the body

Jobs restricted access to Ive's studio and the studio was built around light and large tables holding the models (Isaacson, 2011). Rams designed his own house in Kronberg (1971) as a working example of his principles. Hara's Nippon Design Center office is quiet and object-led. Murch edits standing, which he compares in In the Blink of an Eye to a surgeon or a cook, because the body stays alert and the editor can step back from the screen. Scher and Frere-Jones draw by hand first: Frere-Jones draws glyphs in pencil and digitises after, and Scher's identities start on paper. House setting: daylight on the working tables, a quiet rule during blocks, real objects of the client's category on the table (the stone sample, the bottle, the menu, the swatch), a standing position for editing passes, and a pencil sketch before the first Figma frame.

### 5.12 The ratio of looking to making

A working master spends more time looking than a novice assumes. Deakins reads before listing; Murch watches before cutting; Blazy sits in the atelier before drawing. House ratio across a week: about 30 percent looking (reading, references, the outside diet of 5.4, other people's work in the crit), 50 percent making, 20 percent reflecting and administering. In a project, immersion and framing (8.1, 8.2) take about 25 percent of elapsed time before the first composition exists.

### 6.2 Type design

The cycle is drawing, then spacing, then testing, then kerning, in that order, and it starts from the control characters: the lowercase n and o (which set the stem, the bowl, the x-height and the standard sidebearings), the uppercase H and O (cap height and round overshoot), then a, e, d, p and v. Walter Tracy's Letters of Credit (1986) gives the spacing method: set the sidebearings of n and o first and derive the rest. Tobias Frere-Jones (Frere-Jones Type, 2015; Retina for the Wall Street Journal, 2016) draws in pencil before digitising. Jonathan Hoefler (Hoefler&Co, acquired by Monotype in 2021) tests every family in running text before release. Matthew Carter designed for the worst conditions first: Bell Centennial (1978) for telephone directories, Verdana and Georgia (1996) for screens. Kris Sowersby (Klim Type Foundry, Wellington, since 2005; Söhne, 2019) publishes the historical reasoning for each face. Dinamo (Basel and Berlin, since 2013; ABC Diatype, 2019) treats a typeface as a system with variable axes rather than a set of styles. See 03-typography.md sections 1 and 4 for anatomy and spacing.

### 6.3 Product and UI

Rams's ten principles (section 7.4) are a question list: is it useful, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough to the last detail. Ive's additions, from his interviews and Isaacson: care, and the unseen (the inside of the machine finished as well as the outside). Apple's Human Interface Guidelines took their three themes (clarity, deference, depth) from the iOS 7 reset (2013); the September 2025 update added Liquid Glass guidance (7.6). Karri Saarinen wrote Airbnb's Design Language System in 2016 and founded Linear (2019) on "quality is our first principle" and opinionated software (7.10). Rasmus Andersson designed Inter (2017) and Figma's early interface. Rauno Freiberg's "Invisible Details of Interaction Design" (2023) is the method of building the interaction to find the feeling. Emil Kowalski (Sonner, Vaul, the "Animations on the Web" course, 2024) and Paco Coursey (cmdk; Linear) are design engineers whose thinking happens in code. Jordan Singer's Diagram (acquired by Figma in 2023) led Figma's AI features. Mike Matas (Push Pop Press, 2011; Facebook Paper, 2014) and Soleio Cuervo (the Facebook Like button; Dropbox design) built interfaces from physical metaphors. Katie Dill (Stripe, head of design since 2021; earlier Airbnb and Lyft) runs quality through reviews of the live product rather than the mock-up; Julie Zhuo (Facebook, The Making of a Manager, 2019) wrote the critique and management method for product design. Brian Chesky's eleven-star exercise (Masters of Scale, with Reid Hoffman) extrapolates one moment of the experience from one star to eleven and then finds the feasible point on the curve. Bret Victor (7.2) and Alan Kay (the Dynabook, 1972; "the best way to predict the future is to invent it", 1971) set the horizon; Bill Buxton (3.9) sets the sketch. See 06-ui-design-craft.md and 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md.

### 6.4 UX and research

Don Norman (The Design of Everyday Things, 1988; revised 2013) gives the vocabulary: affordance (what an object permits), signifier (what tells you so), mapping, feedback, conceptual model. Jared Spool's "$300 million button" (2009) is the case for removing a step. Teresa Torres (Continuous Discovery Habits, 2021) structures discovery as weekly customer contact and an opportunity solution tree (outcome at the top, opportunities beneath, solutions beneath those, experiments at the leaves). Erika Hall (Just Enough Research, 2013) sets the minimum: ask before building, and ask the question whose answer would change the design. Jesse James Garrett's five planes (The Elements of User Experience, 2002: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface) order the decisions. Indi Young (Mental Models, 2008) listens for the user's reasoning rather than their requests. See 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md and 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md.

### 6.5 Motion

Saul Bass's method (Vertigo, 1958; Anatomy of a Murder, 1959; Psycho, 1960) finds one symbol that holds the film and animates only that. Kyle Cooper (Se7en, 1995; Imaginary Forces, 1996; Prologue Films, 2003) builds the title sequence as a short film with its own arc. Buck (since 2004) locks styleframes before animation, so the animation problem is movement alone. Ordinary Folk (Vancouver) and ManvsMachine (London, since 2007) think in material and physics first. Ash Thorp and GMUNK (Bradley Munkowitz; Tron: Legacy, 2010; Oblivion, 2013) design screen graphics as if they were real instruments. Beeple's everydays and Andrew Kramer's tutorials are the practice regimes. Studio Dumbar's DEMO festival (first edition 7 November 2019 on the 80 screens of Amsterdam Centraal; 2022 edition on more than 5,000 screens across the Netherlands; 2025 Cities edition with 5,334 submissions and 600 selected works) made performative type, type whose meaning is in its movement, a public medium; creative director Liza Enebeis. Pixar's appeal principle, taught by John Lasseter and Glen Keane, holds that a character is appealing when its inner life is readable in its line, which is why Keane draws the thought before the pose. See 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md and 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md.

### 6.6 Editing and film

Murch's rule of six with its weights (4.15) and the blink as the natural cut point. Thelma Schoonmaker has cut every Scorsese feature since Raging Bull (1980), reviewing dailies with him and cutting to the music he chose before the shoot. Kirk Baxter and Angus Wall won consecutive editing Oscars for The Social Network (2010 film) and The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo (2011 film), working within Fincher's method of many takes and a camera that moves only when motivated. Paul Machliss cut Baby Driver (2017) on set, so that Edgar Wright could reshoot to the music's beat the same day. Vashi Nedomansky publishes shot-count and cut-rate analyses that make editorial rhythm measurable. Deakins thinks from the light source and its direction before the lens; Emmanuel Lubezki thinks in natural light and wide lenses (The Revenant, 2015). Fincher thinks in control and repetition: the opening scene of The Social Network ran to 99 takes. Jonathan Glazer thinks in the image as evidence: The Zone of Interest (2023) was shot with about ten fixed, concealed cameras inside the house and no crew in the rooms, so the actors played whole scenes without a set around them. See 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md.

### 6.7 Fashion and creative direction

Abloh (4.4, 4.8). Jonathan Anderson: surreal objects inside classical tailoring at Loewe; appointed at Dior in 2025, with a menswear debut in June 2025 and a first women's show on 1 October 2025. Matthieu Blazy: material immersion at Bottega Veneta, then Chanel, with a debut at the Grand Palais during the October 2025 shows. Phoebe Philo: reduction, and a return in 2023 with her own label sold in drops. Miuccia Prada with Raf Simons as co-creative directors (since 2020): the plain made strange. Demna: Balenciaga (2015 to 2025), then Gucci from 2025, opening with a lookbook in September 2025 and a first runway show in early 2026. Hedi Slimane: one silhouette and one photographic style across Dior Homme, Saint Laurent and Celine (2018 to 2024). Jacquemus: scale as the idea. Pharrell Williams at Louis Vuitton menswear (appointed February 2023, first show on the Pont Neuf, June 2023): the show as cultural event. Alessandro Michele (Gucci 2015 to 2022; Valentino from 2024): maximalism as a method, in which accumulation follows a system of recurring motifs rather than taste alone. Sabyasachi Mukherjee (Kolkata, since 1999): world building, with the store built as a museum of the brand's references and the jewellery, textiles and interiors sharing one archive. For Mask360's clients in Jaipur jewellery and Mumbai couture, Sabyasachi's archive-first method is the local reference. See 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md section 8.

### 6.8 AI product design, as of October 2026

The interface problem of this decade is designing for a system whose output is uncertain and whose agency competes with the user's. Jakob Nielsen (Nielsen Norman Group, 18 June 2023) called it the third user-interface paradigm after batch processing and command-based interaction: intent-based outcome specification, where the user states the outcome and the system chooses the method, and he listed its costs (opaque results, hard refinement). Emily Campbell's The Shape of AI pattern library organises the responses into six families (wayfinders, inputs, tuners, governors, trust builders, identifiers) with about 57 patterns when checked for this file. Amelia Wattenberger's "Why Chatbots Are Not the Future" (2023) argues for interfaces that expose controls and intermediate states; Maggie Appleton's "The Expanding Dark Forest and Generative AI" (2023) and her language-model sketches argue for designing the proof of human presence; Linus Lee's prototypes (Notion AI and his own experiments) treat the model's latent space as a navigable material. Jordan Singer's work at Figma put generation inside the design canvas rather than in a chat. The published principle sets: no formal interface-principle document from Anthropic's design team was found as of October 2026; the observable decisions are the Artifacts side panel (2024), which keeps the conversation and the made thing in separate panes, and the Claude Design research preview (April 2026) with chat on the left and a live canvas on the right. OpenAI's observable decisions are the single input, Canvas (October 2024) as a side document, and the hardware direction after the io acquisition with Jony Ive's LoveFrom (May 2025, about 6.5 billion dollars). Google's is Material 3 Expressive (7.7) and Gemini inside existing surfaces. Design consequences the house applies: show uncertainty where it exists (confidence, sources, the ability to see the intermediate step); make every generated action reversible; keep the user's artefact separate from the model's talk; stream so the wait is legible; and never let the model's default taste set the brand's (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 5).

### 6.9 Creative technology and generative art

Refik Anadol treats data as pigment (Unsupervised at MoMA, 2022 to 2023); his Dataland museum at The Grand LA was scheduled to open on 20 June 2026 after a delay from 2025, and its status should be checked before citing it as open. Zach Lieberman has posted a daily coding sketch since 2016, and the practice is the method: one constraint, one hour, one post. Casey Reas and Ben Fry built Processing (2001, at the MIT Media Lab) so that sketching in code was possible. Golan Levin (Carnegie Mellon's Studio for Creative Inquiry) teaches computation as a drawing instrument. Mario Klingemann (Memories of Passersby I, 2018; Botto, 2021) designs the system and lets it generate. Holly Herndon (PROTO, 2019; Holly+, 2021) built a model of her own voice and designed its governance. Claire Silver works with AI as a collaborator and names it in the credit. Sougwen Chung draws with robots (the D.O.U.G. series, since 2015) and treats the machine's error as a mark. The shared discipline is the sketch a day: the system is the artwork, and the daily sketch is how the system is tuned.

### 6.10 Web

Bruno Simon's portfolio (2019) and Three.js Journey (2020) made the browser a physical space. Locomotive (Montreal; the Locomotive Scroll library) thinks in scroll as a timeline. Active Theory (Los Angeles) and Immersive Garden (Paris) think in the loading sequence, the first second, and the frame budget. The structure they share: define the one interaction that carries the idea, prototype it in the browser in the first week, and build everything else as still as possible around it (design-taste section 5, motion). See 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md.

### 6.11 Japanese thinking

Hara's ex-formation (seminars at Musashino Art University from 2004; Ex-formation, English edition 2015) is the opposite of information: making a known thing unknown again so it can be seen. His emptiness (White, 2010) is a vessel the viewer fills. Fukasawa's without thought is design for the action the body performs unconsciously (the CD player pulled by its cord). Jasper Morrison and Fukasawa's Super Normal (exhibition 2006, book 2007) honours objects that disappear into use. Wabi sabi (Leonard Koren's 1994 account) is the beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete, and is the reference behind the "imperfection on purpose" rule in design-taste section 2. Ma is the interval: the pause between elements that carries meaning, which is the spacing rule behind every luxury page.

### 6.12 Scandinavian and Dutch rationalism

Wim Crouwel (Total Design, 1963; the New Alphabet, 1967) designed from the system outward, so that every poster for the Stedelijk followed one grid. Gert Dumbar's studio (since 1977) built identities for the Dutch Railways and the Dutch police as systems, and under Liza Enebeis the studio's identities became living systems (generative marks whose rules are the identity). Irma Boom makes the book as object: the SHV Think Book (1996) is 2,136 pages made over five years; her Chanel No 5 book (2013) is embossed with no ink. Stockholm Design Lab's reductive identities (SAS, Askul) apply the same discipline in Sweden. The shared structure: design the programme (Gerstner, 7.8), then let the programme produce the pieces.

## 7. How masters create principles and systems

### 7.1 The cycle

A principle set is made in six steps, and every set dissected below followed them whether or not its author named them.

1. Observe what works in finished work. Rams wrote his principles in the late 1970s after two decades at Braun, looking at his own objects and at the "impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises" around them (Vitsoe's account).
2. Name it. A principle is a short sentence with a verb: "start with user needs", "good design is honest".
3. Test it against counterexamples. Does any admired piece break it? If so, either the piece is wrong or the principle is.
4. Codify it so that it excludes something (7.2). A principle that nothing could violate is a value.
5. Enforce it in review. The crit cites the principle by name; a piece that breaks one is sent back with the principle quoted.
6. Revise it when the work outgrows it. Apple's HIG themes of 2013 were revised in 2025 (7.6); Material's paper metaphor of 2014 became Expressive in 2025 (7.7); GOV.UK added an eleventh principle in 2025 (7.11). A principle set with no revision date is a monument.

### 7.2 Principles as decision tools and values as aspiration

Bret Victor's "Inventing on Principle" (CUSEC, Montreal, 20 January 2012) distinguishes having a principle (a specific thing you believe is wrong with the world, which you can recognise being violated and act on) from having a style or a taste. His own principle, that creators need an immediate connection to what they create, is operational: a tool that makes the creator wait violates it, and he can show the violation. The rule the house takes from this: a principle must forbid something. "Be bold" forbids nothing and is a value. "One typeface per brand" forbids a second family and is a principle. "Clarity" is a value until it is written as "every control is labelled in words; no icon stands alone", at which point it forbids the unlabelled icon. Test every line in a principle set by asking what it would send back in a crit; a line that sends nothing back is deleted or rewritten.

### 7.3 Rams's ten principles

Written in the late 1970s, after Rams had led Braun's design from 1961 (he joined in 1955 and stayed to 1995) and designed the 606 shelving (1960) and 620 chair (1962) for Vitsoe. The ten, as Vitsoe publishes them: good design is innovative, makes a product useful, is aesthetic, makes a product understandable, is unobtrusive, is honest, is long-lasting, is thorough down to the last detail, is environmentally friendly, is as little design as possible. In use at Braun and Vitsoe they were a filter applied to every proposal: each principle names a way a product can fail (dishonest, obtrusive, short-lived). The tenth is the subtraction move (4.5) written as a rule. Ive has acknowledged Rams's influence publicly, and the lineage from the Braun T3 pocket radio (1958) to the first iPod (2001) is the clearest case of a principle set crossing companies and decades.

### 7.4 The Vignelli canon

Massimo Vignelli published The Vignelli Canon in 2010 as a free PDF. Its first part is the intangibles: semantics (the meaning of the thing, found before any form is drawn), syntactics (the consistency of the parts: grid, type, colour, scale), pragmatics (whether it works for the reader). Then discipline, appropriateness, ambiguity (as a value, used on purpose), "design is one" (the same thinking from a spoon to a city), visual power, intellectual elegance, timelessness, responsibility, equity. The second part is the tangibles: paper sizes, grids, typefaces (the few he used), flush left, scale, texture, colour, layout, sequence, binding, identity. The canon is a working example of 7.2: "timelessness" forbids the trend; "discipline" forbids the second typeface; the tangibles turn each into a setting.

### 7.5 The Pixar story rules and the Braintrust

The "22 rules of storytelling" began as a series of tweets by Emma Coats, then a Pixar storyboard artist, posted over about two months in 2011 and compiled by others into a list; they are her distillation of what she heard in story rooms rather than an official Pixar document, and Pixar has never published them as such. The institution behind them is the Braintrust (Creativity, Inc., 2014): the film's reels are screened to a room of directors and story leads, who say what is not working with candour; the room has no authority to mandate a fix, and the director leaves with notes and full responsibility. Two of Coats's rules are moves from section 4 written as story rules: "simplify, focus, combine characters, hop over detours" is subtraction; "come up with your 4th, 5th, 12th idea; the first thing that comes to mind is usually the obvious" is quantity then selection and the first-idea trap (9.1).

### 7.6 Apple's Human Interface Guidelines and the 2025 revision

The iOS 7 redesign (2013) set three themes for the HIG: clarity (legible type, precise icons, focus on function), deference (the interface serves the content; fluid motion and a light interface keep content primary), depth (layers and realistic motion convey hierarchy). The HIG was restructured into a single cross-platform document in 2022. At WWDC on 9 June 2025 Apple announced Liquid Glass, a translucent material that refracts and reflects its surroundings, for iOS 26, iPadOS 26, macOS Tahoe 26, watchOS 26 and tvOS 26; the HIG was updated on 9 September 2025 with guidance on using the material semantically (for controls and navigation, floating above content), on legibility and contrast, and on avoiding unnecessary glass layers; the systems shipped on 15 September 2025. For the house the revision is a lesson in 7.1 step 6, and the material itself is on design-taste's peaking list, so Mask360 uses glass only where the material is the concept and once.

### 7.7 Material Design from metaphor to Expressive

Material Design (Google I/O, June 2014) was built on a metaphor: surfaces of paper and ink with physical light and shadow, so that every elevation and shadow had a rule. Material You and Material 3 (2021) moved to dynamic colour drawn from the user's wallpaper. Material 3 Expressive appeared in May 2025 (its announcement post was published early, before Google I/O on 20 May 2025, and taken down, then republished). Google cited 46 research studies with more than 18,000 participants, and claimed that participants found key interface elements up to four times faster and that users aged 45 and over performed on a par with younger users. The arc from paper to expressive is the arc of a principle set revised as the work outgrew it: the metaphor had become decoration, and the revision named emotion as the function.

### 7.8 Gerstner's Designing Programmes

Karl Gerstner's Designing Programmes (1964) holds that the designer designs the programme (the set of rules and parameters) and the programme produces the pieces. His tool is the morphological box, adapted from Fritz Zwicky: parameters in rows (typeface, size, colour, position, material), options in columns, and a design as one path through the box. The box makes the solution space explicit, exposes which combinations have never been tried, and prevents the designer from mistaking one path for the only one. It is the direct ancestor of the design token (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md) and of the living identity (7.12).

### 7.9 Alexander's pattern language

A Pattern Language (1977) gives 253 patterns from "independent regions" down to "things from your life", each written as a context, a problem stated with its forces, and a solution, linked to the larger patterns it completes and the smaller ones that complete it. The form is why patterns are thinking tools: the problem field carries the evidence, and a pattern without one is a style. Design systems that copy the component library and drop the problem statements lose the thinking.

### 7.10 Airbnb's DLS and Linear's quality principle

Karri Saarinen's "Building a Visual Language" (Airbnb Design, 2016) explains why the Design Language System was written: as the team grew, every designer brought a style and the old screens never died. The DLS set four principles (unified, universal, iconic, conversational), one typeface and a tight palette, and documented components as living things with owners. Alex Schleifer was Airbnb's VP of design in that period and spoke publicly on the collaboration tooling; his role in authoring the DLS itself is not documented in the sources found. Saarinen co-founded Linear (2019) and wrote its method: "quality is our first principle", opinionated software (purpose-built, so that it does the heavy lifting rather than making users its designers), fixed-length cycles (two weeks most common), one named owner per issue, and specs that state the why, the what and the how in brief. The lesson for a principle set: Linear's rules each forbid something (a flexible workflow, an ownerless issue, a long spec).

### 7.11 Stripe, Polaris and GOV.UK

Stripe has not published a formal design-principles page as far as could be verified in October 2026; its method is visible in Katie Dill's public talks and in the practice of reviewing the live product (the "walk the store" ritual she has described in interviews). Shopify's Polaris publishes experience values (including "familiar", and a value on attention to detail and transparency as the basis of trust) and a component library with usage rules. The UK Government Design Principles were first published on 3 April 2012 and were updated on 2 April 2025 to add an eleventh: start with user needs; do less; design with data; do the hard work to make it simple; iterate, then iterate again; this is for everyone; understand context; build digital services rather than websites; be consistent without being uniform; make things open because it makes things better; minimise environmental impact. "Start with user needs" was called the first and most important by Ben Terrett, then GDS head of design, and the guidance warns that what users ask for is not always what they need. Every one of the eleven forbids something: "do less" forbids the feature; "consistent without being uniform" forbids the identical template.

### 7.12 Living identities and editorial systems

Studio Dumbar's identities under Liza Enebeis (Alzheimer Nederland, 2019, among others) are generative: the identity is the rule set and the software that applies it, and the output changes while the identity does not. Bureau Borsche's editorial systems for Zeit Magazin and the Bayerische Staatsoper fix the structure (grid, typeface, hierarchy) and allow one gesture per issue. Both are Gerstner's programme in production. For a Mask360 hospitality client the equivalent is an identity defined as a rule set (the mark's behaviour, the type scale, the two colours, the one motion) that the hotel's own team can apply for years.

### 7.13 The Mask360 design-taste rules as a live principle set

The house layer in design-taste section 7 is a principle set built by the cycle in 7.1, and it is the house's working example. Each line forbids something: Space Grotesk only forbids a second family; 300 body with 500 bold forbids the medium weight; two colours plus one accent forbids a palette; 70 visual to 30 text forbids the deck wall of words; no em dashes forbids a punctuation habit; "execute the brief only" forbids the unrequested extra. The set has an observation base (the canon file), a test battery (section 4 of the skill), an enforcement format (the critique format in section 8 of the skill) and a revision file dated 2026 (currency-2026.md). When writing a principle set for a client, copy this shape and never the content.

### 7.14 Writing a principle set for a client in 90 minutes

| Minutes | Step | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 15 | Observe: lay out the client's five best pieces and five from the canon world (design-taste worlds.md); name what the best share in one line each | 10 observation lines |
| 15 to 30 | Name: turn the shared observations into candidate principles with a verb | 8 to 12 candidates |
| 30 to 45 | Test: for each candidate, find one admired piece that breaks it; keep the candidate only if the piece is worse for breaking it | 6 to 8 survivors |
| 45 to 60 | Forbid: rewrite each survivor until it names what it excludes ("one image per screen" rather than "image-led") | 5 to 7 principles |
| 60 to 75 | Settings: attach one measurable setting to each (the typeface, the margin ratio, the duration, the colour count) | A one-page token sheet |
| 75 to 90 | Enforcement: write the crit question for each principle ("which element would this principle send back?") and the revision date | The crit card and a date 12 months out |

Five to seven principles is the ceiling; Rams needed ten for a whole discipline and GOV.UK needed eleven for a government. A client brand needs fewer.

### 7.15 A motion language in five rules and a type system in one page

A motion language is written as five rules, each with its setting, because five is the number a team remembers: the one move that carries the brand, the easing family (one curve for entrances, one for exits), the duration band (for example 200 to 400 ms for interface, 600 to 1,200 ms for narrative), what never moves, and the stagger rule. The method and the settings are in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md sections 3 to 5. A type system on one page states the family and the weights in use, the scale (the ratio and the number of steps), the measure and leading per step, the two or three roles (display, text, micro), and the forbidden settings (tracked caps on every label, the medium weight). The method is in 03-typography.md section 7.

## 8. The operating system of a master designer

The procedure Claude runs on a creative task. Time shares are of elapsed project time and are the house defaults; a two-hour task and a two-month identity use the same shares. Each step names its inputs, outputs, the techniques from sections 2 to 7, the failure mode, and how a master catches it.

| Step | Share of time | Inputs | Outputs | Techniques |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 Immerse | 15 percent | Brief, subject, place, material, audience, the canon world | Immersion notes; the single question drafted | 4.1, 2.7 preparation, 5.4 outside diet |
| 8.2 Frame | 10 percent | Immersion notes | Three frames in Dorst's form; one chosen; the single idea in one sentence; the reference board with borrowed relations | 3.4, 4.2, 4.3, 2.5 |
| 8.3 Generate wide | 15 percent | The frame, the board (then closed) | 10 compositions or 30 thumbnails across at least four categories; one direction per inversion, one cross-domain | 2.1, 2.14, 4.4, 4.7, 4.8, 2.16 walk |
| 8.4 Constrain | 5 percent | The directions | The system: tokens, the "what we are not doing" list, the constraint set | 2.10, 4.6, 7.14 |

### 8.1 Immerse

Read the brief, then the subject (the hotel's floor plan, the jeweller's stone sources, the developer's site photographs at three times of day), the place (the street, the climate, the light), the material (the stone sample, the paper stock, the fabric), the audience (who holds the phone, in which language, at which hour), and the canon world (design-taste worlds.md). Produce immersion notes in the notebook and a first draft of the single question. Failure mode: skipping to the board, which loads other designers' solutions before the problem is understood (3.13). A master catches it by refusing to open a reference until the single question is written.

### 8.2 Frame

Write three frames in the form "if we look at this as X, then Y follows" (3.4). Choose one; write the single idea as a sentence that describes a feeling (design-taste section 4). Build the reference board of three to five items and write the borrowed relation for each (2.5). Close the board. Failure mode: one frame, chosen because it arrived first (3.3, 9.1). A master catches it because the rule is three frames, and the second and third are often where the idea is.

### 8.3 Generate wide

Walk first (2.16). Then produce the count without judgement (2.1): ten compositions or thirty thumbnails, in a loose medium (3.8), spread across at least four categories (flexibility), with at least one inversion (4.4), one cross-domain borrowing (4.7) and one deliberately plain direction (4.16). Failure mode: judging during generation, which collapses the count to three safe options; and producing ten variants of one idea, which is vertical transformation disguised as fluency. A master catches it by counting categories, not sheets.

### 8.4 Constrain

Write the system: the tokens (type, colour, spacing, motion, image treatment), the constraint set in the form "we will not use", and the "what we are not doing" list of the category's tropes (design-taste section 4 step 3). Failure mode: constraining before generating (which makes the constraints inherited rather than chosen) or never constraining (which makes the make stage a negotiation). A master catches it by checking that every constraint excludes something that appeared in 8.3.

### 8.5 Make

Sketch the three directions, prototype the one interaction or styleframe the key shot, then build the real thing with real copy, real photography and the real typeface, inside the first fifth of the make time. Work in the loose medium until the structure holds and only then in the precise one (3.8). Failure mode: pixel-perfect too early; lorem ipsum, which hides every typographic problem; polishing a direction that has not passed critique. A master catches it by refusing to open the precise tool before the sketch has survived a squint.

### 8.6 Critique

Run the tests: the seven in design-taste section 4 (cover the logo; would Ssense, Aesop or The Gentlewoman run it; four hundred hours; one thing remembered tomorrow; anything moving without need; any word selling; 2026 and 2029), the discipline battery in the file numbered 23, and the squint (blur the eyes or zoom to 25 percent: does the hierarchy survive). Hold the crit in the braintrust form (4.12). Failure mode: the crit as a list of nitpicks, which design-taste section 8 forbids; the crit with a mandate, which removes the designer's responsibility; the crit by the client before the studio's own. A master catches it by starting every crit with the verdict in one line and the idea problem before the craft problem.

### 8.7 Subtract

Remove elements until the piece breaks, put the last one back, then remove one accessory (design-taste section 4 step 6). Failure mode: subtracting the idea along with the decoration, which produces the empty piece (4.16). A master catches it with the "plain or empty" question and the "one thing remembered" test.

### 8.8 Polish

The two-pixel alignment, the optical kerning of the display line, the easing curve and duration, the cut point on the blink, the ending of the page. Failure mode: polishing as avoidance of a structural problem the crit named; and polishing on a tired eye at the end of the day. A master catches it by polishing in the morning block and by re-running the squint after every polish pass.

### 8.9 Reflect

Write what the work taught in the notebook, revise any principle the work outgrew (7.1 step 6), add the piece and its references to the library with the borrowed relations named, and record the ratio produced to shipped. Failure mode: skipping reflection because the next brief has arrived. A master catches it by scheduling reflection as the first 30 minutes of the next project rather than the last of this one.

## 9. Creativity blockers and how masters avoid them

| Blocker | What it is | Cognitive basis | How masters avoid it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9.1 The first idea trap | The first arrival is taken as the best because it is the most available | Mednick's steep hierarchy (2.13); Jansson and Smith fixation (3.13) | Log the first idea, set it aside, require three frames and ten compositions; Coats's rule of the fourth, fifth and twelfth idea |
| 9.2 Reference regurgitation | The reference's surface reproduced with no borrowed relation named | Surface mapping (2.5); fixation on the example | Name the relation; close the board before making; the 3 percent change declared (4.8) |
| 9.3 The fear of plain | Adding elements because plainness feels like absence | Taste anxiety (5.5) | The "plain or empty" question; Prada, Philo and Muji as permission; the one-thing-remembered test |
| 9.4 The clever trap | The idea exists to show the designer is clever rather than to serve the brief | Bisociation without a purpose (2.4) | Bierut's one idea explained to the client; "is any word selling" extended to "is any element showing off" |
| 9.5 Perfectionism and the unfinished | Vertical refinement before the structure holds; nothing ships | Goel (3.8); flow miscalibrated (2.8) | Loose medium first; a shipping date in the brief; Linear's rule that details must be correct without being perfect |
| 9.6 Decoration as avoidance | Ornament added where the idea is weak | Polish as avoidance (8.8) | Idea problem first in the crit; subtract before polish |
| 9.7 Over-research | Reading replaces making because reading feels safe | Preparation without the make loop (3.1) | The 25 percent cap on immersion and framing (5.12); make within the first fifth of make time |
| 9.8 Under-constraint | Freedom produces the habitual answer | Stokes (2.10) | Add a constraint when flat; write "we will not use" before making |
| 9.9 The committee | Group generation and group approval average the work | Diehl and Stroebe (2.17); the braintrust's no-mandate rule | Generate alone in writing; converge together; one owner decides (Linear, Pixar) |
| 9.10 The trend trap | Adopting what is peaking because it is visible | Currency (design-taste section 6) | The 2029 test; the currency file read before the board |
| 9.11 Tool seduction | The tool's new feature becomes the idea | 4.11 | Learn tools in practice time; on the job push one step only |
| 9.12 AI default convergence | The model's median taste (cream, serif, terracotta; the symmetrical hero) becomes the output | Steep hierarchy in the model (2.13); see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 5 | Prompt from the named reference and the borrowed relation; reject the first generation on principle; the cheap-tells list |
| 9.13 Burnout and the pace problem | The maker schedule destroyed; no incubation; decisions made tired | 2.6, 2.16, 4.13 | Protected blocks; the walk; sleep before sign-off; the sabbatical in some form (a week a year with no brief) |
| 9.14 The taste plateau | Output stabilises at competent; the eye stops improving | Perceptual learning needs new discriminations (2.12); the library stops widening (2.9) | Change the diet (5.4); paired-comparison drills at a harder level; a crit from outside the studio; one project a year in a discipline the designer does not know |

The plateau deserves one more line: it is broken by discomfort on purpose. Sagmeister's sabbatical, Abloh's move from architecture to fashion, Glazer's move from commercials to a film with no crew in the room, and Blazy's move from a leather house to a tweed house are all the same move.

## 10. Creativity under commercial conditions

### 10.1 Protecting the idea through clients and production

Bierut's method is to present one recommendation with its reasoning and to show the alternatives as what was considered and rejected, so the client decides between a reasoned idea and its absence rather than between three unexplained options. Saville's method with Factory was total creative freedom in exchange for total commercial risk, and it produced both the sleeves and the lateness; the house takes the freedom and adds the deadline. Deakins protects the image by agreeing it in preparation: shot lists and lighting plans worked through with the director (Villeneuve, Mendes, the Coens) before the shoot, so that on the day the decisions are already made and the pressure of the set cannot unmake them. The shared principle: the idea is protected by moving the decision earlier and writing it down.

### 10.2 The brief as a creative instrument

The brief is rewritten by the studio and returned to the client for agreement before any making. It contains the single question (4.2), the single idea in one sentence, the three frames considered, the "what we are not doing" list, the constraint set, the tokens, the tests that will be used to judge the work, and the date. A brief with those nine items turns the presentation from a taste argument into a test result: the client agreed the tests before the work existed. The format is in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md section 2.

### 10.3 How Pentagram partners run a project

Pentagram (founded 1972) is a partnership of about two dozen equal partners, each running a small team and each responsible for their own clients, without account managers between designer and client. The partner is in the first meeting and the last. The work is critiqued by other partners at partner meetings. The economics are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md; the thinking consequence is that the person who frames the problem is the person who presents the answer, which keeps the single idea intact through production.

### 10.4 How Buck runs a job

Buck's production order is brief, then ideation on boards, then styleframes signed off by the client, then an animatic (the timed sequence of still frames with sound), then animation, then polish. The client decision is concentrated at the styleframe, where change is cheap, and the animation stage is protected from redesign because the look is locked. The house applies the same lock to every motion and video job: no animation starts before the styleframe is approved (13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md).

### 10.5 How Fincher runs a set

Fincher's set is a control system: a shot list that is the plan rather than a guide, a camera that holds still unless the movement is motivated, digital capture that permits many takes, and repetition until the take is right. The editor works with him from the first day, and the number of takes is the method rather than a cost overrun. The transferable rule for Mask360 shoots (art-direction skill; 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md) is that control is bought in preparation and spent on the day.

### 10.6 The agency reality and the house process

The agency conditions that destroy the structure are known: the brief that arrives as a deck of competitor screenshots, the three-options request, the client crit before the studio crit, the two-day turnaround that deletes incubation, the account layer that paraphrases the work. The house process in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md and 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md answers each: the rewritten brief (10.2), one idea presented, the studio crit fixed in the calendar, a minimum of one night between generate and present, and the designer in the room.

### 11.1 Self-tests for each move

| Inversion | Which direction is the opposite of the obvious one, and did I make it? |

### 11.3 Running a studio that thinks like this

Rituals, in calendar order: the walk and the block (mornings, no meetings before 13:00 on at least three days); the reference channel review (Monday, 30 minutes, every active client); the crit (two fixed hours a week, braintrust form, verdict first); the principle review (quarterly: which principle did the work outgrow, which rule sent nothing back and should be deleted); the library day (one half-day a quarter spent adding to the outside diet, with the relations written); the annual week with no brief (the house sabbatical in miniature). Objects: real materials of each client's category on the tables; a printed wall per client; daylight. Measures kept per quarter: produced to shipped ratio, categories per generate session, crit verdict distribution (expensive, competent, cheap), and the number of principle revisions.

### 11.4 How a creative director teaches thinking rather than output

Teach the question before the answer: ask "what is the quietest version" and wait. Teach the frame: ask for three before accepting one. Teach the relation: when a designer shows a reference, ask what is borrowed and refuse "the feel". Teach the count: ask how many existed before this one. Teach the crit by running it in the braintrust form and by naming the idea problem before the craft problem, so that the designer learns to see the structure under the surface. Teach reflection by reading the notebook entry aloud at the start of the next project. Teach by example of the body: sketch by hand in the room, stand for the editing pass, close the laptop for the walk. The measure of the teaching is that the designer's self-tests (11.1) are answered without prompting.

### 12.2 Cognitive findings and their design consequences

| Finding | Researcher and year | Measured result | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divergent and convergent thinking are separate | Guilford 1950, 1967 | Four scores: fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration | Separate generation from judgement in time |
| Forms before meanings | Finke, Ward and Smith 1992 | Preinventive forms made before the category was known were judged more creative | Styleframes and compositions before the rationale |
| Blending needs two structured inputs | Fauconnier and Turner 2002 | Emergent structure arises only from specific input spaces | Name two specific spaces in the brief |
| Creativity is bisociation | Koestler 1964 | Two matrices collide | One collision per piece |
| Analogy maps relations | Gentner 1983 | Deep analogies map systems of relations | Borrow the relation, never the surface |
| Creativity is network cooperation | Beaty et al. 2018 | Default, salience and executive network connectivity predicted creativity in 163 people and replicated | Alternate unfocused and evaluative modes deliberately |
| Insight has a run-up | Jung-Beeman et al. 2004; Kounios and Beeman 2015 | Alpha burst about 1.5 s before, gamma about 300 ms before the answer | Look away from the screen on purpose |
| Incubation works under conditions | Sio and Ormerod 2009 | Larger effects for divergent tasks and low-demand fillers | Fill gaps with walking or admin, never another design task |
| Flow has conditions | Csikszentmihalyi 1990 | Clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge slightly above skill | Fast-feedback tools; 90-minute blocks; calibrate challenge |
| The adjacent possible is set by the inventory | Kauffman 2000; Johnson 2010 | Next steps are combinations of what exists | Widen the library beyond design |
| Constraints promote novelty | Stokes 2006; Haught-Tromp 2017 | Constrained rhymes rated more creative | Add a constraint when flat |
| Experts chunk | Chase and Simon 1973 | About 50,000 chunks; no advantage on random boards | Build the library of precedents; recognise, then act |
| Deliberate practice explains part of variance | Macnamara et al. 2014 | 26 percent games, 21 music, 18 sports, 4 education, under 1 profession | Make practice game-like: short tasks, fast feedback, a record |
| The eye is trained by trials | Kellman 1997, 2009 | Hundreds of short trials with feedback build expert discrimination | 50 paired comparisons a week |
| Creative minds have flat hierarchies | Mednick 1962 | Remote associates reachable | Feed the network with outside material |
| Hits scale with output | Simonton 1997 | Linear relation; constant ratio | Ten to three to one |
| Selection matters as much as variation | Campbell 1960 | Blind variation, selective retention | Written tests and a crit ritual |
| Activating positive mood raises creativity | Baas, De Dreu and Nijstad 2008 | Meta-analytic effect for activating positive moods | Treat studio mood as a production input |
| Sleep enables insight | Wagner et al. 2004 | About 59 percent found the hidden rule after sleep against about 23 percent awake | A night between generate and present |
| Walking raises divergent output | Oppezzo and Schwartz 2014 | 81 to 100 percent of walkers more creative; convergent slightly worse | Walk before generating, sit before converging |
| Groups block production | Diehl and Stroebe 1987 | Nominal groups about twice as many ideas | Generate alone, converge together |
| Examples fixate | Jansson and Smith 1991 | Example features reproduced even when told not to | Many references, relations named, board closed |
| Designers co-evolve problem and solution | Dorst and Cross 2001 | Partial solutions reveal the problem | The brief is a draft until round two |
| The frame is the creative unit | Dorst 2015 | Reframing produced the solution in the cases studied | Three frames before choosing |
| Sketching alternates two readings | Goldschmidt 1991 | Seeing as and seeing that in rapid alternation | Sketch by hand; name the mode of each crit comment |
| Loose media support lateral moves | Goel 1995 | Precise media push vertical refinement | Generate loose, refine precise |

## Terms introduced

- abductive reasoning: inference to the best explanation; given a desired value and no known form, proposing a form that would deliver it (Cross, Dorst).
- adjacent possible: the set of states reachable in one step from what currently exists; the edge innovation explores (Kauffman, Johnson).
- affordance: what an object permits a user to do; a signifier is what tells the user so (Norman).
- animatic: a timed sequence of still frames with sound, used to test a motion piece before animation.
- bisociation: the collision of two self-consistent, habitually separate frames of reference that produces a creative act (Koestler).
- blind variation and selective retention: the model of creativity as variations produced without foreknowledge of success, then selected (Campbell).
- Braintrust: Pixar's review ritual in which directors and story leads critique reels with candour and without authority to mandate changes.
- chunking: encoding a complex pattern as one familiar unit, the basis of expert recognition (Chase and Simon).
- co-evolution: the process in which a partial solution reveals a new aspect of the problem, which reshapes the solution (Dorst and Cross).
- conceptual blending: mapping two input mental spaces into a blended space that carries emergent structure (Fauconnier and Turner).
- control characters: the glyphs drawn first in a typeface (n, o, H, O, then a, e, d, p, v) because they set the stems, bowls, heights and spacing of the rest.
- convergent thinking: narrowing to the single correct answer (Guilford).
- cycle: in Linear's method, a fixed-length work period, commonly two weeks, into which unfinished work rolls over.
- dailies: the daily review of the previous day's footage or animation in front of the director.
- deep work: a block of uninterrupted, cognitively demanding work (Newport).
- default mode network: the brain system active in memory, imagination and unfocused thought.
- deliberate practice: effortful activity designed to improve performance, at the edge of ability, with feedback (Ericsson).
- design fixation: adherence to the features of an example solution, including its flaws, even when instructed to avoid them (Jansson and Smith).
- divergent thinking: producing many candidate answers to an open problem, scored by fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration (Guilford).
- elaboration: the detail added to an idea, one of Guilford's four divergent scores.
- eleven-star exercise: extrapolating one moment of an experience from one star to eleven and then selecting the feasible point (Chesky).
- emptiness: Hara's concept of the object or image as a vessel the viewer fills with meaning.
- equal odds rule: a creator's number of hits is a linear function of total output with a roughly constant ratio (Simonton).
- everydays: Beeple's practice of one finished image per day since 1 May 2007.
- executive control network: the brain system that sustains attention and evaluates.
- ex-formation: making a known thing unknown again so that it can be seen (Hara).
- flexibility: the number of categories of idea produced, one of Guilford's four scores.
- flow: absorbed performance under clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge slightly above skill (Csikszentmihalyi).
- fluency: the count of ideas produced, one of Guilford's four scores.
- frame: the statement of what kind of problem this is, in the form "if we look at the situation as X, then Y follows"; reframing is changing it (Schön, Dorst).
- Geneplore: the two-phase model of generation (preinventive structures) and exploration (interpretation) (Finke, Ward and Smith).
- ill-structured problem: a problem whose goal, constraints and operators are not all given (Simon).
- immersion: the move of loading the brief, subject, place, material, audience and canon before any making.
- incubation: a scheduled gap after preparation during which unconscious processing continues (Wallas).
- intent-based outcome specification: the interface paradigm in which the user states the desired outcome and the system chooses the method (Nielsen).
- inversion: the move of generating the opposite, the extreme scale or the sole element as a direction.
- lateral transformation: moving from one idea to a different idea; vertical transformation is refining the same idea (Goel).
- Liquid Glass: Apple's translucent interface material announced on 9 June 2025 for the 26-series operating systems.
- living identity: an identity defined as a rule set and the software that applies it, whose output varies while the identity holds (Studio Dumbar).
- ma: the Japanese concept of the interval or pause between elements that carries meaning.
- maker schedule: a calendar of half-day or full-day blocks without meetings (Graham).
- morphological box: a grid of parameters (rows) and options (columns) in which a design is one path (Gerstner, after Zwicky).
- nominal group: people generating ideas alone whose output is pooled, the baseline against which interacting groups are measured.
- opinionated software: software built for a specific purpose so that it does the work rather than making users its designers (Linear).
- opportunity solution tree: an outcome at the top, opportunities beneath, solutions beneath those, experiments at the leaves (Torres).
- originality: the statistical rarity of an idea, one of Guilford's four scores.
- pattern: a context, a problem with its forces, and a solution, linked to larger and smaller patterns (Alexander).
- perceptual learning module: a training format of many short discrimination trials with immediate feedback (Kellman).
- performative type: typography whose meaning is carried by its movement.
- preinventive structure: a mental form produced before any meaning is assigned to it (Finke, Ward and Smith).
- primary generator: the single strong idea an architect chooses early to narrow the problem enough to start (Darke, Lawson).
- principle: a statement that forbids something and can be cited to send work back; a value is an aspiration that forbids nothing (after Bret Victor).
- production blocking: the loss of ideas in interacting groups caused by waiting to speak (Diehl and Stroebe).
- prototype: a specific, resolved build that tests a known question; a sketch is quick, cheap, disposable and ambiguous (Buxton).
- ratio of looking to making: the house weekly split of about 30 percent looking, 50 percent making, 20 percent reflecting.
- readymade: an existing object repositioned as art by context and signature (Duchamp).
- reflection in action: thinking by acting in the material and reading its response (Schön).
- remote associates: distant associations reachable from a cue; the Remote Associates Test asks for the word linking three others (Mednick).
- resistance: the daily avoidance of creative work (Pressfield).
- rule of six: Murch's weighted criteria for a cut: emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4.
- salience network: the brain system that switches between the default and executive networks.
- satisficing: accepting the first option that meets a threshold because full search is impossible (Simon).
- seeing as and seeing that: the two readings of a sketch, figural possibility and non-figural fact, alternating during design (Goldschmidt).
- semantics, syntactics, pragmatics: Vignelli's three questions: what it means, how it is built, whether it works.
- sensemaking: the social process of giving data meaning during synthesis (Kolko).
- sidebearing: the space on either side of a glyph that sets its spacing before kerning.
- single idea: the one sentence, written as a feeling, that a piece serves; the house rule.
- single question: the one question under twelve words asked before generating.
- squint: blurring the eyes or zooming out to test whether a hierarchy survives.
- structure mapping: analogy as the mapping of systems of relations between domains (Gentner).
- styleframe: a single still frame that fixes the final look of a shot before animation.
- subtraction: removing elements until the piece breaks, then putting one back.
- super normal: objects that disappear into use (Morrison and Fukasawa).
- taste plateau: the state in which output stabilises at competent and the eye stops improving.
- test battery: the written set of tests that supplies the stopping rule for a wicked problem.
- the gap: the years in which a maker's taste outruns their ability (Ira Glass).
- three percent approach: changing an existing object by about three percent and declaring the source (Abloh).
- token: a named design value (a colour, a size, a duration) shared across a system; see 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md.
- Vorkurs: the Bauhaus preliminary course built on exercises and critique.
- wabi sabi: the beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete.
- Wallas four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination, verification.
- "what we are not doing" list: the written refusal of a category's tropes, made before making.
- wicked problem: a problem with no definitive formulation, no stopping rule and no true or false solutions (Rittel and Webber).
- without thought: design for the action the body already performs unconsciously (Fukasawa).
