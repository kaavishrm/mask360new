## 1. How to run this file

### 1.1 Where this file sits

The design-taste skill owns judgement: what reads expensive, the canon, the cheap tells, the house layer. 00-curriculum-and-method.md owns the syllabus and the exercise ladder. This file owns the mind: the operations a master performs between receiving a brief and signing off a piece, the evidence that those operations work, and the daily and yearly rhythm that keeps them working. 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md holds the biography and documented method of each master named here, so a name appears in this file only to illustrate a move; 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md owns the crit methods (section 4) and the self-critique checklists per deliverable (section 5) that this file calls. When a session asks "how would a great designer approach this", run section 8 as a procedure and consult sections 2 to 7 for the reason behind each step.

### 1.2 The thesis in four claims

1. Mastery is a repeatable structure of sixteen named moves (4.1 to 4.16), each with a documented cognitive basis (sections 2 and 3). The process moves are general across disciplines, and the judgement that makes them work is domain-specific and trained discipline by discipline. Whether creativity is domain-general is contested; 2.22 gives the positions and the house choice.
2. The structure is trained by a daily rhythm (section 5) and a principle-making cycle (section 7), both of which can be scheduled and audited.
3. The best evidence says the structure is learnable and the ceiling varies by person. Deliberate practice explains between under 1 percent and 26 percent of performance variance depending on domain (Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, 2014), and a direct replication of the founding violin study put it at about a quarter (Macnamara and Maitra, 2019), so the house trains the structure and promises no ceiling.
4. Under commercial conditions the structure survives only when it is protected by ritual: the single-idea presentation, the crit, the brief as instrument (section 10).

### 1.3 Reading rules

Every move in section 4 cites at least three named practitioners and the source where the habit is documented. Where a habit is reported second-hand or the attribution is unverified, the text says so. Nothing in this file overrides the house layer in design-taste section 7.

## 2. The science of creative cognition

The consolidated table is in section 12.2.

### 2.1 Divergent and convergent thinking (Guilford)

J. P. Guilford's 1950 presidential address to the American Psychological Association and his Structure of Intellect model (1967), which classified intellectual abilities by operation, content and product into 120 cells, separated divergent thinking (producing many candidate answers to an open problem) from convergent thinking (narrowing to the one correct answer). His divergent tests score four properties: fluency (count of ideas), flexibility (count of categories), originality (statistical rarity) and elaboration (detail added). Consequence: generation and judgement are separate operations and must be separated in time. A sketch session that judges as it goes produces fewer and more conventional candidates. The house generate step (section 8.3) bans evaluation until a fixed count is reached; the count is the fluency score, and the category spread is the flexibility score.

### 2.2 The Geneplore model (Finke, Ward and Smith)

Ronald Finke, Thomas Ward and Steven Smith's Creative Cognition (1992) describes a two-phase loop: a generative phase producing preinventive structures (mental forms, images or part-combinations that have no assigned meaning yet), and an exploratory phase that interprets those forms, tests them against constraints and sends them back for regeneration. Their experiments asked participants to combine three simple parts (a sphere, a hook, a tube) before being told the product category, and the forms produced before the category was assigned were judged more creative. Consequence: make forms before meanings. Styleframes precede storyboards at Buck (10.4); type experiments precede the brand rationale at Studio Dumbar; the house asks for ten compositions before the one-sentence idea is finalised, then interprets.

### 2.3 Conceptual blending (Fauconnier and Turner)

Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner's The Way We Think (2002) models creative meaning as blending: two input mental spaces are mapped through a generic space into a blended space that carries emergent structure present in neither input. The desktop metaphor blends an office and a file system; Jacquemus's 2023 Paris stunt blended a Bambino bag and a city bus. Consequence: a blend needs two specific inputs. "Luxury" plus "hospitality" produces nothing because neither is a space with structure; "a Jaipur haveli courtyard at 6am" plus "a Swiss timetable" produces a layout with a thermal rhythm and a strict grid. Name both inputs in the brief.

### 2.4 Bisociation (Koestler)

Arthur Koestler's The Act of Creation (1964) called the creative act bisociation: the collision of two self-consistent frames of reference (he called them matrices) that are habitually kept apart. He argued that humour, scientific discovery and art share this one mechanism and differ in emotional tone. Consequence: the "tension" rule in design-taste section 1 is bisociation applied: one collision per piece, two matrices named, and the collision placed where the eye lands first. Two collisions cancel; none reads as corporate. The dose has a quantitative basis in Uzzi and colleagues' finding that conventional work with one atypical intrusion has the most impact (4.8).

### 2.5 Analogical reasoning and structure mapping (Gentner)

Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory (1983) showed that strong analogies map systems of relations between domains while weak ones map surface attributes. "The atom is like the solar system" maps orbits and attraction; "the logo is like a sunset" maps a colour. Consequence: when design-taste's reference-board rule asks for "the exact thing borrowed", the borrowed thing should be a relation (the ratio of silence to event in a Deakins frame, the pacing of a Jonathan Glazer cut, the way Aesop's product grid subordinates the bottle to the label). Surface borrowing (the beige) is what makes work read as copied.

### 2.6 Incubation, the default mode network and insight

Roger Beaty and colleagues (PNAS, 2018, 115(5), pages 1087 to 1092) scanned 163 people with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) while they generated alternative uses for objects, and found that individual creative ability was predicted by connectivity between three brain systems that normally work in opposition: the default mode network (active in memory, imagination and unfocused thought), the executive control network (sustained attention and evaluation) and the salience network (which switches between them). The connectivity pattern generalised to three external datasets: two task-fMRI samples and one resting-state sample. John Kounios and Mark Beeman's insight studies (Jung-Beeman et al., PLoS Biology, 2004; The Eureka Factor, 2015) recorded a burst of alpha activity over right posterior parietal (parieto-occipital) cortex from about 1.4 seconds before the answer, read as the brain gating out visual input, followed by a gamma burst in the right anterior superior temporal gyrus about 300 milliseconds before the answer. Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod's 2009 meta-analysis of incubation (Psychological Bulletin) found real incubation effects, larger for divergent tasks and larger when the interval is filled with a low-demand task than with a demanding one. Consequence: schedule gaps on purpose; fill them with low-demand work only: walking, washing up or administration; and treat the moment of looking away from the screen as part of the method.

### 2.7 The Wallas stages, intimation and the evidence on each

Graham Wallas's The Art of Thought (1926) named preparation, incubation, illumination and verification, and inside illumination described a component the textbook version drops: intimation, the felt sense just before illumination that a solution is approaching. Eugene Sadler-Smith ("Wallas' four-stage model of the creative process: more than meets the eye?", Creativity Research Journal, 2015) recovered it from the original text and showed that Wallas thought the feeling could be trained and protected. The evidence a century later: preparation is the best supported stage, because every expertise study (section 2.11) shows that what arrives during incubation is drawn from what was loaded during preparation; incubation is supported with the conditions in 2.6; illumination exists as a measurable neural event but it is the end of a process and arrives only to a prepared mind; verification is where the professional's hours actually go and is the stage the eureka myth deletes. Consequence: the house operating system (section 8) assigns 55 percent of elapsed time to preparation and verification combined (8.1, 8.2 and 8.6 to 8.9). When a designer says "something is close", the house protects the moment: no meeting, no switch of task, because intimation is the behavioural sign of the alpha-then-gamma run-up in 2.6.

### 2.8 Flow (Csikszentmihalyi)

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's Flow (1990) identified the conditions of absorbed performance: clear goals, immediate feedback, a challenge slightly above current skill, and the resulting merging of action and awareness with loss of self-consciousness and distorted time sense. Consequence: three operational rules. Use tools that give feedback in under a second (the Figma canvas, a live browser, a scrub bar), which is also Bret Victor's principle (section 7.2). Protect blocks of at least 90 minutes: the 90 is a house convention, since the ultradian-rhythm evidence sometimes cited for it is weak, while the entry cost is documented: Tom DeMarco and Timothy Lister (Peopleware, 1987) report that immersion takes about 15 minutes or more and that every interruption restarts it. Calibrate challenge: boredom means add a constraint; anxiety means cut scope.

### 2.9 The adjacent possible (Kauffman, Johnson)

Stuart Kauffman's Investigations (2000) defined the adjacent possible as the set of states reachable in one step from what exists now; Steven Johnson's Where Good Ideas Come From (2010) applied it to innovation, arguing that ideas arrive when their components become available. Consequence: a studio's reference library sets the edge of what it can reach. A library of only design work makes the adjacent possible a set of other design work; a library that includes architecture, film, science and craft widens the edge. Section 5.4 sets the ratio.

### 2.10 Constraints as creative fuel, and the dose (Stokes, Acar)

Patricia Stokes's Creativity from Constraints (2006) argued from case studies (Monet's series paintings, Motherwell's single motif) that constraints work by precluding the habitual response and so promoting the novel one, and that masters impose constraints on themselves before anyone else does. Catrinel Haught-Tromp (Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 2017) found that people asked to write greeting-card rhymes containing a required noun produced rhymes rated more creative than those written without the requirement. C. Page Moreau and Darren Dahl (Journal of Consumer Research, 2005) found that restricting the components people could use made their designs more creative, because the restriction blocked the path of least resistance; Anne-Laure Sellier and Darren Dahl (Journal of Marketing Research, 2011) found that restricted choice in craft tasks raised both enjoyment and rated creativity. The dose matters. Oguz Ali Acar, Murat Tarakci and Daan van Knippenberg's cross-disciplinary review (Journal of Management, 2019; summarised by the authors in Harvard Business Review the same year as covering about 145 empirical studies) describes an inverted U: creativity rises with constraints up to a moderate level and falls beyond it. Stokes's self-imposed constraint fits the evidence: a chosen constraint carries intrinsic motivation, while an imposed one can feel like surveillance (Amabile, 2.20). Consequence: the house constraint set (design-taste sections 2, 3 and 7) is a creativity device and a taste device at once. Each piece carries one to three self-chosen input constraints (one family, a fixed palette, one material). When a piece is flat and the brief is loose, add one; when the brief already binds hard on time and budget, remove a constraint before adding another.

### 2.11 Expertise, chunking and deliberate practice (Chase and Simon, Ericsson, Macnamara)

William Chase and Herbert Simon (1973) showed that chess masters recall real board positions far better than novices because they encode them as a few familiar chunks, and Simon with Kevin Gilmartin (1973) estimated a master's vocabulary at between 10,000 and 100,000 chunks, usually summarised as about 50,000; on random positions masters were no better than novices. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer (1993) defined deliberate practice as effortful activity designed to improve performance, at the edge of current ability, with feedback; the best violin students in their Berlin sample had accumulated about 10,000 hours by age 20, which Malcolm Gladwell (Outliers, 2008) turned into a rule the original authors never stated. Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald's meta-analysis (Psychological Science, 2014; 157 effect sizes, more than 11,000 participants) found deliberate practice explained 26 percent of performance variance in games, 21 percent in music, 18 percent in sports, 4 percent in education and under 1 percent in professions. Ericsson disputed their coding. Brooke Macnamara and Megha Maitra (Royal Society Open Science, 2019) then repeated the 1993 violin study: deliberate practice explained about a quarter of the variance in skill, and the best violinists had not accumulated more solitary practice than the good ones, against the original result; Ericsson disputed the method. Consequence: design is a profession with delayed, noisy feedback, which is exactly the condition under which hours convert worst. The fix is to make practice game-like: short defined tasks, a right answer or a crit within the hour, and a record. That is the design of the exercise ladder in 00-curriculum-and-method.md section 6.

### 2.12 Perceptual learning and the trained eye (Kellman)

Philip Kellman's perceptual learning modules (Kellman and Kaiser, 1994, for aviation instrument reading; Kellman and Garrigan, Physics of Life Reviews, 2009) train discrimination through hundreds of short trials with immediate feedback, and show that expert-level pattern recognition can be built in hours when the trials are structured. Consequence: the two-second "expensive or cheap" read in design-taste section 1 is a perceptual skill and is trained the same way: paired comparisons (which of these two kerning settings, which of these two margins, which of these two cuts) with an answer, 50 to 100 per session. Section 11.2 schedules them.

### 2.13 Remote associates and semantic networks (Mednick, Kenett)

Sarnoff Mednick (1962) proposed that creative people have flatter associative hierarchies: a cue activates many associations at similar strength, so remote ones are reachable. His Remote Associates Test gives three words (cottage, Swiss, cake) and asks for the fourth (cheese). Network science now measures the structure directly. Yoed Kenett, David Anaki and Miriam Faust (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2014) found that the semantic networks (maps of how strongly concepts are linked in a person's memory) of highly creative people were more connected and less modular, with shorter paths between concepts; Roger Beaty and Yoed Kenett ("Associative thinking at the core of creativity", Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2023) review the field. Jay Olson and colleagues' Divergent Association Task (PNAS, 2021; about 8,900 participants) asks for ten nouns as different from one another as possible and scores the average semantic distance between them; scores tracked established creativity measures. Consequence: the network is fed by what is consumed. A designer who consumes only design has a steep hierarchy in which "luxury hotel" activates "serif, cream, terracotta" and nothing else, which is the AI-default convergence in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 10.1. Section 5.4 is the counter-diet, and the Divergent Association Task, taken monthly (11.2), checks whether the diet is widening the network.

### 2.14 Quantity, persistence and the creative cliff (Simonton, Nijstad, Lucas)

Dean Keith Simonton's equal odds rule (1997) states that across a career a creator's number of hits is a positive, linear, stochastic function of total output, and that the hit ratio stays roughly constant: more work, more hits, same ratio. Within a single session the relation also holds: Rex Jung and colleagues (Frontiers in Psychology, 2015) found that people who produced more responses on a divergent task also produced more highly rated ones, and Roger Beaty and Paul Silvia (Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 2012) measured the serial order effect, in which ideas produced later in a session are rated more original than the first ones, because the obvious answers are used up first and executive effort is then needed to reach remote ones. Bernard Nijstad, Carsten De Dreu, Eric Rietzschel and Matthijs Baas's dual pathway to creativity model (European Review of Social Psychology, 2010) holds that originality comes either through flexibility (switching between categories) or through persistence (exploring a few categories deeply), and that both routes produce original ideas. Brian Lucas and Loran Nordgren (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2015) found that people underestimate how many ideas, and how good, continued effort will yield. Their creative cliff illusion (PNAS, 2020, eight studies) is the expectation that creativity falls across a session when it actually holds or rises; in the eighth study the expectation of decline cut persistence and creative output. Consequence: produce many and select hard. The house asks for ten compositions, three directions, one presented; every generate session has a breadth sheet and a deep run (8.3); and the session stops when the count is met, whatever the feeling of running dry says, because that feeling is the illusion Lucas and Nordgren measured. Beeple's everydays (4.9) are the extreme case.

### 2.15 Selection: blind variation, selective retention and the bias against novelty

Donald Campbell (1960) proposed that creative thought works like natural selection: variations produced without foreknowledge of which will succeed ("blind"), then selected and retained. The selection evidence is worse than the generation evidence. Eric Rietzschel, Bernard Nijstad and Wolfgang Stroebe (Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2006) found that nominal groups generated more ideas, and more original ones, than interactive groups, yet the ideas they selected were no better, and selection was no better than chance; selectors favoured feasible, conventional ideas. Their 2010 follow-up (British Journal of Psychology) found that telling selectors to choose for originality raised the originality of what they chose. Jennifer Mueller, Shimul Melwani and Jack Goncalo ("The bias against creativity", Psychological Science, 2012) found that under uncertainty people hold an implicit bias against creative ideas and fail to recognise creativity they say they value. Justin Berg ("Balancing on the creative highwire", Administrative Science Quarterly, 2016), studying circus acts, found that creators forecast the success of peers' novel ideas better than managers did, that managers undervalued novelty, and that a short divergent warm-up before judging improved forecasts. Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely's IKEA effect (Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2012), the higher value people put on things they made themselves, explains why makers overrate their own work. Consequence: selection is its own step, with written criteria that name originality; a peer outside the project forecasts which direction will land; whoever selects generates on the brief for five minutes before judging; and the client sees the reasoning before the work (10.1), which lowers the uncertainty that drives the bias. A studio with no crit ritual, no test battery and no written principles generates variation and retains noise. Sections 7 and 11 are the retention mechanism.

### 2.16 Mood, sleep and walking

Matthijs Baas, Carsten De Dreu and Bernard Nijstad's meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin, 2008) found that activating positive moods (happiness, elation) raise creative output, deactivating moods (relaxation, calm) do not, and activating negative moods (anger, anxiety) raise it under some conditions. Ullrich Wagner and colleagues (Nature, 2004, "Sleep inspires insight") trained people on a number task with a hidden shortcut; about 59 percent of those who slept eight hours before retesting found the shortcut, against about 23 percent of those kept awake. Célia Lacaux and colleagues ("Sleep onset is a creative sweet spot", Science Advances, 2021) had 103 people rest holding an object that would drop as they fell asleep, the technique attributed to Edison: spending at least 15 seconds in N1, the first and lightest stage of sleep, tripled the chance of finding a hidden rule (83 percent against 30 percent for those who stayed awake), and the benefit vanished in those who went on into deeper sleep. Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz (Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014) ran four experiments with 176 participants. Walkers produced more novel alternative uses in 81 to 100 percent of cases depending on the experiment, and about twice as many in one experiment. Stanford's summary put the average increase at about 60 percent. The benefit held indoors on a treadmill facing a wall, persisted briefly after sitting down, and reversed slightly for single-answer convergent tasks. Consequence: walk before generating and sit before converging; a short rest between generating and selecting is a legitimate studio technique; the final sign-off call is never made at the end of a late night; and a flat studio mood is treated as a production problem.

### 2.18 What replaces group brainstorming

Karan Girotra, Christian Terwiesch and Karl Ulrich ("Idea generation and the quality of the best idea", Management Science, 2010) compared team brainstorming with a hybrid in which people first generate alone and then build together: the hybrid produced more ideas, better ideas on average and better best ideas, and both formats were poor at picking their own best (2.15). Bernd Rohrbach's 6-3-5 brainwriting (1969) removes production blocking: six people each write three ideas in five minutes and pass the sheet on, for six rounds, which yields up to 108 ideas in 30 minutes, each round building on the ideas already on the sheet. Amy Edmondson ("Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams", Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999) showed that teams report and learn from errors only when interpersonal risk feels safe, the condition she named psychological safety; Google's Project Aristotle (published 2015) ranked it first of five factors in its effective teams. Consequence: the house ideation session is 10 minutes of silent individual writing, then 6-3-5 (six five-minute rounds, 30 minutes; 4-3-5 for a team of four), then discussion; selection is a separate step with written criteria (2.15); and the director opens each crit by naming a flaw in their own work, which buys the candour the braintrust form needs (4.12).

### 2.19 Generative AI and human ideation

As of October 2026 most studio ideation happens beside a model, and Claude is itself one. Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser ("Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content", Science Advances, July 2024) had 293 writers produce short stories with no AI help, one GPT-4 story idea, or up to five, and 600 evaluators rate them. With access to five ideas, stories were rated 8.1 percent more novel and 9.0 percent more useful than human-only stories, with the gain concentrated among the least creative writers; the AI-assisted stories were also more similar to one another, so each writer gained while the set lost diversity. Samangi Wadinambiarachchi and colleagues ("The effects of generative AI on design fixation and divergent thinking", CHI 2024) gave participants no support, Google image search or Midjourney while they generated design ideas: the Midjourney group fixated most and produced fewer, less varied and less original ideas. Barrett Anderson, Jash Shah and Max Kreminski ("Homogenization effects of large language models on human creative ideation", ACM Creativity and Cognition, 2024) found that ideas produced with ChatGPT converged across users. Karan Girotra, Lennart Meincke, Christian Terwiesch and Karl Ulrich ("Ideas are dimes a dozen", Wharton working paper, 2023) found that GPT-4 product ideas outscored students' ideas on rated purchase intent: generating volume is now cheap, and selection and distinctiveness are scarce. Consequence: the human frames (8.2) and the first thumbnails exist before any model output is seen, because model output is a fixation source like any example (3.13); the model is used for volume after framing; and the spread of the whole set is measured, because the documented failure is every studio producing the same thing (9.12; 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 10.1).

### 2.20 Motivation, evaluation and time pressure (Amabile)

Teresa Amabile's componential model (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1983; Creativity in Context, 1996) holds that creativity needs three components at once: domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes and intrinsic task motivation (doing the work for its own interest). Expected evaluation, surveillance and narrowly contingent reward lower it ("How to kill creativity", Harvard Business Review, 1998). Kris Byron and Shalini Khazanchi's meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin, 2012) adds the nuance: rewards that are contingent on creative performance and carry information about what good looks like raise creativity. Amabile, Constance Hadley and Steven Kramer ("Creativity under the gun", Harvard Business Review, 2002), working from daily diaries, found that high time pressure lowers creative thinking except when the person is protected and focused on one important task ("on a mission"); fragmented high-pressure days ("on a treadmill") are the worst. The Progress Principle (Amabile and Kramer, 2011; about 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 people in 26 project teams) found progress in meaningful work to be the strongest daily motivator. The Consensual Assessment Technique (Amabile, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1982) is the field's standard measure of creative products: several domain experts rate the work independently against their own implicit standards, and inter-rater reliability (the agreement between raters) is checked. Consequence: a two-day turnaround is named in the fee as a creativity cost (10.6); a pressured project is run as a mission (one task, no switching, the designer shielded from other briefs); every crit ends with one visible step of progress; and the quarterly measure in 11.3 uses three independent raters who score blind before any discussion.

### 2.21 Who decides what is creative (Csikszentmihalyi, Kaufman and Beghetto)

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's systems model (in Robert Sternberg, ed., The Nature of Creativity, 1988; Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention, 1996) locates creativity in the interaction of three parts: a person, a domain (the symbolic rules and works of a practice) and a field (the gatekeepers who decide which novelty enters the domain). His interviews with 91 eminent creators found ten pairs of apparently paradoxical traits, among them playful and disciplined, humble and proud, rebellious and traditional. James Kaufman and Ronald Beghetto's Four C model (Review of General Psychology, 2009) separates mini-c (personal insight while learning), little-c (everyday creativity), Pro-c (professional-level creativity) and Big-C (eminent creativity that changes a domain). Consequence: in luxury the field is small and nameable: editors, buyers, critics and juries (Business of Fashion, Wallpaper*, D&AD, Cannes Lions), and in India and the Gulf the regional editors and the families who own the houses. The studio maps that field for each client and makes the work legible to it. This file trains Pro-c; Big-C depends on the field as well as the work.

### 2.22 General or domain-specific (Baer, Plucker)

John Baer ("The case for domain specificity of creativity", Creativity Research Journal, 1998) showed that ratings of the same people's creativity in one domain (poems, stories, collages) barely predict their ratings in another. Jonathan Plucker replied in the same issue that self-reported creative achievement shows a general factor. John Baer and James Kaufman's Amusement Park Theoretical model (Roeper Review, 2005) reconciles the two as a hierarchy: initial requirements shared by all creative work (intelligence, motivation, a suitable environment), then general thematic areas, then domains, then micro-domains. House position: the process moves (frame, generate wide, subtract, critique) are general, and the judgement that makes them work (what subtraction means in a film cut against a printed page) is domain-specific and is trained discipline by discipline, which is why section 6 exists.

## 3. Design cognition and the designerly way of knowing

### 3.1 Schön: reflection in action

Donald Schön's The Reflective Practitioner (1983) observed architects and found that the practitioner thinks by acting: a move is made in the material, the situation "talks back", the practitioner reads the talk-back and moves again. He called this reflection in action and the whole loop a conversation with the materials of the situation. Framing is the act of naming what kind of problem this is; reframing is changing that name when the talk-back resists. Consequence: Claude makes a draft early and reads it before planning further. A layout that resists is a frame that is wrong, so the first response to resistance is to reframe.

### 3.2 Cross: designerly ways of knowing

Nigel Cross ("Designerly ways of knowing", Design Studies, 1982; the 2006 book; Design Thinking, 2011) summarised four decades of protocol studies: designers are solution-focused (they learn about the problem by proposing solutions), they reason abductively (abduction is inference to the best explanation: given a desired value and no known form, propose a form that would deliver it), and the problem and the solution co-evolve (Dorst and Cross, Design Studies, 2001): a partial solution reveals a new aspect of the problem, which reshapes the solution. Consequence: the brief is a draft until the second round. Writing a complete brief before any making produces a brief for the wrong problem.

### 3.3 Lawson: primary generators and problem-first versus solution-first

Bryan Lawson's How Designers Think (1980; fourth edition 2006) reported Jane Darke's 1979 interviews with architects, who began from a primary generator: a single strong idea (a view, a courtyard, a material) that narrows the problem enough to start, chosen before analysis is complete. Lawson's own experiment (Ergonomics, 1979) set final-year architecture students and postgraduate science students a coloured-block arrangement task with a hidden rule: scientists worked problem-first, testing combinations to find the rule; architects worked solution-first, proposing arrangements and discarding those that failed. Consequence: a master starts from one generator and tests it hard, which is the single-idea discipline in section 4.3. The risk is fixation (3.13), so the generator is held loosely for the first round. Breadth-first and depth-first working are in 3.14.

### 3.4 Dorst: frame innovation

Kees Dorst's Frame Innovation (2015) makes the frame the creative unit: "if we look at the situation as X, then Y follows". His worked cases (Sydney's Kings Cross nightlife, reframed from policing to hosting a festival) show that the most valuable design move is often a new frame and the artefacts follow from it. His nine-step method runs archaeology (how did the problem come to be), paradox, context, field, themes, frames, futures, transformation, integration. Consequence: the house demands three frames before choosing one (section 8.2), and writes each in Dorst's form. "If we look at this Dubai residence launch as a private members' invitation, then the site has no public navigation."

### 3.5 Rittel and Webber: wicked problems

Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber ("Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning", Policy Sciences, 1973) listed ten properties of wicked problems: no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, solutions good or bad (there is no true or false), no immediate test, every attempt counts (one-shot), no enumerable solution set, every problem unique, every problem a symptom of another, the explanation chosen determines the solution, and the planner has no right to be wrong. Consequence: brand and campaign problems are wicked; UI problems are often tame (a tame problem has a definitive formulation, a stopping rule and an answer that can be tested as right or wrong). Treat a brand problem as tame (one brief, one answer, one test) and the work will be correct and dead. The stopping rule is supplied by the test battery (design-taste section 4, and 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md section 5), because the problem will not supply one.

### 3.6 Simon: satisficing and ill-structured problems

Herbert Simon's The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) treated design as the science of the artificial: devising courses of action to change existing situations into preferred ones. Satisficing (1956) is accepting the first option that meets a threshold because the full search is impossible; ill-structured problems are those whose goal, constraints and operators are not all given. Consequence: set the threshold explicitly and high (the test battery), so that satisficing stops only when the work reads expensive.

### 3.7 Goldschmidt: seeing as and seeing that

Gabriela Goldschmidt ("The dialectics of sketching", Creativity Research Journal, 1991) analysed architects' sketch protocols and found a rapid alternation between seeing as (reading a figural, metaphoric possibility in the marks: this blob could be a canopy) and seeing that (reading a non-figural fact: that span is too long). Consequence: sketching is cognition, which is why hand sketching survives among masters (section 5.11); and a crit should name which mode a comment is in, because "this feels heavy" (seeing as) and "the margin is 12px" (seeing that) need different replies.

### 3.8 Goel: lateral and vertical transformations

Vinod Goel's Sketches of Thought (1995) distinguished lateral transformations (moving from one idea to a different idea) from vertical transformations (refining the same idea), and showed that ambiguous, freehand media support lateral moves while precise media (CAD, and today a pixel-exact Figma frame) push designers into vertical refinement too early. Consequence: generate in a loose medium (pencil, grey boxes, a styleframe at 25 percent zoom) and refine in a precise one. A pixel-perfect first draft is a vertical move disguised as a start.

### 3.9 Buxton: sketching versus prototyping

Bill Buxton's Sketching User Experiences (2007) separates sketches (quick, timely, cheap, disposable, plentiful, ambiguous, suggestive, exploring) from prototypes (specific, resolved, testing a known question). The error he documents is prototyping before sketching: building one thing well before many things have been considered lightly. Consequence: ten sketches before one prototype; a prototype answers a question written down in advance.

### 3.10 Kolko: abductive synthesis and sensemaking

Jon Kolko ("Abductive Thinking and Sensemaking", Design Issues, 2010) described synthesis as the externalised, abductive act of organising research into a frame that implies a design, using affinity diagrams (observations written one per note and clustered by similarity until themes emerge), concept maps and insight statements. Sensemaking is the social process of giving data meaning. Consequence: research without a wall of notes that the team reorganises is data, and data does not imply a design. The synthesis session goes in the calendar like a client meeting.

### 3.11 Brown and Katz, and the Jen critique

Tim Brown with Barry Katz (Change by Design, 2009) codified design thinking as a repeatable process (inspiration, ideation, implementation) usable by non-designers. Natasha Jen, a Pentagram partner, argued in 2017 ("Design Thinking is Bullshit", 99U) that the codified version replaced craft and critique with post-its and produced work that no one reviewed against a standard. House position: Brown is right that the moves are teachable; Jen is right that without a crit against a standard the moves produce nothing. This corpus follows Jen on the standard (the test battery) and Brown on the teachability.

### 3.12 Nelson and Stolterman, Krippendorff, Alexander

Harold Nelson and Erik Stolterman's The Design Way (2003; second edition 2012) argued design is a distinct tradition of enquiry alongside science and art, with its own judgement types (framing, appreciative, compositional), and located the designer's "guarantor" (what warrants a decision) in the desiderata of the client, where science locates it in truth. Klaus Krippendorff's The Semantic Turn (2006) holds that artefacts are designed for meaning: people act on what things mean to them, so the designer's object is meaning and the form is the carrier. Christopher Alexander's Notes on the Synthesis of Form (1964) decomposed a problem into a tree of misfit variables and solved subtrees independently; A Pattern Language (1977, with Ishikawa and Silverstein) published 253 patterns, each in the form context, problem, solution, with links to larger and smaller patterns. Consequence: a pattern is a thinking tool because it packages a frame and a solution with the evidence that they belong together; a design system (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md) that lacks the "problem" field for each component is a parts list.

### 3.13 Fixation, analogical distance and the library in the head

David Jansson and Steven Smith ("Design fixation", Design Studies, 1991, 12(1), pages 3 to 11) gave engineering students briefs (a car-mounted bicycle rack, a measuring cup for the blind, a spill-proof disposable coffee cup); those shown an example solution reproduced its features, including its flaws, even when instructed to avoid them. Julie Linsey and colleagues (Journal of Mechanical Design, 2010) found that engineering design faculty fixated too, and that written warnings against fixation helped only partly. Lawson's later work describes the precedent library: the stock of prior solutions an experienced designer carries and recognises as applicable. The two findings pull against each other: the library makes a master fast; the example makes a novice fixated.

The later literature sets the rules for a reference board. Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak (Cognitive Psychology, 1980) found that people who had just read an analogous story rarely used it on Duncker's radiation problem until told to (in the figures textbooks usually cite from the paper, about 30 percent solved without the hint and about 75 percent with it): analogies are retrieved by deliberate instruction. Joel Chan and colleagues (Journal of Mechanical Design, 2011) found that far-field and less common examples raised novelty; Katherine Fu and colleagues (Journal of Mechanical Design, 2013) found that examples too far from the problem lowered quality, so analogical distance (how far the source domain sits from the problem's domain) has a middle range that works best. Luis Vasconcelos and Nathan Crilly's review ("Inspiration and fixation", Design Studies, 2016) concludes that examples both inspire and fixate, depending on timing, abstraction and distance. Masters avoid fixation by loading many precedents (so none dominates), by borrowing only the named relation (2.5), by deliberately generating a direction that contradicts the example, and by looking at the board once and then covering it.

Consequence: the board holds one near, two middle-distance and one far reference; references are shown only after the first independent thumbnails exist, and in abstracted form (cropped, greyscale, or a one-line description); the question "which relation from reference 3 applies here?" is asked aloud, because exposure alone does not produce transfer; and generative-model output counts as an example (2.19). Claude reads the references, writes the borrowed relation in one line each, then designs from the lines with the references closed.

### 3.14 Studies of outstanding designers, and the levels of expertise

Nigel Cross studied three outstanding designers in depth, Gordon Murray (Formula One cars), Kenneth Grange (products) and Victor Scheinman (robotics), in "Expertise in design: an overview" (Design Studies, 2004) and Design Thinking (2011), and found three shared strategies: they take a broad systems view of the problem and question the given criteria; they frame the problem in a distinctive, often personal way; and they design from first principles. Kees Dorst's seven levels of expertise (in Bryan Lawson and Kees Dorst, Design Expertise, 2009, adapted from Hubert and Stuart Dreyfus) run naive, novice, advanced beginner, competent, expert, master and visionary, and each level changes what the designer sees as the problem: the competent designer chooses among known strategies, the expert recognises situations and responds, the master innovates within the field's practice, and the visionary redefines the field. Linden Ball and Thomas Ormerod ("Structured and opportunistic processing in design: a critical discussion", Design Studies, 1995) found that expert designers work mainly breadth-first, sketching the whole at low resolution with opportunistic deep dives where a risk appears, while novices go deep on one component first. Consequence: 8.3 enforces breadth-first generation; 11.1 carries a level rubric; and 11.4 teaches each designer the behaviour of the level above their own.

## 4. The moves shared by masters

### 4.1 Immersion

Basis: preparation (2.7), the library (3.13). Roger Deakins has said in interviews and on the Team Deakins podcast (from 2020) that he reads a script repeatedly, without notes at first, until images start to arrive, and only then begins lists. Jony Ive's immersion is in material and process: Walter Isaacson's Steve Jobs (2011) records his months with aluminium suppliers and the unibody machining process, and Ive's own account of caring about the parts no one sees comes from the same source. Matthieu Blazy's Bottega Veneta debut (February 2022) was built in the atelier: the "denim" trousers and "flannel" shirts were printed and worked nubuck leather, a trompe l'oeil (making that deceives the eye into reading one material as another) that only an immersion in the leather workshop produces. Walter Murch (The Conversations, with Michael Ondaatje, 2002) describes judging a cut with the sound turned off to see whether the pictures hold by themselves, and separately with the picture off. Consequence: the immersion stage (8.1) reads the brief, the subject, the place, the material and the audience before a reference board is opened.

### 4.2 The single question

Basis: framing (3.1, 3.4). Dieter Rams's standing question was whether a thing is necessary; his "Weniger, aber besser" (less, but better) is the answer. Jony Ive's stated aim, in Gary Hustwit's film Objectified (2009) and later interviews, is a solution that seems inevitable, so obvious that the design stops announcing itself; the house compresses it into "what is the quietest version?". Kenya Hara, Muji's art director, frames the brand's aim as kore de ii ("this will do") set against kore ga ii ("this is what I want"): satisfaction by sufficiency, which is why Muji products carry no promise and why the house asks "what would this be if it were honest?". Consequence: before generating, write one question in under twelve words. The house defaults: "What is the quietest version?", "What would this be if it were honest?", "What is unnecessary?"

### 4.3 The single idea discipline

Basis: primary generator (3.3), bisociation (2.4). Bill Bernbach's Doyle Dane Bernbach built the Volkswagen "Think small" campaign (1959, art director Helmut Krone, copywriter Julian Koenig) on one idea per advertisement. Saul Bass reduced each film to one symbol and moved only that: the dismembered figure of Anatomy of a Murder (1959), the spiral of Vertigo (1958). Michael Bierut (How To, 2015) presents one idea to a client and explains why; the alternatives he shows are the ones he does not recommend, labelled as such. The house rule in design-taste section 4 is the same: one sentence, as a feeling, or there is no design yet. Consequence: the frame stage (8.2) ends when one sentence exists; everything that does not serve it is cut at 8.7.

### 4.4 Inversion and the what if

Basis: lateral transformation (3.8), blending (2.3). Virgil Abloh's method, laid out in his Harvard Graduate School of Design lecture (2017, published as Insert Complicated Title Here, 2018), is a set of "cheat codes": quotation marks around a word on a garment turn the object into a comment on itself; the "3 percent approach" (4.8) is the inversion of originality. Simon Porte Jacquemus inverts scale: the Chiquito bag (2018) too small to hold a phone, the 2023 CGI (computer-generated imagery) stunt of bus-sized Bambino bags driving through Paris (made with Ian Padgham). Jonathan Anderson at Loewe placed surreal objects inside classical tailoring (the balloon heels of 2022, the pixelated clothes of the January 2023 men's show). Consequence: at 8.3 every direction is run through "what if the opposite", "what if ten times the size", "what if it were the only thing on the page".

### 4.5 Subtraction as the primary move

Basis: constraints (2.10), satisficing threshold (3.6). Rams's tenth principle: good design is as little design as possible. The Row (Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen, 2006) removed the logo, then the camera: guests at its February 2024 Paris show were asked not to photograph or post, and found notebooks and pencils on their seats. Hara's concept of emptiness (White, 2010) is a receptacle the user fills with meaning, which is why a Muji label says nothing. Massimo Vignelli's canon (2010) subtracts typefaces to a handful. Consequence: the subtract stage (8.7) runs "remove until it breaks, put one back".

### 4.6 Constraint setting

Basis: 2.10. Charles Eames's Design Q&A (prepared for the 1969 exhibition "Qu'est-ce que le design?" at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, Paris, and filmed in 1972) is the master source: asked by Madame L'Amic whether design admits constraint, Eames answers "Design depends largely on constraints", and names as one of the few effective keys to the design problem the designer's ability to recognise as many constraints as possible, and the willingness and enthusiasm to work within them. Vignelli listed in The Vignelli Canon the few typefaces he used (Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Futura, Times and Helvetica) and argued the discipline was the point. Peter Saville's Unknown Pleasures sleeve (Joy Division, Factory Records, 1979) carries no band name and no title on the front, only the pulsar plot; Factory's willingness to leave it off was the constraint that made the image an icon. Muji's name (mujirushi ryohin, no-brand quality goods, 1980) is a constraint written as a company. David Carson broke rules he knew: setting a Bryan Ferry interview in Ray Gun (1994) entirely in Zapf Dingbats was legible only as a decision about an unreadable interview, and Carson has said the readable version appeared later in the issue. Consequence: constraints are written at 8.4 before making, in the form "we will not use", and the list is shown to the client as a feature.

### 4.7 Cross-domain transfer

Basis: structure mapping (2.5), the adjacent possible (2.9). Fashion from sculpture and architecture: Rei Kawakubo's "Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body" (Comme des Garçons, spring 1997) padded the body into sculpture; Iris van Herpen's collaborations with architect Philip Beesley brought structural systems into couture. UI from physical objects at Apple: Bas Ording's inertial scrolling and rubber-band bounce (iPhone, 2007) map the physics of a thrown object onto a list; the iOS 7 reset (2013) kept the physics and removed the leather. Motion from physics: spring-based animation (the Apple springs, and the spring model in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md section 3) replaced hand-drawn curves. Editing from music: Thelma Schoonmaker cuts Scorsese's sequences to songs chosen before shooting (Goodfellas, 1990, the "Layla" coda); Paul Machliss cut Baby Driver (2017) to the pre-cleared soundtrack on set with Edgar Wright. Architecture from cosmology: Charles Correa's Jawahar Kala Kendra in Jaipur turns a nine-square mandala into a plan (6.13). Consequence: at 8.3 at least one direction borrows its structure from a domain outside design, and the borrowed relation is written down.

### 4.8 Reference as raw material and the three percent change

Basis: 2.5, 3.13. Abloh's "3 percent approach" (Harvard GSD lecture, 2017): take an existing object and change it by about 3 percent, enough to make it a new idea, and declare the source. Duchamp's Fountain (1917) is the readymade: an existing object repositioned as art by context and signature. The line "good artists copy, great artists steal" is routinely attributed to Picasso; the attribution is unverified, and Steve Jobs quoted it in 1996 (Triumph of the Nerds). Brian Uzzi, Satyam Mukherjee, Michael Stringer and Ben Jones ("Atypical combinations and scientific impact", Science, 2013) analysed about 17.9 million papers: the highest-impact papers combined a deeply conventional base with a small intrusion of atypical combinations, and papers that were wholly novel or wholly conventional were less likely to become hits. Paul Hekkert, Dirk Snelders and Piet van Wieringen's MAYA finding, in which typicality and novelty jointly predict aesthetic preference, is in 02-perception-and-psychology.md section 10.4. Consequence: the category's structure stays recognisable and the atypical element sits in one place, which gives the three percent change and the one-collision rule (2.4) an evidence base. The reference is named, the borrowed relation is named, and the change is deliberate and visible. Unnamed borrowing is the reference regurgitation of 9.2.

### 4.9 Quantity then selection

Basis: 2.14, 2.15. Beeple (Mike Winkelmann) has made and posted one finished image every day since 1 May 2007; his site's counter read 7,086 when checked in October 2026, which is the figure for 23 September 2026 (the page lags), and an unbroken run reaches day 7,122 on 29 October 2026. Stefan Sagmeister's studio produces by iteration and discards most of it; his sabbatical (4.13) exists to refill. Paula Scher sketches identities in pencil first (the Citibank mark, 1998, began as a napkin sketch in a first meeting, and she has said it took a few seconds and decades of practice). Bierut's numbered notebooks (kept since 1982, more than 100 volumes) are a quantity record. Jonathan Hoefler and Tobias Frere-Jones drew typefaces glyph by glyph: a family like Gotham (2000) grew from its first weights into narrow, condensed and rounded versions, and every style needs its own drawn and spaced glyph set of several hundred characters. Consequence: at 8.3 the fluency target is ten compositions or thirty thumbnails, plus a deep run of ten in the strongest category; at 8.6 three survive; one ships.

### 4.10 Making as thinking

Basis: Schön (3.1), Geneplore (2.2). Apple's industrial design studio under Ive built CNC-machined (computer numerically controlled) models of nearly every variant (Isaacson, 2011); Pixar storyboards every film and cuts it into story reels (storyboard drawings timed to scratch dialogue and cut together as a rough film) before animation begins, and reworks the reels until they play (Ed Catmull, Creativity, Inc., 2014). Figma is the sketchbook of product teams because a frame is faster than a sentence. Rauno Freiberg ("Invisible Details of Interaction Design", 2023, and his craft archive of Vercel work) builds the interaction to find the feeling, then writes the rule. Buck's production method (reported studio practice, 10.4) puts styleframes (single still frames that fix the final look of a shot) before any animation. Consequence: the first real-content draft exists within the first fifth of the make stage (8.5), built with the real material (real copy, real photography, real font); lorem ipsum is banned (8.5).

### 4.11 The tool mastered to invisibility, then pushed

Basis: chunking (2.11), flow (2.8). A line widely attributed to John Lasseter at Pixar holds that "the art challenges the technology, and the technology inspires the art"; the original occasion was not located for this file. Andrew Kramer (Video Copilot: free After Effects tutorials from the mid 2000s; the Element 3D plug-in, 2012) built the After Effects skill of a generation by making the tool disappear and then building plug-ins to push it. Bruno Simon's 2019 portfolio (a car driven across a landscape rendered in WebGL, the browser's 3D graphics interface) and his course Three.js Journey do the same for WebGL. Consequence: a master learns tools in practice time (section 11), and on a client job pushes the tool one step past what it did last time.

### 4.12 The critique loop

Basis: selective retention (2.15), reflection (3.1). Pixar's Braintrust (Creativity, Inc., 2014) grew out of the Toy Story story group (John Lasseter, Andrew Stanton, Pete Docter, Lee Unkrich, Joe Ranft) and proved itself in the Toy Story 2 rescue of 1999: a room of directors and story leads watches the reels, speaks with candour, and has no authority to mandate changes, which leaves the director responsible. Apple's design reviews under Jobs were near-daily visits to the studio (Isaacson, 2011). Pentagram's partners (23 listed in October 2026, all practising designers who own the business and each run a team) present work to each other at partner meetings and critique it. The Bauhaus Vorkurs (Itten, then Albers) built teaching on the crit of made work. Candour depends on psychological safety (2.18). Consequence: 8.6 runs a crit with the design-taste tests, in the braintrust form: candour, no mandate, the designer decides.

### 4.13 Rhythm and time

Basis: incubation (2.6), sleep (2.16). Sagmeister closes his studio for one year every seven (the first in 2000 in New York, the second in Bali in 2008 to 2009), and his TED talk "The power of time off" (TEDGlobal, Oxford, 2009) argues that the sabbatical's experiments supply the next seven years of work. Paul Graham's "Maker's schedule, manager's schedule" (2009) explains why one meeting destroys an afternoon. Cal Newport's Deep Work (2016) sets the block. Consequence: section 5.3 sets the house rhythm.

### 4.14 The long game

Basis: 2.14 over a career. Rams designed for Braun from 1955 to 1995 and the Vitsoe 606 shelving system (1960) is still sold and still compatible with its first pieces. Hermès (1837) plans in generations. The Row has grown for nearly two decades without a logo on the product. Consequence: a brand decision is tested against a ten-year horizon ("does it still hold in 2036"), which is the long form of design-taste's "will it still hold in 2029" test.

### 4.15 Intuition trained by analysis, and where to trust it

Basis: 2.11, 2.12, 3.1. Schön's practitioners act fast because they have reflected slowly. Adriaan de Groot's chess studies (1946; English edition 1965) found that masters consider few moves because recognition has already pruned the tree. Murch (In the Blink of an Eye, 1992; second edition 2001) counted cuts and blinks, derived the rule of six with its weights (emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4; eye-trace is where the viewer's eye sits on the frame across the cut), and then cuts by feel. The boundary condition comes from Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein ("Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree", American Psychologist, 2009): intuition is skilled only where the environment is regular enough to be predictable and the person has had long practice with prompt, clear feedback. Klein's recognition-primed decision model (Sources of Power, 1998) describes experts acting on the first workable option they recognise. Robin Hogarth (Educating Intuition, 2001) separates kind learning environments, where feedback is fast and accurate, from wicked ones, where it is delayed, missing or misleading (Hogarth's wicked environment is a different idea from Rittel and Webber's wicked problem in 3.5). Type spacing, cut points and interface easing are kind domains; campaign effect, brand longevity and audience response are wicked ones. Consequence: trained intuition is trusted on kerning, cuts and easing; on strategy and audience response the reasoning is written down and tested, because feedback there has never been fast enough to train the gut; the paired-comparison drills (11.2) turn parts of a wicked domain into a kind one; and analysis is scheduled in practice time and in the reflect stage (8.9), which keeps the make stage fast.

### 4.16 Play and seriousness, and the readiness to be plain

Basis: 2.16 (activating positive mood). Paul Rand's essay "Design and the play instinct" (A Designer's Art, 1985) argues that play within rules produces form. Scher's TED talk (2008) distinguishes serious play, which produces her best work, from solemnity, which produces her worst. Naoto Fukasawa's "without thought" workshops and his wall-mounted CD player for Muji (1999) design for the action the body already performs. The readiness to be plain is the other face: Miuccia Prada's black nylon (1984) and "ugly chic", Phoebe Philo's Céline (2008 to 2018) and her 2023 return, Muji's entire catalogue. Consequence: the work is allowed to be plain when plainness is the idea, and the crit asks "is this plain or is this empty" (design-taste's "is there one thing you will remember tomorrow" test).

### 4.17 Two kinds of master (Galenson)

David Galenson (Old Masters and Young Geniuses: The Two Life Cycles of Artistic Creativity, 2006) used auction prices and the frequency of illustrations in textbooks to separate two types of innovator. Conceptual innovators start from a preconceived idea, plan carefully and tend to peak young: Picasso painted Les Demoiselles d'Avignon in 1907, in his mid twenties. Experimental innovators work by trial, are rarely satisfied and tend to peak late: Cézanne's most valued work came in his last years (he died at 67). The moves in 4.1 to 4.16 are shared by both types; the order and the time spent differ. Consequence: diagnose each designer's type. A conceptual worker frames long and makes fast (Rand's booklet, Abloh's quotation marks); an experimental worker makes early and finds the frame through the work (Murch, Blazy in the atelier). The time shares in section 8 suit a conceptual worker; for an experimental one, move 5 percent of elapsed time from 8.2 to 8.5. Table 12.1 gives the house reading of each master's type.

## 5. How masters function day to day

### 5.1 The notebook habit

Michael Bierut has kept numbered notebooks since 1982, two years into his decade at Vignelli Associates (he joined in 1980), and had filled more than 100 by the mid 2010s; each is the same format, which removes the choice of where to write. Leonardo da Vinci's surviving notebook pages number about 7,200 (the Codex Leicester alone, 72 pages, sold to Bill Gates in 1994 for about 30.8 million dollars). Paula Scher's identities begin in pencil, and the Citibank mark was first drawn on a napkin in the first client meeting (1998). House setting: one notebook per designer, same format, dated pages, carried to every meeting; the sketch goes in the notebook before it goes in Figma.

### 5.2 The reference wall, the box and the library

Are.na (launched in the early 2010s by Charles Broskoski and collaborators) is a reference board widely used by design studios, because it has no algorithmic feed and blocks are connected by hand. Physical boards persist at Buck, at Pentagram and in fashion ateliers because a wall is read peripherally all day. Twyla Tharp (The Creative Habit, 2003) starts every project with a labelled box into which every note, clipping, video and book goes from the first day, so immersion has a container. Peter Saville's method is the library: the Power, Corruption and Lies sleeve (New Order, 1983) is Henri Fantin-Latour's A Basket of Roses from the National Gallery, taken from the gallery postcard; the colour-code alphabet beside it is Saville's own system. David Carson collects found type and photographs signage. House setting: one Are.na channel and one physical box per client. The board inside the channel holds four references by the distance rule of 3.13 (one near, two middle, one far), each annotated with the borrowed relation in one line (2.5, 4.8); the channel is opened after the first independent thumbnails and closed during the make stage.

### 5.3 Morning, deep work and review cadence

Graham's maker schedule (2009) and Newport's Deep Work (2016) converge on blocks of three to four hours with no meeting inside them. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) observed that elite performers sustained only about four hours of deliberate practice a day, in sessions of about an hour, which caps the protected block. Mason Currey's Daily Rituals (2013) compiles the documented routines of about 160 writers, artists and composers; the common elements are a fixed start, a protected morning block and a walk. Tharp's fixed start (the taxi to the gym at dawn) makes beginning the day a non-decision. Pixar's "dailies" review animation every morning in front of the director; Apple's design studio under Jobs was reviewed nearly daily; Linear's published method calls two-week cycles "the most common" and puts the responsibility for each issue on "a single person". House setting: designers hold one protected block of three to four hours before 13:00 with notifications off, run as sessions of about an hour with a short walk between them; the studio crit is fixed at the same hour on the same two days each week; client reviews are never scheduled inside the block. A creative director's own calendar follows the same rule on at least three days a week or the director stops designing within a quarter.

### 5.4 How they consume outside design

Steve Jobs and Jony Ive held long walking conversations (Isaacson, 2011). Kenya Hara's thinking is built on Japan itself: Muji's "Horizon" campaign (2003) photographed in the Uyuni salt flat and Mongolian grassland came from his reading of emptiness in landscape. Walter Murch worked for years on the Titius-Bode relation in astronomy and translated Curzio Malaparte (The Bird That Swallowed Its Cage, 2013). Roger Deakins trained in graphic design and photography at Bath before film. Kris Sowersby's Klim essays trace each typeface to printing history. Frédéric Godart, William Maddux, Andrew Shipilov and Adam Galinsky ("Fashion with a foreign flair", Academy of Management Journal, 2015) rated the creativity of collections from about 270 fashion houses across 21 seasons against their creative directors' professional experience abroad: depth (time worked abroad) predicted creativity most consistently, while breadth (the number of countries) and cultural distance helped up to a point and then hurt. House setting: the ratio of looking to making (5.12) assigns at least a third of the looking to art, architecture, film, literature, science, nature and travel, and the diet favours depth in a few cultures over a long list of short visits; a bilingual studio working across India and the UAE counts as an asset under Godart's evidence. For Mask360 that means Mumbai's Art Deco, Jaipur's city grid and Jantar Mantar instruments, Goa's Portuguese houses, Dubai's 1970s modernism (the World Trade Centre, 1979) and Abu Dhabi's Louvre (2017) are in the library before any luxury-brand reference.

### 5.5 Fear and taste anxiety

Ira Glass's "the gap" (from "Ira Glass on Storytelling", an interview series filmed in 2009 and widely transcribed) names the condition: taste outruns ability for years, and most people quit in the gap. Steven Pressfield's The War of Art (2002) names the daily avoidance as resistance and prescribes turning up at the same hour regardless. Sagmeister's first sabbatical (2000) was triggered by the fear that the work was repeating itself. House setting: fear is handled by procedure. The first move of the day is making, before the inbox is opened; the crit separates the work from the person by naming the element and the fix (design-taste section 8); and the "plain or empty" question (4.16) is asked aloud so that plainness is recorded as a decision.

### 5.6 How they decide when something is done

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's rule (Terre des hommes, 1939) that a thing is finished when nothing more can be removed is the subtraction test. Rams's tenth principle is the same. Murch stops when the cut plays for an audience without his noticing the cuts. The house uses written tests so that "done" is a verdict anyone can check: the seven tests in design-taste section 4, the self-critique checklists in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md section 5, and the subtract rule (8.7). A piece is done when every test passes and one more removal breaks it.

### 5.7 How they recover from bad work

Ed Catmull's rule at Pixar is to fail early and fail fast, because every film is bad in its first reels and the Braintrust exists to say so while fixing is cheap. David Fincher reshoots and repeats takes until the scene is right, and treats the repetition as the method itself. Bierut's How To includes projects that were killed or rejected, presented as part of the practice. House setting: a bad piece is diagnosed in the crit format (idea problem first, craft problem second), the diagnosis is written into the reflect stage (8.9), the loop-back rule in 8.12 says where to restart, and the designer starts the next piece the same day.

### 5.8 How much they produce and discard

Beeple: one finished image a day for more than 7,000 days. Zach Lieberman: a daily coding sketch posted since 2016. Hoefler and Frere-Jones: hundreds of glyphs drawn for each of dozens of styles, with whole styles discarded. Pixar: every film is storyboarded and cut into reels several times over before animation. House ratio: ten compositions or thirty thumbnails to three directions to one presented, and the discards are kept in a dated archive, because Simonton's rule (2.14) says the next hit is in the next ten.

### 5.9 How they teach

Paul Rand taught at Yale from 1956 and returned in the 1970s; Josef Albers built the Yale programme on exercises with one variable at a time (Interaction of Color, 1963). Bierut and Scher have taught at Yale and the School of Visual Arts for decades, and both teach by crit of made work. Sowersby teaches through essays that show the drawing decisions of each typeface. Deakins teaches through a forum and a podcast where he answers specific lighting questions. Murch teaches through two books that give the rule and the counts. House setting: a creative director teaches thinking by asking the question (4.2) before giving the answer, by running the crit in the braintrust form, by teaching the behaviour of the next level up (11.1, 11.4), and by publishing the principle set with the reason for each rule (section 7).

### 5.10 Their relationship with clients and briefs

Paul Rand charged Steve Jobs 100,000 dollars for the NeXT logo (1986), delivered one solution in a bound booklet that walked Jobs through the reasoning, and told him that if he wanted options he should talk to other people (Isaacson, 2011). Bierut presents one recommendation and shows the alternatives as the reasons it was chosen. Saville's relationship with Factory Records gave him total freedom and no commercial control, which produced the sleeves and the lateness (the FAC 1 poster of 1978 arrived after the event it advertised). The Eames design diagram, drawn for the same 1969 Paris exhibition as Design Q&A (4.6), shows three overlapping areas (the interests and concerns of the design office, of the client and of society as a whole) and places the work where all three overlap; it is the house tool for the first client meeting. House setting: one idea is presented; the brief is rewritten by the studio and sent back for agreement before work starts (section 10.2); the client sees the "what we are not doing" list as a feature of the proposal.

### 5.11 The studio environment and the body

Jobs restricted access to Ive's studio and the studio was built around light and large tables holding the models (Isaacson, 2011). Rams designed his own house in Kronberg (1971) as a working example of his principles. Hara's Nippon Design Center office is quiet and object-led. Murch edits standing, which he compares in In the Blink of an Eye to a surgeon or a cook, because the body stays alert and the editor can step back from the screen. Scher and Frere-Jones draw by hand first: Frere-Jones draws glyphs in pencil and digitises after, and Scher's identities start on paper. House setting: daylight on the working tables, a quiet rule during blocks, real objects of the client's category on the table (the stone sample, the bottle, the menu, the swatch), a standing position for editing passes, and a pencil sketch before the first Figma frame.

### 5.12 The ratio of looking to making

A working master spends more time looking than a novice assumes. Deakins reads before listing; Murch watches before cutting; Blazy sits in the atelier before drawing. House ratio across a week: about 30 percent looking (reading, references, the outside diet of 5.4, other people's work in the crit), 50 percent making, 20 percent reflecting and administering. In a project, immersion and framing (8.1, 8.2) take about 25 percent of elapsed time before the first composition exists.

### 6.2 Type design

The cycle is drawing, then spacing, then testing, then kerning, in that order, and it starts from the control characters: the lowercase n and o, which set the stem, the bowl, the x-height (the height of the lowercase without ascenders) and the standard sidebearings; the uppercase H and O, which set the cap height (the height of the capitals) and the round overshoot (the small excess by which round letters pass the baseline and cap line so they look the same size as flat ones); then a, e, d, p and v. Walter Tracy's Letters of Credit (1986) gives the spacing method: set the sidebearings of n and o first and derive the rest. Tobias Frere-Jones (Frere-Jones Type, founded 2015 after he left Hoefler & Frere-Jones) draws in pencil before digitising. Jonathan Hoefler (Hoefler&Co, acquired by Monotype in 2021) tests every family in running text before release. Matthew Carter designed for the worst conditions first: Bell Centennial (1978) for telephone directories, Verdana and Georgia (1996) for screens. Kris Sowersby (Klim Type Foundry, Wellington, since 2005; Söhne, 2019) publishes the historical reasoning for each face. Dinamo (founded 2013 by Johannes Breyer and Fabian Harb; ABC Diatype) treats a typeface as a system of variable axes (continuous design ranges, such as weight or width, inside one font file). See 03-typography.md sections 1, 4 and 10 for anatomy, spacing and variable fonts.

### 6.3 Product and UI

Rams's ten principles (7.3) are a question list: is it useful, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough to the last detail. Charles and Ray Eames treated furniture, film and exhibitions as one practice; Powers of Ten (1977) uses change of scale as a method, and their statements on constraint and on the client are in 4.6 and 5.10. Ive's additions, from his interviews and Isaacson: care, and the unseen (the inside of the machine finished as well as the outside). Apple's Human Interface Guidelines took their three themes (clarity, deference, depth) from the iOS 7 reset (2013); the June 2025 revision added Liquid Glass guidance and the June 2026 revision reintroduced a design principles page (7.6). Karri Saarinen wrote Airbnb's Design Language System in 2016 and co-founded Linear (2019), whose method opens "There is a lost art of building true quality software" (7.10). Rasmus Andersson designed Inter (2017) and Figma's early interface. Rauno Freiberg's "Invisible Details of Interaction Design" (2023) is the method of building the interaction to find the feeling. Emil Kowalski (Sonner, Vaul, the "Animations on the Web" course) and Paco Coursey (cmdk; Linear) are design engineers: designers who build their own interfaces in production code, so the design decisions are made in the material. Jordan Singer's Diagram (acquired by Figma in 2023) led Figma's AI features. Mike Matas (Push Pop Press, 2011; Facebook Paper, 2014) and Soleio Cuervo (the Facebook Like button; Dropbox design) built interfaces from physical metaphors. Katie Dill (Airbnb; vice president of design at Lyft from 2017; then head of design at Stripe) treats quality as a leadership behaviour exercised by reviewing the shipped product; Julie Zhuo (Facebook, The Making of a Manager, 2019) wrote the critique and management method for product design. Brian Chesky's eleven-star exercise (Masters of Scale, with Reid Hoffman, episode "Handcrafted", 2017) extrapolates one moment of the experience from one star to eleven and then finds the feasible point on the curve. Bret Victor (7.2) and Alan Kay (the Dynabook, 1972; "the best way to predict the future is to invent it", 1971) set the horizon; Bill Buxton (3.9) sets the sketch. See 06-ui-design-craft.md and 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md.

### 6.4 UX and research

Don Norman (The Design of Everyday Things, 1988; revised 2013) gives the vocabulary: affordance (what an object permits), signifier (what tells you so), mapping, feedback, conceptual model. Jared Spool's "The $300 million button" (2009), in which replacing a forced registration step at checkout with a plain "Continue" raised an e-commerce site's sales by 300 million dollars in the first year, is the case for removing a step. Teresa Torres (Continuous Discovery Habits, 2021) structures discovery as weekly customer contact and an opportunity solution tree (outcome at the top, opportunities beneath, solutions beneath those, experiments at the leaves). Erika Hall (Just Enough Research, 2013) sets the minimum: ask before building, and ask the question whose answer would change the design. Jesse James Garrett's five planes (The Elements of User Experience, 2002), the layers of decisions from the most abstract to the most concrete (strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface), order the decisions. Indi Young (Mental Models, 2008) listens for the reasoning behind what users ask for. See 08-ux-research-and-strategy.md, 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md and 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md.

### 6.5 Motion

Saul Bass's method (Vertigo, 1958; Anatomy of a Murder, 1959; Psycho, 1960) finds one symbol that holds the film and animates only that. Kyle Cooper (Se7en, 1995; Imaginary Forces, 1996; Prologue Films, 2003) builds the title sequence as a short film with its own arc. Buck (since 2004) locks styleframes before animation, so the animation problem is movement alone. Ordinary Folk and ManvsMachine (London) present their work as thinking in material and physics first (reported practice; see 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 5.2). Ash Thorp and GMUNK (Bradley Munkowitz; Tron: Legacy, 2010; Oblivion, 2013) design screen graphics as if they were real instruments. Beeple's everydays and Andrew Kramer's tutorials are the practice regimes. The DEMO festival, founded by Studio Dumbar/DEPT and Global, made performative type (type whose meaning is in its movement) a public medium: the first edition (2019) ran for 24 hours on the 80 screens of Amsterdam Centraal with 400 works by 253 designers; the 6 October 2022 edition took over more than 5,000 screens across the Netherlands with 815 works chosen from more than 5,300 submissions; the 2025 Cities edition showed more than 600 works chosen from 5,400 submissions across 15 cities in 7 countries; the next edition is announced for 28 January 2027. Studio Dumbar's creative director Liza Enebeis is the method's best-known voice. Appeal, one of the twelve principles in Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston's The Illusion of Life (1981), holds that a character is appealing when its inner life is readable in its line; John Lasseter, a Disney-trained animator, carried the principles to Pixar, and Glen Keane (Disney: Ariel, the Beast; the Oscar-winning short Dear Basketball, 2017) teaches that a pose must show what the character is thinking. See 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md and 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md.

### 6.6 Editing and film

Vashi Nedomansky publishes shot-count and cut-rate analyses that make editorial rhythm measurable. Deakins thinks from the light source and its direction before the lens; Emmanuel Lubezki thinks in natural light and wide lenses (The Revenant, 2015). Jonathan Glazer thinks in the image as evidence: The Zone of Interest (2023) was shot with about ten fixed, concealed cameras inside the house and no crew in the rooms, so the actors played whole scenes without a set around them. ### 6.8 AI product design, as of October 2026

The interface problem of this decade is designing for a system whose output is uncertain and whose agency competes with the user's. Jakob Nielsen (Nielsen Norman Group, 18 June 2023) called it the third user-interface paradigm after batch processing and command-based interaction: intent-based outcome specification, where the user states the outcome and the system chooses the method, and he listed its costs (opaque results, hard refinement). The established principle sets predate the current models. Saleema Amershi and colleagues' 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (Microsoft, CHI 2019) were tested by 49 design practitioners against 20 AI products and are grouped by when they apply: initially, during interaction, when wrong, and over time. Google PAIR's People + AI Guidebook (2019, updated 2021) and Microsoft's HAX Toolkit turn the same ground into worksheets; see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 9.1. Emily Campbell's The Shape of AI pattern library organises the responses into six families (wayfinders, inputs, tuners, governors, trust builders, identifiers) with about 57 patterns when checked for this file. Amelia Wattenberger's "Why Chatbots Are Not the Future" (2023) argues for interfaces that expose controls and intermediate states; Maggie Appleton's "The Expanding Dark Forest and Generative AI" (2023) and her language-model sketches argue for designing the proof of human presence; Linus Lee's prototypes (Notion AI and his own experiments) treat the model's latent space (the internal space of representations from which it generates outputs) as a navigable material. Jordan Singer's work at Figma put generation inside the design canvas, next to the frames it changes.

Published positions and observable decisions: no general interface-principle document from Anthropic was found as of October 2026; the published design position is the frontend-design skill in the anthropics/skills repository, which aims at distinctive interfaces that avoid generic AI aesthetics (28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 4.14). The observable decisions are the Artifacts side panel (2024), which keeps the conversation and the made thing in separate panes, and the Claude Design research preview (launched by Anthropic Labs on 17 April 2026) with a chat beside a live canvas. Anthropic's Labs team also published "Harness design for long-running application development" (24 March 2026), in which a generator agent and an evaluator agent iterate on front-end design in a loop: blind variation and selective retention (2.15) built into a tool. Apple's HIG gained a Generative AI page on 9 June 2025, revised on 8 June 2026 with guidance on letting people refine results and give feedback during generation. OpenAI's observable decisions are the single input, Canvas (October 2024) as a side document, and the hardware direction after OpenAI acquired io, the device start-up co-founded by Jony Ive (May 2025, about 6.5 billion dollars in stock), with Ive's LoveFrom remaining independent and leading design. Google's is Material 3 Expressive (7.7) and Gemini inside existing surfaces.

Agency moved the interface problem from the chat turn to supervising an agent (an AI system that carries out multi-step actions on the user's behalf): the interface has to show the plan, ask permission, offer checkpoints, allow interruption and support undo. The visible cases are Claude Code (research preview February 2025, general availability May 2025), ChatGPT agent (July 2025) and the ChatGPT Atlas browser (October 2025). Design consequences the house applies: show uncertainty where it exists (confidence, sources, the intermediate step), which is Amershi's "make clear why the system did what it did"; make every generated action reversible and every agent step interruptible, which is "support efficient correction"; show the plan before an agent acts and ask before anything irreversible; keep the user's artefact separate from the model's talk; stream so the wait is legible; and keep the model's default taste away from the brand (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 5).

### 6.9 Creative technology and generative art

Refik Anadol treats data as pigment (Unsupervised at MoMA, 2022 to 2023); his Dataland museum at The Grand LA, Los Angeles, is open as of October 2026 (dataland.art: Tuesday to Sunday, tickets from 49 dollars). Zach Lieberman has posted a daily coding sketch since 2016, and the practice is the method: one constraint, one hour, one post. Casey Reas and Ben Fry built Processing (2001, at the MIT Media Lab) so that sketching in code was possible. Golan Levin (Carnegie Mellon's Studio for Creative Inquiry) teaches computation as a drawing instrument. Mario Klingemann (Memories of Passersby I, 2018; Botto, 2021) designs the system and lets it generate. Holly Herndon (PROTO, 2019; Holly+, 2021) built a model of her own voice and designed its governance. Claire Silver works with AI as a collaborator and names it in the credit. Sougwen Chung draws with robots (the D.O.U.G. series, since 2015) and treats the machine's error as a mark. The conceptual root is Sol LeWitt's "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art" (Artforum, June 1967): "the idea becomes a machine that makes the art", and his wall drawings are carried out by others from written instructions. Vera Molnar (1924 to 2023), plotting with computers from 1968, put a small, deliberate dose of disorder (her "one percent of disorder") inside strict geometric order, the generative counterpart of Abloh's three percent (4.8). Tyler Hobbs (Fidenza, 2021) publishes essays on composition in generative systems, treating flow fields and the probability of each element as design decisions. The shared discipline is the sketch a day: the system is the artwork, and the daily sketch is how the system is tuned. Consequence: in a generative brand system the designer specifies both the rule and the dose of disorder.

### 6.10 Web

Bruno Simon's portfolio (2019) and his Three.js course made the browser a physical space. Locomotive (Montreal; the Locomotive Scroll library) treats scroll as a timeline, and Active Theory (Los Angeles) and Immersive Garden (Paris) design the loading sequence, the first second and the frame budget (the time available to draw one frame: about 16.7 ms at 60 Hz and 8.3 ms at 120 Hz; see 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md section 1.3), as their case studies and talks present the work (reported practice; see 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 8.1). ### 6.11 Japanese thinking

Hara's ex-formation (seminars at Musashino Art University from 2004; Ex-formation, English edition 2015) is the opposite of information: making a known thing unknown again so it can be seen. His emptiness (White, 2010) is a vessel the viewer fills. Fukasawa's without thought is design for the action the body performs unconsciously (the CD player pulled by its cord). Jasper Morrison and Fukasawa's Super Normal (exhibition 2006, book 2007) honours objects that disappear into use. Wabi sabi (Leonard Koren's 1994 account) is the beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete, and is the reference behind the "imperfection on purpose" rule in design-taste section 2. Ma is the interval: the pause between elements that carries meaning, which is the spacing rule behind every luxury page.

### 6.12 Swiss, Dutch and Scandinavian rationalism

Josef Müller-Brockmann's Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981) treats the grid as the expression of a certain mental attitude: an ordering discipline the designer chooses. Emil Ruder's Typographie (1967) and the HfG Ulm (1953 to 1968), with Otl Aicher's programme for the 1972 Munich Olympics, extended the grid into programme design. See 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md sections 2.3 and 2.5 and 05-layout-grids-and-composition.md section 3. Wim Crouwel (Total Design, 1963; the New Alphabet, 1967) designed from the system outward, so that every poster for the Stedelijk followed one grid. Gert Dumbar, who had worked on the Dutch Railways identity at Tel Design, founded his studio in 1977 and built the PTT and Dutch police identities as systems, and under Liza Enebeis the studio's identities became living systems (generative marks whose rules are the identity). Irma Boom makes the book as object: the SHV Think Book (1996) is 2,136 pages made over five years; her Chanel No 5 book (2013) is embossed with no ink. Stockholm Design Lab's reductive identities (SAS, Askul) apply the same discipline in Sweden. The shared structure: design the programme (Gerstner's term for the set of rules and parameters that produces the pieces, 7.8), then let the programme produce the pieces.

### 6.13 India and the Gulf

Charles Correa's Jawahar Kala Kendra in Jaipur (designed from 1986, completed in the early 1990s) maps the navagraha mandala, a nine-square diagram of the nine planets, onto the plan and displaces one square, as Jaipur's own 1727 plan does, so that a cosmology becomes the circulation. His essay "Form follows climate" (1980) gives the Indian frame for the open-to-sky space. B. V. Doshi (Pritzker Prize 2018; his studio Sangath in Ahmedabad and the Aranya low-cost housing in Indore) designed through climate, ritual and incremental growth. The National Institute of Design in Ahmedabad (1961) grew from Charles and Ray Eames's India Report of April 1958 (01-history-and-movements.md section 10.2). In Indian fashion, Rajesh Pratap Singh is the case of reduction, set against Sabyasachi's accumulation (6.7). In the Gulf, Jean Nouvel's dome for the Louvre Abu Dhabi (2017), about 180 metres across and pierced by 7,850 star-shaped openings, carries the filtered shade of the palm frond and the mashrabiya (a carved timber lattice screen) into a "rain of light": a vernacular relation transferred into structure (4.7). For the documented methods of Indian masters see 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md sections 2.25, 3.8 and 7.9. Consequence: a Mask360 brief in Jaipur, Mumbai, Dubai or Abu Dhabi names the regional precedent that already solved a version of the problem, and the chosen frame must differ from it (8.11).

### 6.14 Advertising and campaign thinking

James Webb Young's A Technique for Producing Ideas (1940) is the agency version of Wallas: gather specific and general material, digest it, drop it (incubation), let the idea arrive, then shape it against reality; his line that "an idea is nothing more nor less than a new combination of old elements" is Koestler and Kauffman in advertising terms. Jean-Marie Dru's Disruption (1996, at TBWA) is a three-step method: name the category convention, disrupt it, attach the disruption to a brand vision; it is inversion (4.4) run as an agency procedure. The art director and copywriter pair, made the working unit by Bernbach's Doyle Dane Bernbach from 1949 (Helmut Krone and Julian Koenig on Volkswagen in 1959), is shared thinking between two people: one generates while the other evaluates, then they swap, which separates divergence from convergence (2.1) inside one conversation. Joshua Wolf Shenk's Powers of Two (2014) documents the same structure in other creative pairs. See 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md sections 5.1 to 5.3 for Young's technique, lateral thinking, the 100 bad ideas and the territory method.

## 7. How masters create principles and systems

### 7.1 The cycle

A principle set is made in six steps, and every set dissected below followed them whether or not its author named them.

1. Observe what works in finished work. Rams wrote his principles in the late 1970s after two decades at Braun, looking at his own objects and at the "impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises" around them (Vitsoe's account).
2. Name it. A principle is a short sentence with a verb: "start with user needs", "good design is honest".
3. Test it against counterexamples. Does any admired piece break it? If so, either the piece is wrong or the principle is.
4. Codify it so that it excludes something (7.2). A principle that nothing could violate is a value.
5. Enforce it in review. The crit cites the principle by name; a piece that breaks one is sent back with the principle quoted.
6. Revise it when the work outgrows it. Apple's HIG themes of 2013 were revised in 2025 (7.6); Material's paper metaphor of 2014 became Expressive in 2025 (7.7); GOV.UK added an eleventh principle in 2025 (7.11). A principle set with no revision date is a monument.

Sections 7.3 to 7.11 read each set for its cognitive lesson; the full dissections (origin, what each principle forbids, enforcement, ageing) are in 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.

### 7.2 Principles as decision tools, and the ranked trade-off

Bret Victor's "Inventing on Principle" (CUSEC, Montreal, 20 January 2012) distinguishes having a principle (a specific thing you believe is wrong with the world, which you can recognise being violated and act on) from having a style or a taste. His own principle, that creators need an immediate connection to what they create, is operational: a tool that makes the creator wait violates it, and he can show the violation. The rule the house takes from this: a principle must forbid something. "Be bold" forbids nothing and is a value. "One typeface per brand" forbids a second family and is a principle. "Clarity" is a value until it is written as "every control is labelled in words; no icon stands alone", at which point it forbids the unlabelled icon. Test every line in a principle set by asking what it would send back in a crit; a line that sends nothing back is deleted or rewritten.

Principles get used at the moment two of them collide, so the set is ranked. The Manifesto for Agile Software Development (2001) writes each value as "X over Y" ("individuals and interactions over processes and tools") and adds: "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more". The format forces each principle to name the good thing it will give up. Lean and agile practitioners write the same thing as "even over" statements ("craft even over speed"). IBM's Enterprise Design Thinking (from the mid 2010s) adds three alignment devices: Hills (outcome statements naming who, what and the wow), Playbacks (scheduled reviews in which the team plays the work back to stakeholders) and Sponsor Users (real users who join the team for the project). Rule: rank the set, write one "over" pair for each principle, and when two collide in a crit the higher-ranked one wins and the crit cites the rank.

### 7.3 Rams's ten principles

Formulated from the late 1970s, after Rams had led Braun's design from 1961 (he joined in 1955 and stayed to 1995). Each of the ten, as Vitsoe publishes them, names a way a product can fail (dishonest, obtrusive, short-lived), so the set worked as a filter on every proposal. The cognitive lesson: the tenth, "as little design as possible", is the subtraction move (4.5) written as a rule, and the lineage from the Braun T3 pocket radio (1958) to the first iPod (2001) shows a principle set crossing companies and decades. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.2.

### 7.4 The Vignelli canon

The Vignelli Canon (2010, published as a free PDF) splits into the intangibles (semantics, syntactics, pragmatics, discipline, appropriateness, ambiguity, timelessness and the rest) and the tangibles (paper sizes, grids, typefaces, layout, sequence, identity). The cognitive lesson: each intangible is turned into a setting by a tangible, so "timelessness" forbids the trend and "discipline" forbids the second typeface. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.2.

### 7.5 The Pixar story rules and the Braintrust

The 22 rules of storytelling began as tweets by Emma Coats, then a Pixar storyboard artist, posted over about two months in 2011 and compiled by others; they are her distillation of what she heard in story rooms, and Pixar has never published them as an official document. The institution behind them is the Braintrust (4.12). The cognitive lesson: rule 12, "Discount the 1st thing that comes to mind. And the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th: get the obvious out of the way. Surprise yourself.", is the serial order effect (2.14) and the first-idea trap (9.1) written as a story rule. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.3.

### 7.6 Apple's Human Interface Guidelines and the 2025 and 2026 revisions

The iOS 7 redesign (2013) set three themes, clarity, deference and depth. At WWDC on 9 June 2025 Apple announced Liquid Glass, a translucent material that refracts and reflects its surroundings, for the 26-series systems (shipped 15 September 2025); its guidance entered the HIG the same day, was revised on 28 July, 9 September and 16 December 2025, and reserves the material for the control and navigation layer floating above content (WWDC25 session 219; 06-ui-design-craft.md section 2.1). The HIG changelog records a Design principles page reintroduced on 8 June 2026, alongside iOS 27; read it from the live page before quoting it. The cognitive lesson is 7.1 step 6: themes fixed in 2013 were revised twelve years later. The material is on design-taste's peaking list, so Mask360 uses glass only where the material is the concept, and once. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.4.

### 7.7 Material Design from metaphor to Expressive

Material Design (Google I/O, June 2014) was built on a metaphor of paper and ink with physical light and shadow, so that every layer's height and shadow had a rule. Material You (2021) drew colour from the user's wallpaper. Material 3 Expressive appeared in May 2025, ahead of Google I/O (20 May 2025), after Google's own post went out early and was withdrawn; Google cited 46 research studies with more than 18,000 participants and reported that participants spotted key elements, such as the email send button, up to four times faster than in Material 3. These are Google's own figures and have not been peer-reviewed. The cognitive lesson: the metaphor had become decoration, and the revision named emotion as the function. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.4.

### 7.8 Gerstner's Designing Programmes

Karl Gerstner's Designing Programmes (1964) holds that the designer designs the programme (the set of rules and parameters) and the programme produces the pieces. His tool is the morphological box, adapted from Fritz Zwicky: parameters in rows (typeface, size, colour, position, material), options in columns, and a design as one path through the box. The box makes the solution space explicit, exposes which combinations have never been tried, and stops the designer mistaking one path for the only one. It is the direct ancestor of the design token (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md section 3) and of the living identity (7.12).

### 7.9 Alexander's pattern language

A Pattern Language (1977) gives 253 patterns from "independent regions" down to "things from your life", each written as a context, a problem stated with its forces, and a solution, linked to the larger patterns it completes and the smaller ones that complete it. The cognitive lesson: the problem field carries the evidence, and a pattern without one is a style, so design systems that copy the component library and drop the problem statements lose the thinking. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.6.

### 7.10 Airbnb's DLS and Linear's method

Karri Saarinen's "Building a Visual Language" (Airbnb Design, 2016) explains the Design Language System: as the team grew, every designer brought a style and old screens never died, so the DLS set four principles (unified, universal, iconic, conversational) and documented components with owners. Saarinen co-founded Linear (2019), whose method opens "There is a lost art of building true quality software" and states rules that each forbid something: productivity software must be purpose-built, because "flexible software lets everyone invent their own workflows, which eventually creates chaos as teams scale"; "2-week cycles are the most common"; the responsibility for an issue lies "with a single person"; "short specs are more likely to be read"; and "sometimes the most important thing is to make a decision, and move on". The cognitive lesson: each rule forbids a habit (the flexible workflow, the ownerless issue, the long spec, the endless deliberation). Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md sections 4.9 and 11.5.

### 7.11 Stripe, Polaris and GOV.UK

Stripe has not published a formal design-principles page as far as could be verified in October 2026; its method is visible in Katie Dill's talks on quality as a leadership behaviour. Polaris's earlier experience-values page now redirects to shopify.dev, where Polaris is documented as a web-component UI framework, so its earlier values are quoted from an archived copy or left out. The UK Government Design Principles were first published on 3 April 2012, and an eleventh, "Minimise environmental impact", was added on 2 April 2025. "Start with user needs" stands first, Ben Terrett, then GDS head of design, called it the most important, and the guidance warns that "what they ask for isn't always what they need". The cognitive lesson: every one of the eleven forbids something; "Do less" forbids the feature. Dissection: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.5.

### 7.12 Living identities and editorial systems

Studio Dumbar's identities under Liza Enebeis are generative: the identity is the rule set and the software that applies it, and the output varies while the identity holds. Bureau Borsche's editorial systems for Zeit Magazin and the Bayerische Staatsoper fix the structure (grid, typeface, hierarchy) and allow one gesture per issue. Both are Gerstner's programme in production. For a Mask360 hospitality client the equivalent is an identity defined as a rule set (the mark's behaviour, the type scale, the two colours, the one motion) that the hotel's own team can apply for years.

### 7.13 The Mask360 design-taste rules as a live principle set

The house rules in design-taste sections 2 and 7 are a principle set built by the cycle in 7.1, and the house's working example. Each line forbids something: Space Grotesk only forbids a second family; two colours plus one accent forbids a palette; 70 visual to 30 text forbids the deck wall of words; no em dashes forbids a punctuation habit; "execute the brief only" forbids the unrequested extra. The set has an observation base (the canon file), a test battery (section 4 of the skill), an enforcement format (the critique format in section 8 of the skill) and a revision file dated 2026 (currency-2026.md). When writing a principle set for a client, copy this shape and write the content from the client's own best work (7.14). The full dissection is in 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 11.7.

### 7.14 Writing a principle set for a client in 90 minutes

This is the short form of the one-day method in 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md section 12; use the day when the client's team is in the room.

| Minutes | Step | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 15 | Observe: lay out the client's five best pieces and five from the canon world (design-taste, references/worlds.md); name what the best share in one line each | 10 observation lines |
| 15 to 30 | Name: turn the shared observations into candidate principles with a verb | 8 to 12 candidates |
| 30 to 45 | Test: for each candidate, find one admired piece that breaks it; keep the candidate only if the piece is worse for breaking it | 6 to 8 survivors |
| 45 to 60 | Forbid: rewrite each survivor until it names what it excludes ("image-led" becomes "one image per screen") | 5 to 7 principles |
| 60 to 75 | Rank and set: rank the principles (7.2), write one "X over Y" pair for each, and attach one measurable setting to each (the typeface, the margin ratio, the duration, the colour count) | A ranked list and a one-page token sheet |
| 75 to 90 | Enforcement: write the crit question for each principle ("which element would this principle send back?") and the revision date | The crit card and a date 12 months out |

Five to seven principles is the ceiling; Rams needed ten for a whole discipline and GOV.UK needed eleven for a government. A client brand needs fewer.

### 7.15 A motion language in five rules and a type system in one page

A motion language is written as five rules, each with its setting, because five is the number a team remembers: the one move that carries the brand, the easing family (one curve for entrances, one for exits), the duration band (for example 200 to 400 ms for interface, 600 to 1,200 ms for narrative), what never moves, and the stagger rule. The method and the settings are in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md sections 3 to 5, and the brand use in section 14. A type system on one page states the family and the weights in use, the scale (the ratio and the number of steps), the measure and leading per step, the two or three roles (display, text, micro), and the forbidden settings (tracked caps on every label, any weight the scale does not name). The method is in 03-typography.md section 7.

## 8. The operating system of a master designer

The procedure Claude runs on a creative task. Time shares are of elapsed project time and are the house defaults, set for a conceptual worker (4.17); for an experimental worker, move 5 percent from 8.2 to 8.5. A two-hour task and a two-month identity use the same shares. Each step names its inputs, outputs, the techniques from sections 2 to 7, the failure mode, and how a master catches it; 8.12 says where to loop back when a step fails.

| Step | Share of time | Inputs | Outputs | Techniques |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 Immerse | 15 percent | Brief, subject, place, material, audience, the canon world, the regional precedent | Immersion notes; the single question drafted | 4.1, 2.7 preparation, 5.4 outside diet, 6.13 |
| 8.2 Frame | 10 percent | Immersion notes | Three frames in Dorst's form; one chosen; the single idea in one sentence; five independent thumbnails; then the board (one near, two middle, one far) with borrowed relations | 3.4, 4.2, 4.3, 2.5, 3.13 |
| 8.3 Generate wide | 15 percent | The frame, the board (then closed) | A breadth sheet of 10 compositions or 30 thumbnails across at least four categories, with one inversion, one cross-domain and one plain direction; a deep run of ten variants in the strongest category; three directions selected in a separate pass | 2.1, 2.14, 2.15, 3.14, 4.4, 4.7, 4.8, 2.16 walk |
| 8.4 Constrain | 5 percent | The three directions | The system: tokens, the "what we are not doing" list, one to three self-chosen input constraints | 2.10, 4.6, 7.14 |
| 8.5 Make | 25 percent | The three directions, the system | Sketch, then prototype or styleframe, then the real thing with real content, early | 3.1, 3.8, 3.9, 4.10 |
| 8.6 Critique | 10 percent | The made thing | Test results; crit notes in the braintrust form; the squint result | 4.12, design-taste section 4 tests, 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md section 5 |
| 8.7 Subtract | 5 percent | The surviving direction | The reduced piece; the one element put back | 4.5, 5.6 |
| 8.8 Polish | 10 percent | The reduced piece | The 2px alignment, the kerning, the easing, the cut point, the ending | 2.12, 03, 12, 16 |
| 8.9 Reflect | 5 percent | The shipped piece and the process record | What the work taught; principle revisions; library additions; the notebook entry | 3.1, 7.1 step 6, 5.1 |

### 8.1 Immerse

Read the brief, then the subject (the hotel's floor plan, the jeweller's stone sources, the developer's site photographs at three times of day), the place (the street, the climate, the light), the material (the stone sample, the paper stock, the fabric), the audience (who holds the phone, in which language, at which hour), the canon world (design-taste, references/worlds.md) and the regional precedent that already solved a version of the problem (6.13). Produce immersion notes in the notebook and a first draft of the single question. Failure mode: skipping to the board, which loads other designers' solutions before the problem is understood (3.13). A master catches it by refusing to open a reference until the single question is written.

### 8.2 Frame

Write three frames in the form "if we look at this as X, then Y follows" (3.4). Choose one; write the single idea as a sentence that describes a feeling (design-taste section 4). Make five independent thumbnails before any reference is opened (3.13). Then build the board of four references by distance (one near, two middle, one far), in abstracted form, with the borrowed relation written for each (2.5), and name the precedent the frame must differ from (6.13). Close the board. Failure mode: one frame, chosen because it arrived first (3.3, 9.1). A master catches it because the rule is three frames, and the second and third are often where the idea is.

### 8.3 Generate wide

Walk first (2.16). Then produce two sheets without judgement (2.1). The breadth sheet: ten compositions or thirty thumbnails in a loose medium (3.8), spread across at least four categories (flexibility), with at least one inversion (4.4), one cross-domain borrowing (4.7) and one deliberately plain direction (4.16); this is the expert's breadth-first habit (3.14). The deep run: ten variants inside the strongest category (persistence, 2.14). Stop on the count. Then, in a separate pass, select three directions against written criteria that include originality, after five minutes of generating on the brief to warm the selector up (2.15). Failure mode: judging during generation, which collapses the count to three safe options; one idea dressed up as ten (vertical transformation disguised as fluency); and stopping when the ideas feel used up, which is the creative cliff illusion. A master catches it by counting categories on the breadth sheet and variants on the deep run.

### 8.4 Constrain

Write the system: the tokens (type, colour, spacing, motion, image treatment), the constraint set in the form "we will not use", and the "what we are not doing" list of the category's tropes (design-taste section 4 step 3). Failure mode: constraining before generating, which makes the constraints habits inherited from the last job; or never constraining, which makes the make stage a negotiation. A master catches it by checking that every constraint excludes something that appeared in 8.3, and by keeping self-chosen input constraints to one to three (2.10).

### 8.5 Make

Sketch the three directions, prototype the one interaction or styleframe the key shot, then build the real thing with real copy, real photography and the real typeface, inside the first fifth of the make time. Work in the loose medium until the structure holds and only then in the precise one (3.8). Failure mode: pixel-perfect too early; lorem ipsum, which hides every typographic problem; polishing a direction that has not passed critique. A master catches it by refusing to open the precise tool before the sketch has survived a squint.

### 8.6 Critique

Run the tests: the seven in design-taste section 4 (step 7), the checklist for the deliverable in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md section 5, and the squint (blur the eyes or zoom to 25 percent: does the hierarchy survive). Hold the crit in the braintrust form (4.12), opened by the director naming a flaw in their own work (2.18). Failure mode: the crit as a list of nitpicks, which design-taste section 8 forbids; the crit with a mandate, which removes the designer's responsibility; the crit by the client before the studio's own. A master catches it by starting every crit with the verdict in one line and the idea problem before the craft problem; a failed idea goes back by the rules in 8.12.

### 8.7 Subtract

Remove elements until the piece breaks, put the last one back, then remove one accessory (design-taste section 4 step 6). Failure mode: subtracting the idea along with the decoration, which produces the empty piece (4.16). A master catches it with the "plain or empty" question and the "one thing remembered" test.

### 8.8 Polish

The two-pixel alignment, the optical kerning of the display line (pairs spaced by eye so the gaps look equal, where metric kerning uses the font's stored values), the easing curve and duration, the cut point on the blink, the ending of the page. Failure mode: polishing as avoidance of a structural problem the crit named; and polishing on a tired eye at the end of the day. A master catches it by polishing in the morning block and by re-running the squint after every polish pass.

### 8.9 Reflect

Write what the work taught in the notebook, revise any principle the work outgrew (7.1 step 6), add the piece and its references to the library with the borrowed relations named, and record the ratio produced to shipped. Failure mode: skipping reflection because the next brief has arrived. A master catches it by scheduling reflection as the first 30 minutes of the next project, where the new brief cannot crowd it out.

### 8.10 Running the operating system inside one Claude session

Claude has no walk, no night's sleep and no wall, so each human condition is replaced by an equivalent that serves the same cognitive function. The equivalents are mandatory on any creative task longer than a single reply.

| Human condition | Function it serves | Claude's equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Walk before generating | Lowers evaluation and widens association (2.16) | Generate in a separate pass with an explicit rule to rank nothing; write the whole list before judging any item |
| A night between generate and present | Incubation and sleep consolidation (2.6, 2.16) | Do other work between the two (the system at 8.4, the copy), then reread the directions cold; never choose in the pass that generated |
| Frames before references and before any model output | Fixation control (3.13, 2.19) | Write the three frames and five thumbnail descriptions before reading references or calling any image or video model; generated output enters only at 8.3, for volume |
| Closing the reference board | Fixation control (3.13) | Write the borrowed relation for each reference in one line, then design from the lines and stop looking at the reference images or URLs |
| A loose medium | Lateral transformation (3.8) | Thumbnails as one-line descriptions or grey-box layouts before any styled HTML; the first styled build (an HTML page or a Claude Artifact) is a structural sketch |
| The count, then a deep run | Persistence and the creative cliff (2.14) | Write the full count even when later items feel weaker, then ten variants of the strongest; the feeling of running dry is ignored |
| The spread of the set | Collective diversity (2.19, 2.13) | Read the one-line descriptions of all compositions side by side before selecting; where two share a frame, a palette or a layout skeleton, replace one |
| A selector who has just generated | Selection bias (2.15) | Select in a fresh pass, with criteria written first and originality named among them |
| The crit room | Selective retention (2.15, 4.12) | A written pass of the design-taste tests and the checklist in 23-critique-taste-and-craft-judgement.md section 5, verdict line first, then a reread as the client would see it: on a phone, in Mumbai or Dubai, in the second language |
| The library in the head | Chunking and precedent (2.11, 3.13) | Load the category's world in design-taste, references/worlds.md and the relevant masters in 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md before framing |
| Late ideas beat early ones | The serial order effect and the master's rut (2.14, 9.15) | Treat the first three ideas for any brief as the category default; keep one only if it survives comparison with the later ones |
| A model's own taste | Steep associative hierarchy (2.13, 9.12) | Check every output against the cheap-tells list in design-taste section 3; the cream, serif and terracotta default is rejected on sight |

What reaches the user follows Bierut and the house rule "execute the brief only": one recommendation, the frame it came from, the borrowed relation for each reference, and the count it was chosen from. The alternatives stay internal unless the user asks for options.

Ten working days are about 80 hours; the shares in the table at the head of this section become the plan below. The client is a restored haveli inside Jaipur's walled city (a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2019); guests book on a phone from Mumbai, Delhi, Dubai and London; the identity must work in English and Devanagari (03-typography.md section 15).

| Days | Step and hours | What happens |

## 9. Creativity blockers and how masters avoid them

| Blocker | What it is | Cognitive basis | How masters avoid it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9.1 The first idea trap | The first arrival is taken as the best because it is the most available | Mednick's steep hierarchy (2.13); Jansson and Smith fixation (3.13) | Log the first idea, set it aside, require three frames and ten compositions; Coats's rule 12 (discount the first five ideas) |
| 9.2 Reference regurgitation | The reference's surface reproduced with no borrowed relation named | Surface mapping (2.5); fixation on the example | Name the relation; open the board only after five thumbnails and close it before making; the 3 percent change declared (4.8) |
| 9.3 The fear of plain | Adding elements because plainness feels like absence | Taste anxiety (5.5) | The "plain or empty" question; Prada, Philo and Muji as permission; the one-thing-remembered test |
| 9.4 The clever trap | The idea exists to display the designer's cleverness at the brief's expense | Bisociation without a purpose (2.4) | Bierut's one idea explained to the client; "is any word selling" extended to "is any element showing off" |
| 9.5 Perfectionism and the unfinished | Vertical refinement before the structure holds; nothing ships | Goel (3.8); flow miscalibrated (2.8) | Loose medium first; a shipping date in the brief; Linear's rule "decide and move on" |
| 9.6 Decoration as avoidance | Ornament added where the idea is weak | Polish as avoidance (8.8) | Idea problem first in the crit; subtract before polish |
| 9.7 Over-research | Reading replaces making because reading feels safe | Preparation without the make loop (3.1) | The 25 percent cap on immersion and framing (5.12); the first real-content draft within the first fifth of the make stage |
| 9.8 Under-constraint | Freedom produces the habitual answer | Stokes; the inverted U (2.10) | One to three self-chosen input constraints; write "we will not use" before making |
| 9.9 The committee | Group generation and group approval average the work | Diehl and Stroebe (2.17); Girotra et al. (2.18); the braintrust's no-mandate rule | Silent writing, then 6-3-5, then discussion (2.18); one owner decides (Linear, Pixar); the director names a flaw in their own work first |
| 9.10 The trend trap | Adopting what is peaking because it is visible | Currency (design-taste section 6) | The 2029 test; the currency file read before the board |
| 9.11 Tool seduction | The tool's new feature becomes the idea | 4.11 | Learn tools in practice time; on the job push one step only |
| 9.12 AI default convergence | The model's median taste (cream, serif, terracotta; the symmetrical hero) becomes the output, and every studio's set narrows | Steep hierarchy in the model (2.13); collective narrowing (Doshi and Hauser, 2.19); see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md section 5 | Frame before any model output; prompt from the named reference and the borrowed relation; reject the first generation on principle; measure the spread of the set; the cheap-tells list |
| 9.13 Burnout and the pace problem | The maker schedule destroyed; no incubation; decisions made tired | 2.6, 2.16, 2.20, 4.13 | Protected blocks; the walk; sleep before sign-off; the pressured project run as a mission; the sabbatical in some form (a week a year with no brief) |
| 9.14 The taste plateau | Output stabilises at competent; the eye stops improving | Perceptual learning needs new discriminations (2.12); the library stops widening (2.9) | Change the diet (5.4), with depth abroad favoured over breadth; paired-comparison drills at a harder level; a crit from outside the studio; one project a year in a discipline the designer does not know |
| 9.15 The master's rut | A learned procedure keeps being applied where a better one exists | Einstellung and functional fixedness (Luchins 1942, Duncker 1945); Bilalić, McLeod and Gobet 2008; cognitive entrenchment (Dane 2010) | A move of house or medium; a sabbatical; a crit from outside the discipline; a senior's first solution logged and set aside like a junior's |
| 9.16 Stopping when it feels empty | The session ends when ideas feel used up | The creative cliff illusion (Lucas and Nordgren 2020; 2.14) | The count is the stopping rule; the deep run of ten |
| 9.17 Selecting the safe one | Selectors favour the feasible and conventional | Rietzschel et al. 2006; Mueller et al. 2012 (2.15) | Selection as a separate step with originality named; a forecast from a peer outside the project |

The master's rut (9.15) is the blocker of experts, and its evidence is specific. Abraham Luchins's water-jar studies (Psychological Monographs, 1942) showed that people who had learned a working procedure kept using it when a simpler one was available: the Einstellung or set effect. Karl Duncker's candle problem (1945) named functional fixedness, the inability to see an object outside its usual function. Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet ("Why good thoughts block better ones", Cognition, 2008) eye-tracked chess experts who said they were searching for a better move while their eyes kept returning to the familiar one; only players well above the problem's level escaped. Erik Dane (Academy of Management Review, 2010) calls the general condition cognitive entrenchment: deep domain expertise reduces flexibility. For Claude, the familiar solution is the strongest prior, which is why 8.10 treats the first three ideas as the category default.

The plateau and the rut are both broken by discomfort on purpose. Sagmeister's sabbatical, Abloh's move from architecture to fashion, Glazer's move from commercials to a film with no crew in the room, Anderson's move from Loewe to Dior and Blazy's move from a leather house to a tweed house are all the same move.

## 10. Creativity under commercial conditions

### 10.1 Protecting the idea through clients and production

Bierut's method is to present one recommendation with its reasoning and to show the alternatives as what was considered and rejected, so the client's decision is between a reasoned idea and its absence. Showing the reasoning before the work also lowers the uncertainty that drives the bias against novelty (Mueller, Melwani and Goncalo, 2.15). Saville's method with Factory was total creative freedom in exchange for total commercial risk, and it produced both the sleeves and the lateness; the house takes the freedom and adds the deadline. Deakins protects the image by agreeing it in preparation: shot lists and lighting plans worked through with the director (Villeneuve, Mendes, the Coens) before the shoot, so that on the day the decisions are already made and the pressure of the set cannot unmake them. The shared principle: the idea is protected by moving the decision earlier and writing it down.

### 10.2 The brief as a creative instrument

The brief is rewritten by the studio and returned to the client for agreement before any making. It contains the single question (4.2), the single idea in one sentence, the three frames considered, the "what we are not doing" list, the constraint set, the tokens, the creativity dimension the work is built on (usually originality plus elaboration, 10.7), the tests that will be used to judge the work, and the date. A brief with those nine items turns the presentation from a taste argument into a test result: the client agreed the tests before the work existed. The format is in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md section 2.

### 10.3 How Pentagram partners run a project

Pentagram (founded in London in 1972) lists 23 partners as of October 2026, all practising designers; in its own words "the owners of the business are the creators of the work and serve as the primary contact for every client". Each partner runs a small team, with no account layer between designer and client. The partner is in the first meeting and the last. The work is critiqued by other partners at partner meetings. The economics are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md; the thinking consequence is that the person who frames the problem is the person who presents the answer, which keeps the single idea intact through production.

### 10.4 How Buck runs a job

Buck's production order, as the studio presents it in talks and case studies (reported practice; no published method document was found), is brief, then ideation on boards, then styleframes signed off by the client, then an animatic (the timed sequence of still frames with sound), then animation, then polish. The client decision is concentrated at the styleframe, where change is cheap, and the animation stage is protected from redesign because the look is locked. The house applies the same lock to every motion and video job: no animation starts before the styleframe is approved (13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md section 2).

### 10.5 How Fincher runs a set

Fincher's set is a control system: a shot list that binds the day, a camera that holds still unless the movement is motivated, digital capture that permits many takes, and repetition until the take is right. The editor works with him from the first day, and the number of takes is budgeted as method. The transferable rule for Mask360 shoots (art-direction skill; 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md) is that control is bought in preparation and spent on the day.

### 10.6 The agency reality and the house process

The agency conditions that destroy the structure are known: the brief that arrives as a deck of competitor screenshots, the three-options request, the client crit before the studio crit, the two-day turnaround that deletes incubation and, on Amabile's diary evidence, lowers creative thinking (2.20), and the account layer that paraphrases the work. The house process in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md and 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md answers each: the rewritten brief (10.2), one idea presented, the studio crit fixed in the calendar, a minimum of one night between generate and present, the designer in the room, and a short turnaround priced as a creativity cost and run as a mission (one task, the designer shielded from other briefs).

### 10.7 The evidence that protects the idea

Werner Reinartz and Peter Saffert ("Creativity in advertising: when it works and when it doesn't", Harvard Business Review, June 2013) scored 437 campaigns on five creativity dimensions adapted from the divergent-thinking literature (originality, flexibility, elaboration, synthesis and artistic value) and linked them to sales. The more creative campaigns had markedly larger sales effects, elaboration and originality were the strongest dimensions, and artistic value on its own added least; read the article before quoting effect sizes. Peter Field's analysis of the IPA Databank with the Gunn Report ("The link between creativity and effectiveness", IPA, 2010) found creatively awarded campaigns markedly more efficient than unawarded ones, and his "The Crisis in Creative Effectiveness" (IPA, 2019) found that by 2018 the advantage had largely gone as short-term activation replaced long campaigns. The figures and the house awards position are in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md sections 4.5 and 15.4. Consequence: the protected idea is argued to the client as an effectiveness decision with a duration, because creativity pays over long campaigns; and the rewritten brief (10.2) names the creativity dimension the work is built on.

### 11.3 Running a studio that thinks like this

Rituals, in calendar order: the walk and the block (mornings, no meetings before 13:00 on at least three days); the reference channel review (Monday, 30 minutes, every active client); the ideation session (10 minutes of silent writing, then 6-3-5, then discussion; 2.18); selection as its own step with written criteria naming originality and an outside forecaster (2.15); the crit (two fixed hours a week, braintrust form, verdict first, opened by the director naming a flaw in their own work); the principle review (quarterly: which principle did the work outgrow, which rule sent nothing back and should be deleted); the library day (one half-day a quarter spent adding to the outside diet, with the relations written); the annual week with no brief (the house sabbatical in miniature). Objects: real materials of each client's category on the tables; a printed wall and a box per client; daylight. Measures kept per quarter: produced to shipped ratio; categories per generate session; the spread of each generate set (2.19); creative quality by the Consensual Assessment Technique, with three independent raters (two from the studio, one from outside) scoring each shipped piece blind before any discussion (2.20); the crit verdict distribution (expensive, competent, cheap); and the number of principle revisions.

### 11.4 How a creative director teaches thinking

Teach the question before the answer: ask "what is the quietest version" and wait. Teach the frame: ask for three before accepting one. Teach the relation: when a designer shows a reference, ask what is borrowed and refuse "the feel". Teach the count: ask how many existed before this one, and whether the designer stopped on the count or on the feeling. Teach the crit by running it in the braintrust form and by naming the idea problem before the craft problem, so that the designer learns to see the structure under the surface. Teach the level above: place each designer on the rubric in 11.1 and coach the behaviour of the next level up, one level at a time. Teach reflection by reading the notebook entry aloud at the start of the next project. Teach by example of the body: sketch by hand in the room, stand for the editing pass, close the laptop for the walk. The measure of the teaching is that the designer's self-tests (11.1) are answered without prompting.

## Terms introduced

- 6-3-5 brainwriting: six people each write three ideas in five minutes and pass the sheet on, for six rounds, up to 108 ideas in 30 minutes without production blocking (Rohrbach).
- abductive reasoning: inference to the best explanation; given a desired value and no known form, proposing a form that would deliver it (Cross, Dorst).
- adjacent possible: the set of states reachable in one step from what currently exists; the edge innovation explores (Kauffman, Johnson).
- affinity diagram: observations written one per note and clustered by similarity until themes emerge.
- affordance: what an object permits a user to do; a signifier is what tells the user so (Norman).
- agent: an AI system that carries out multi-step actions on the user's behalf, which the interface must let the user supervise.
- Amusement Park Theoretical model: creativity as a hierarchy of general requirements, thematic areas, domains and micro-domains (Baer and Kaufman).
- analogical distance: how far the source domain of an example sits from the problem's domain; a middle range works best (Chan, Fu).
- animatic: a timed sequence of still frames with sound, used to test a motion piece before animation.
- appeal: the animation principle that a character's inner life is readable in its drawing (Thomas and Johnston).
- atypical combination: a pairing of elements rarely seen together; high impact comes from one such intrusion in a conventional base (Uzzi).
- bias against creativity: the implicit preference for familiar ideas under uncertainty, even among people who say they value creativity (Mueller, Melwani and Goncalo).
- bisociation: the collision of two self-consistent, habitually separate frames of reference that produces a creative act (Koestler).
- blind variation and selective retention: the model of creativity as variations produced without foreknowledge of success, then selected (Campbell).
- Braintrust: Pixar's review ritual in which directors and story leads critique reels with candour and without authority to mandate changes.
- breadth-first: working on the whole design at low resolution before going deep on any part, the habit of experts; depth-first is the novice's habit of finishing one component first (Ball and Ormerod).
- breadth sheet and deep run: the house generate format: ten compositions across at least four categories, then ten variants inside the strongest category.
- cap height: the height of the capital letters of a typeface.
- CGI: computer-generated imagery.
- chunking: encoding a complex pattern as one familiar unit, the basis of expert recognition (Chase and Simon).
- CNC: computer numerically controlled machining, used to cut physical models directly from digital files.
- co-evolution: the process in which a partial solution reveals a new aspect of the problem, which reshapes the solution (Dorst and Cross).
- cognitive entrenchment: the loss of flexibility that comes with deep domain expertise (Dane).
- componential model: creativity as the product of domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes and intrinsic task motivation (Amabile).
- conceptual and experimental innovators: Galenson's two types: the first plans from a preconceived idea and peaks young, the second searches by trial and peaks late.
- conceptual blending: mapping two input mental spaces into a blended space that carries emergent structure (Fauconnier and Turner).
- Consensual Assessment Technique: rating creative products by several domain experts working independently, with their agreement checked (Amabile).
- control characters: the glyphs drawn first in a typeface (n, o, H, O, then a, e, d, p, v) because they set the stems, bowls, heights and spacing of the rest.
- convergent thinking: narrowing to the single correct answer (Guilford).
- creative cliff illusion: the mistaken expectation that creativity declines across an ideation session (Lucas and Nordgren).
- cycle: in Linear's method, a fixed-length work period, commonly two weeks, into which unfinished work rolls over.
- dailies: the daily review of the previous day's footage or animation in front of the director.
- deep work: a block of uninterrupted, cognitively demanding work (Newport).
- default mode network: the brain system active in memory, imagination and unfocused thought.
- deliberate practice: effortful activity designed to improve performance, at the edge of ability, with feedback (Ericsson).
- design engineer: a designer who builds interfaces in production code, so design decisions are made in the material.
- design fixation: adherence to the features of an example solution, including its flaws, even when instructed to avoid them (Jansson and Smith).
- Divergent Association Task: naming ten nouns as unrelated as possible, scored by their average semantic distance (Olson and colleagues).
- divergent thinking: producing many candidate answers to an open problem, scored by fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration (Guilford).
- domain and field: in Csikszentmihalyi's systems model, the symbolic rules and works of a practice, and the gatekeepers who decide which novelty enters it.
- dual pathway to creativity: originality reached either by flexibility (switching categories) or by persistence (going deep in a few) (Nijstad and colleagues).
- Einstellung effect: the persistence of a learned procedure when a simpler or better one is available (Luchins, Bilalić).
- elaboration: the detail added to an idea, one of Guilford's four divergent scores.
- eleven-star exercise: extrapolating one moment of an experience from one star to eleven and then selecting the feasible point (Chesky).
- emptiness: Hara's concept of the object or image as a vessel the viewer fills with meaning.
- equal odds rule: a creator's number of hits is a linear function of total output with a roughly constant ratio (Simonton).
- even-over statement: a principle written as "A even over B", naming the good thing it gives up.
- everydays: Beeple's practice of one finished image per day since 1 May 2007.
- executive control network: the brain system that sustains attention and evaluates.
- ex-formation: making a known thing unknown again so that it can be seen (Hara).
- eye-trace: where the viewer's eye sits on the frame across a cut, one of Murch's six criteria.
- five planes: Garrett's layers of user-experience decisions from abstract to concrete: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface.
- flexibility: the number of categories of idea produced, one of Guilford's four scores.
- flow: absorbed performance under clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge slightly above skill (Csikszentmihalyi).
- fluency: the count of ideas produced, one of Guilford's four scores.
- fMRI: functional magnetic resonance imaging, which maps brain activity through changes in blood oxygenation.
- Four C model: mini-c, little-c, Pro-c and Big-C creativity, from personal insight to domain-changing work (Kaufman and Beghetto).
- frame: the statement of what kind of problem this is, in the form "if we look at the situation as X, then Y follows"; reframing is changing it (Schön, Dorst).
- frame budget: the time available to draw one frame: about 16.7 ms at 60 Hz and 8.3 ms at 120 Hz.
- functional fixedness: the inability to see an object outside its usual function (Duncker).
- Geneplore: the two-phase model of generation (preinventive structures) and exploration (interpretation) (Finke, Ward and Smith).
- guarantor of design: what warrants a design decision, located by Nelson and Stolterman in the client's desiderata.
- Hills, Playbacks and Sponsor Users: IBM Enterprise Design Thinking's outcome statements, scheduled alignment reviews and real users embedded in the team.
- hybrid ideation: people generate alone first and then build on the pooled ideas together, which beats team brainstorming on the best idea (Girotra, Terwiesch and Ulrich).
- IKEA effect: the higher value people put on things they made themselves (Norton, Mochon and Ariely).
- ill-structured problem: a problem whose goal, constraints and operators are not all given (Simon).
- immersion: the move of loading the brief, subject, place, material, audience and canon before any making.
- incubation: a scheduled gap after preparation during which unconscious processing continues (Wallas).
- intent-based outcome specification: the interface paradigm in which the user states the desired outcome and the system chooses the method (Nielsen).
- inter-rater reliability: the agreement between independent raters of the same work.
- intimation: Wallas's felt sense, just before illumination, that a solution is approaching.
- intrinsic motivation: doing the work for its own interest, the motivational component Amabile found most tied to creativity.
- inversion: the move of generating the opposite, the extreme scale or the sole element as a direction.
- inverted U: the relation in which creativity rises with constraints up to a moderate level and falls beyond it (Acar and colleagues).
- kind and wicked learning environments: environments where feedback is fast and accurate, and those where it is delayed, missing or misleading (Hogarth).
- kore de ii: "this will do", Muji's stated aim of satisfaction by sufficiency, set against kore ga ii, "this is what I want" (Hara).
- latent space: the internal space of representations from which a generative model produces its outputs.
- lateral transformation: moving from one idea to a different idea; vertical transformation is refining the same idea (Goel).
- levels of expertise: Dorst's seven levels: naive, novice, advanced beginner, competent, expert, master, visionary.
- Liquid Glass: Apple's translucent interface material announced on 9 June 2025 for the 26-series operating systems.
- living identity: an identity defined as a rule set and the software that applies it, whose output varies while the identity holds (Studio Dumbar).
- ma: the Japanese concept of the interval or pause between elements that carries meaning.
- maker schedule: a calendar of half-day or full-day blocks without meetings (Graham).
- mashrabiya: a carved timber lattice screen of Arab architecture that filters light and view.
- matra: a vowel sign written above, below or beside a Devanagari consonant.
- MAYA: "most advanced yet acceptable", the finding that typicality and novelty jointly predict aesthetic preference (Hekkert and colleagues).
- mission and treadmill: Amabile's terms for a pressured day focused on one protected task, and a pressured day fragmented across many.
- morphological box: a grid of parameters (rows) and options (columns) in which a design is one path (Gerstner, after Zwicky).
- N1: the first and lightest stage of sleep, at sleep onset (Lacaux and colleagues).
- navagraha mandala: a nine-square diagram of the nine planets used in Indian architecture and city planning.
- nominal group: people generating ideas alone whose output is pooled, the baseline against which interacting groups are measured.
- opportunity solution tree: an outcome at the top, opportunities beneath, solutions beneath those, experiments at the leaves (Torres).
- optical kerning: spacing letter pairs by eye so the gaps look equal; metric kerning uses the values stored in the font.
- originality: the statistical rarity of an idea, one of Guilford's four scores.
- overshoot: the small excess by which round letters pass the baseline and cap line so they look the same size as flat ones.
- pattern: a context, a problem with its forces, and a solution, linked to larger and smaller patterns (Alexander).
- perceptual learning module: a training format of many short discrimination trials with immediate feedback (Kellman).
- performative type: typography whose meaning is carried by its movement.
- precedent library: the stock of prior solutions an experienced designer carries and recognises as applicable (Lawson).
- preinventive structure: a mental form produced before any meaning is assigned to it (Finke, Ward and Smith).
- primary generator: the single strong idea an architect chooses early to narrow the problem enough to start (Darke, Lawson).
- principle: a statement that forbids something and can be cited to send work back; a value is an aspiration that forbids nothing (after Bret Victor).
- production blocking: the loss of ideas in interacting groups caused by waiting to speak (Diehl and Stroebe).
- programme: in Gerstner's sense, the set of rules and parameters that produces the pieces of a design.
- progress principle: the finding that progress in meaningful work is the strongest daily motivator (Amabile and Kramer).
- prototype: a specific, resolved build that tests a known question; a sketch is quick, cheap, disposable and ambiguous (Buxton).
- psychological safety: a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk, the condition for candid error reporting (Edmondson).
- purpose-built software: Linear's term for productivity software designed for one way of working, set against flexible tools.
- ranked trade-off: a principle set written as "X over Y" pairs and ranked, so the higher principle wins a collision (after the Agile Manifesto).
- ratio of looking to making: the house weekly split of about 30 percent looking, 50 percent making, 20 percent reflecting.
- readymade: an existing object repositioned as art by context and signature (Duchamp).
- recognition-primed decision: acting on the first workable option an expert recognises (Klein).
- reflection in action: thinking by acting in the material and reading its response (Schön).
- remote associates: distant associations reachable from a cue; the Remote Associates Test asks for the word linking three others (Mednick).
- resistance: the daily avoidance of creative work (Pressfield).
- rule of six: Murch's weighted criteria for a cut: emotion 51 percent, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, two-dimensional plane 5, three-dimensional space 4.
- salience network: the brain system that switches between the default and executive networks.
- satisficing: accepting the first option that meets a threshold because full search is impossible (Simon).
- seeing as and seeing that: the two readings of a sketch, figural possibility and non-figural fact, alternating during design (Goldschmidt).
- semantic network: a map of how strongly concepts are linked in a person's memory (Kenett).
- semantics, syntactics, pragmatics: Vignelli's three questions: what it means, how it is built, whether it works.
- sensemaking: the social process of giving data meaning during synthesis (Kolko).
- serial order effect: the finding that ideas produced later in a divergent session are rated more original than early ones (Beaty and Silvia).
- shirorekha: the headline that joins the letters of a Devanagari word.
- sidebearing: the space on either side of a glyph that sets its spacing before kerning.
- single idea: the one sentence, written as a feeling, that a piece serves; the house rule.
- single question: the one question under twelve words asked before generating.
- spread of the set: how varied a generated set is across frames, palettes and layout skeletons; the house measure against collective narrowing.
- squint: blurring the eyes or zooming out to test whether a hierarchy survives.
- story reel: storyboard drawings timed to scratch dialogue and cut together as a rough film before animation.
- structure mapping: analogy as the mapping of systems of relations between domains (Gentner).
- Structure of Intellect: Guilford's model classifying intellectual abilities by operation, content and product into 120 cells.
- styleframe: a single still frame that fixes the final look of a shot before animation.
- subtraction: removing elements until the piece breaks, then putting one back.
- super normal: objects that disappear into use (Morrison and Fukasawa).
- systems model: Csikszentmihalyi's account of creativity as the interaction of person, domain and field.
- tame problem: a problem with a definitive formulation, a stopping rule and an answer that can be tested as right or wrong.
- taste plateau: the state in which output stabilises at competent and the eye stops improving.
- test battery: the written set of tests that supplies the stopping rule for a wicked problem.
- the gap: the years in which a maker's taste outruns their ability (Ira Glass).
- three percent approach: changing an existing object by about three percent and declaring the source (Abloh).
- token: a named design value (a colour, a size, a duration) shared across a system; see 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md.
- trompe l'oeil: making that deceives the eye into reading one material as another.
- variable axis: a continuous design range, such as weight or width, inside one font file.
- Vorkurs: the Bauhaus preliminary course built on exercises and critique.
- wabi sabi: the beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete.
- Wallas four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination, verification, with intimation inside illumination.
- WebGL: the browser's interface for drawing 3D graphics on the graphics card.
- "what we are not doing" list: the written refusal of a category's tropes, made before making.
- wicked problem: a problem with no definitive formulation, no stopping rule and no true or false solutions (Rittel and Webber).
- without thought: design for the action the body already performs unconsciously (Fukasawa).
- x-height: the height of a typeface's lowercase letters without ascenders.
