## 1. How to read this file

### 1.1 The method note

Every method claim below is tied to a source named inline, graded as follows:

- Primary: the designer's own book, essay, lecture, published process post or studio case study. Example: Kris Sowersby's Söhne design information essay on klim.co.nz (26 November 2019).
- Near-primary: a long interview or documentary in which the designer speaks for themselves. Example: Gary Hustwit's film "Rams" (2018).
- Reconstructed: a method inferred from the finished work and from third-party accounts because the designer published no process. Marked "reconstructed" (or "reconstructed from the work") wherever it applies. Example: Peter Saville's found-image method (section 2.11).

No quotation appears inside quotation marks unless it is a title, a principle's exact name, or a line the source prints verbatim. Everything else is paraphrase. Where a number is secondhand (press coverage, a trade report) the entry says so. Shorter dissections of several of the same principle sets, and the creation cycle behind them, are in 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.

### 1.2 How to borrow a method

A method is a sequence of decisions with reasons. A style is the residue those decisions left on one body of work. Borrow the sequence; the residue belongs to its author. The test, applied to every entry: can the move be stated without naming the master's visual signature? Rand's one-solution rule can be stated without the IBM stripes. Carson's "intuition over grid" cannot be stated without Carson, which is why it appears here as a warning as much as a method.

Each entry gives the background and the documented method, the principle set where one exists, and ends with "steal" (the one move) and "trap" (what imitation produces). Entries are compact on purpose; the sources section carries the reading.

### 1.3 Why this matters for a 360 creative director

Mask360 sells decisions to clients in Mumbai, Jaipur, Delhi, Goa, Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Riyadh who will ask "why this?". A method with a named source is an answer. "Rams's sixth principle forbids it" ends a debate about a fake gradient faster than taste alone. Section 12 turns the catalogue into a one-day procedure for writing a client's own principle set, because a borrowed set without a client's own counterexamples is a poster, and posters are ignored.

## 2. Graphic design and identity

### 2.1 Paul Rand (1914 to 1996)

Background: Brooklyn-born, art director at Esquire and Apparel Arts by 23, Weintraub advertising, then the corporate identities that defined American modernism: IBM (1956 onward, the eight-bar logo 1972), Westinghouse (1960), ABC (1962), UPS (1961), Enron (1996), NeXT (1986).

Method, documented in "Thoughts on Design" (1947), "A Designer's Art" (1985) and "Design, Form, and Chaos" (1993):

1. Reduce the problem to one idea that can be stated in a sentence. The IBM "Eye Bee M" poster (1981) is a rebus; the logo is the stripe as a device for lending unity to a set of letters of unequal width.
2. Play. Rand wrote that the play principle, taken from Huizinga's "Homo Ludens" (1938), gives the work its life; the designer sets rules (a grid, a colour count, a constraint) and then plays inside them, and the audience feels the game.
3. Present one solution. Steve Jobs's account of commissioning the NeXT logo (1986, a fee of 100,000 dollars by Jobs's own account) is the near-primary source: Rand refused to supply options, said he would solve the problem and the client could pay and then decide whether to use it. The deliverable was a bound presentation book that walked the client through the reasoning page by page, so the logo arrived as the conclusion of an argument. The ABC and IBM presentations followed the same form.
4. Test the mark at the smallest and ugliest reproduction first (the NeXT book shows the cube at stamp size and in one colour).

Principle set, never numbered and practised in the work: form and content are one; a logo is a flag, so it must be simple enough to draw from memory; the designer's job is to make the ordinary extraordinary; humour is a sign of intelligence.

Signature works: IBM, ABC, UPS, Westinghouse, NeXT, the Eye Bee M poster, the children's books with Ann Rand.

- Steal: the presentation book. Give a Jaipur jeweller or a Dubai developer one recommended route with the reasoning in front of it and one alternative held in reserve; show three routes only at the territory stage. The counter-position is the agency three-route method (safe, recommended, wild), which protects the agency when a client's choice fails; the house departs from it because Mask360 is paid for judgement (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 11.3).
- Trap: the one-solution rule only works if the single solution is the product of forty discarded ones. Imitators present one idea because they had one.

### 2.2 Massimo and Lella Vignelli (1931 to 2014; 1934 to 2016)

Background: Milan-trained architects; Unimark International (1965, with Bob Noorda and others); Vignelli Associates New York 1971. New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual (1970, with Noorda), the 1972 subway diagram, American Airlines (1967), Knoll, Bloomingdale's, the National Park Service Unigrid (1977), the Vignelli Canon.

Method, documented in "The Vignelli Canon" (2010, free PDF then Lars Müller print):

1. Semantics: find the meaning before any form.
2. Syntactics: build a grid, a type scale and a colour system, then obey them.
3. Pragmatics: check that the result can be understood by the person who has to use it.
4. Restrict the typeface palette. The Canon lists the faces Vignelli considered sufficient: Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Futura, Times, Helvetica, with a few later additions. The reason stated: discipline forces the design to live in structure and scale, where the real decisions are.
5. Use scale contrast and white space as the main expressive tools; type sizes limited to a handful per document.

Unigrid (1977): one grid for every National Park Service brochure, printed on paper sizes that fold from a single sheet; the system replaced hundreds of one-off layouts with a kit rangers could fill in. The 1972 subway diagram applied the London Underground logic (45 and 90 degree lines, one dot per station) and was withdrawn in 1979 because riders wanted geography; the lesson Vignelli drew in later interviews is that a diagram has to match the mental model of its user, which is the pragmatic test from his own Canon failing in public.

- Steal: the three-level check (semantics, syntactics, pragmatics: meaning, structure, use) run in that order on every deliverable. Lella's role, documented in "Design: Vignelli" (1981) and in her own interviews, was the business and the product side of the firm; the method is a partnership method.
- Trap: six typefaces for a lifetime is a position that produced identical-looking work for very different clients; the house uses the discipline and keeps the face specific to the brand (see design-taste, references/type.md).

### 2.3 Josef Müller-Brockmann (1914 to 1996)

Background: Zurich; concert posters for the Tonhalle from 1950; "The Graphic Artist and his Design Problems" (1961); "Grid Systems in Graphic Design" (1981), the manual that codified the Swiss grid for an English-language audience.

Method, from "Grid Systems":

1. Define the page by its margins first, then divide the live area into fields separated by a fixed gutter; 8, 20 and 32 field grids are worked through in the book.
2. Set type on a baseline grid tied to the field heights so text and image align by arithmetic.
3. Use one sans serif (Akzidenz-Grotesk, later Helvetica), two or three sizes, flush left.
4. Image and type are placed by the grid; the designer's taste is spent on choosing the grid, after which the layout is a consequence.

The Tonhalle posters (1950s to 1970s) apply geometry (arcs, repeated bars) to musical structure; the "Beethoven" poster (1955) is a set of arcs stepping by a constant ratio. The reasoning, stated in the 1961 book: objective design communicates to the largest audience with the least noise.

- Steal: decide the grid as a design decision with reasons, then let it rule; the hours go into the grid and the system, and the deliverables become cheap. This is the right model for a hotel group's collateral where 200 items must agree.
- Trap: the grid as alibi. Müller-Brockmann's posters are tense because the content fights the grid at one point; a grid obeyed everywhere produces the corporate deck.

### 2.4 Wim Crouwel (1928 to 2019)

Background: Groningen; Total Design (co-founded 1963, Amsterdam, with Benno Wissing, Friso Kramer and the Schwarz brothers); the Stedelijk Museum Amsterdam catalogues and posters (1963 to 1985); New Alphabet (1967); the nickname "Mr Gridnik" and the typeface Gridnik (1974).

Method, documented in his lectures and in the 2011 Design Museum retrospective catalogue "Wim Crouwel: A Graphic Odyssey":

1. The grid is the design. Crouwel designed the Stedelijk programme as one system: a square grid, a fixed type position, the artwork cropped into the remaining field; the designer's intervention per poster is which image and which colour.
2. Letters follow the technology. New Alphabet (1967) was a response to the coarse cathode-ray-tube typesetters of the day: a letter made only of horizontal and vertical strokes on a grid, with the reader expected to learn it. Crouwel himself called it a proposal, an experiment for the screens of the day.
3. Neutrality as a value: the designer serves the content and leaves no signature. His famous 1972 debate with Jan van Toorn (published as "The Debate", 2015) set this position against van Toorn's argument that design is always political.

- Steal: a museum or gallery identity as a kit where the content changes and the frame holds, which is what a Mumbai gallery or a Dubai art fair needs for 40 exhibitions a year.
- Trap: New Alphabet as decoration. It appears on Joy Division's "Substance" (1988) because Saville understood it as a historical object; used as a display face in 2026 it reads as a tech-conference cliché.

### 2.5 Otl Aicher (1922 to 1991)

Background: Ulm; co-founder of the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm (1953, with Inge Aicher-Scholl and Max Bill); Lufthansa identity (1962, with the HfG Ulm development group E5); the Munich 1972 Olympic Games design programme; ERCO, Bulthaup, FSB; the typeface Rotis (1988); "The World as Design" (1991, essays).

Method, documented in the Munich 1972 design manual and in "The World as Design":

1. Start from a position about the client's meaning. For Munich the position was "the cheerful games": a palette of light blue, green, silver and orange with no red or black, chosen to put distance between 1972 and the 1936 Berlin games.
2. Build every element from a geometric grammar. The pictograms (with Gerhard Joksch and others) are drawn on a grid of horizontals, verticals and 45-degree diagonals; the figure is built from the same strokes whether it swims or fences, so a visitor learns the system once.
3. Specify the system at the level of the humblest object: tickets, uniforms, signage, the poster series, the mascot Waldi, all from one manual.
4. The Rotis argument (1988): Aicher held that legibility is served by a face between serif and sans, and designed Rotis as a family spanning both; the typographic community largely rejected the thesis (Erik Spiekermann has been a vocal critic). The lesson is that a strong position can be wrong and still be worth publishing with its reasons.

- Steal: the pictogram grammar. An events client in Riyadh or Abu Dhabi with a multilingual audience needs a sign system built from a small stroke set, drawn once, then recombined; Arabic and Latin labels then sit beside symbols that do not depend on either script (see 03-typography.md, section 15, for bilingual setting).
- Trap: Aicher-style pictograms without the grid: the figures drift, and the system reads as clip art.

### 2.6 Dieter Rams (born 1932)

Background: Braun (1955 to 1995, head of design from 1961); Vitsoe (from 1959, the 606 Universal Shelving System 1960); the SK4 record player (1956, with Hans Gugelot), the T3 pocket radio (1958), the ET66 calculator (1987, with Dietrich Lubs). The ten principles date from the late 1970s (Vitsoe's own page places them there without a precise year). Gary Hustwit's film "Rams" (2018) is the near-primary source for the method as practised.

The ten principles, exact names as Vitsoe publishes them: good design is innovative; makes a product useful; is aesthetic; makes a product understandable; is unobtrusive; is honest; is long-lasting; is thorough down to the last detail; is environmentally friendly; is as little design as possible.

Method, from Hustwit's film, from Rams's 1976 speech "Design by Vitsoe" and from Rams's own book "Less but Better" (1995):

1. Begin from the function and the manufacturing process; the designer sits with the engineers (Rams's Braun studio was inside the factory).
2. Remove every element that does not serve use or understanding; what remains is arranged so the product explains itself (the T3 radio's dial and the SK4's clear acrylic lid are both explanations).
3. Prototype in the material, at scale, repeatedly; Rams's drawings are few and his models many.
4. Design for the long life of the object, which means parts that can be replaced (606 shelving has kept its 1960 pin and track so a 1960 shelf fits a 2026 wall).

- Steal: the sixth principle, honesty. A product or page that does not promise more than it delivers is the fastest route to the premium register, because the luxury buyer is the most sensitive to overclaim.
- Trap: Rams as a surface (white, grey, one orange dot). Rams's principles say nothing about colour; a Rams-styled product that hides its function fails the fourth principle while quoting the look.

### 2.7 Saul Bass (1920 to 1996)

Background: New York, then Los Angeles; film titles from "Carmen Jones" (1954) and "The Man with the Golden Arm" (1955); "Vertigo" (1958), "Anatomy of a Murder" (1959), "Psycho" (1960), then Scorsese's "Goodfellas" (1990) and "Casino" (1995); corporate marks for Bell System (1969), AT&T (1983), United Airlines (1974), Continental, Minolta, Kleenex, Quaker Oats. The near-primary source is Jennifer Bass and Pat Kirkham, "Saul Bass: A Life in Film and Design" (2011).

Method, from the Bass and Kirkham book and from Bass's own lectures:

1. Find the single symbol that carries the film or the company: the jagged arm for "Golden Arm", the cut-paper corpse for "Anatomy of a Murder", the bell for Bell System. One symbol, then variations.
2. For titles, treat the sequence as a prologue that sets the emotional key, so the first scene starts with the audience already in the film's mood; the "Vertigo" titles (1958, with John Whitney's mechanically generated spiral animations) and the "Psycho" titles (1960, grey bars slicing the type) are the models.
3. Reduce the mark until a child can draw it, then test it at the scale of a building and of a matchbook (the Bell System presentation books show both).

- Steal: the single symbol method for a campaign: one device (a shape, a cut, a colour) that is the campaign's whole idea, varied across every format.
- Trap: the Bass pastiche (cut-paper figures, Vertigo spirals) used as retro flavour. The method is the discipline of one symbol, and the style was its by-product.

### 2.8 Milton Glaser (1929 to 2020)

Background: Push Pin Studios (1954, with Seymour Chwast, Reynold Ruffins and Edward Sorel); the Bob Dylan poster (1966); New York magazine (1968, with Clay Felker); I Love NY (1977, drawn on a napkin in a taxi, done for the State of New York for no fee); Brooklyn Brewery (1988). "Drawing is Thinking" (2008) is the primary source for the method; the AIGA London talk "Ten Things I Have Learned" (2001) is the principle set.

Method: drawing as the instrument of thought. Glaser argued that drawing slows perception enough to see; a mark on paper is a question asked of the subject, and the answer is the next mark. His process for a mark was a page of thirty rough drawings before any one was considered.

Principle set (the 2001 talk, given on 11 November 2001 and published as an essay on miltonglaser.com; headings paraphrased): you can only work for people you like; if you have a choice never have a job; some people are toxic, avoid them; professionalism is not enough (also titled "the good is the enemy of the great"); less is not necessarily more; style is not to be trusted; how you live changes your brain; doubt is better than certainty; "on aging"; tell the truth.

- Steal: the thirty-rough rule. Thirty drawings on paper before the first screen; the house applies it to every mark and every campaign device.
- Trap: Glaser's warmth (the Dylan hair, the chunky serif) as nostalgia; the drawing method is medium-independent and the look is 1966.

### 2.9 Paula Scher (born 1948)

Background: CBS Records (1970s), Koppel & Scher (1984), Pentagram partner since 1991; the Public Theater (1994 onward, the wood-type identity that reset American cultural branding); Citibank (1998, the umbrella arc first sketched on a napkin in a meeting); Microsoft Windows 8 logo (2012); MoMA, the New York City Ballet, Tiffany & Co. She paints enormous hand-lettered maps (exhibited since the late 1990s; "Paula Scher: MAPS", 2011).

Method, documented in "Make It Bigger" (2002) and in the Netflix "Abstract" episode (2017):

1. Find the vernacular that belongs to the client's world and amplify it. The Public Theater's wood-type and street-poster grammar came from the theatre's own history and from New York street typography; the identity works because it was already there.
2. Serious play: Scher's 2008 TED talk argues that the best work comes from a playful state pursued with full seriousness; the Citibank arc took seconds to draw because it took decades to learn to draw it in seconds (her own account).
3. Scale is content. Her title "Make It Bigger" is the method: the client asks for a bigger logo; the designer answers with a bigger idea, which is environmental graphics at the scale of a building (New Jersey Performing Arts Center, Queens Metropolitan Campus).
4. The maps: painting as a way to keep the hand and the eye ahead of the screen.

- Steal: the vernacular amplification method for an Indian hospitality client: the hotel's own signage history, the city's lettering (Mumbai's Irani cafés, Jaipur's shop boards) as the raw material, drawn with authority and without irony.
- Trap: wood type and tight stacked caps as "theatre"; the Public Theater identity was specific to one institution and has been copied into meaninglessness.

### 2.10 Michael Bierut (born 1957)

Background: Vignelli Associates (1980 to 1990), Pentagram partner 1990 to 2024 and in an advisory role from October 2024 (pentagram.com); Design Observer co-founder (2003); notebooks: he has kept numbered composition books since 1982, more than a hundred, and shows them in talks as the record of every project. Works: Saks Fifth Avenue (2007), the MIT Media Lab system (2014, with Aron Fay), Hillary Clinton's "H" (2015), Mastercard (2016, with the symbol-only form in 2019), Verizon (2015), Slack (2019), the Yale School of Architecture posters.

Method, documented in "Seventy-nine Short Essays on Design" (2007), "How to use graphic design to sell things, explain things, make things look better, make people laugh, make people cry, and (every once in a while) change the world" (2015) and the Design Matters interviews:

1. Write the brief as a story about the client before drawing; most of his essays are the narrative that preceded the mark.
2. Build the system from one rule. MIT Media Lab (2014): a 7 by 7 grid; every lab group's logo is drawn from the same grid so 23 groups look like one institution without a parent logo imposed on them. Saks (2007): the 1973 script logo cut into 64 squares that can be recombined, so every bag is different and every bag is Saks.
3. Keep the equity. Mastercard (2016): the two circles stayed; the word moved out of the circles and then (2019) off the mark entirely, because 80 percent recognition without the name (Mastercard's own research, as reported by Pentagram) made the word redundant.
4. Present with the history in the room: the Mastercard and MIT presentations began with the old marks and the archive.

- Steal: one generative rule that produces infinite correct variants (Saks, MIT) is the modern identity's core; it is how a hotel group with 12 properties gets 12 identities and one brand.
- Trap: the "clever system" that only designers see. Bierut's systems work because the audience sees the sameness without being told the rule.

### 2.11 Peter Saville (born 1955)

Background: Manchester; co-founder of Factory Records (1978, with Tony Wilson and Alan Erasmus; the FAC catalogue numbers include the posters and the club); Joy Division's "Unknown Pleasures" (1979), "Blue Monday" (1983, the die-cut floppy-disk sleeve), New Order's "Power, Corruption and Lies" (1983, Fantin-Latour's "A Basket of Roses", 1890, with a colour-code alphabet standing for the type); later Calvin Klein (2017, with Raf Simons), Burberry (2018), the City of Manchester's creative director (2004). Emily King's "Designed by Peter Saville" (2003) is the near-primary account.

Method, reconstructed from the work and from Saville's many interviews:

1. The found image. "Unknown Pleasures" is a plot of pulsar CP 1919 from the Cambridge Encyclopaedia of Astronomy (1977), brought in by Bernard Sumner; Saville reversed it to white on black and removed every word. The method is appropriation with a change of context, close to Duchamp and to Abloh's later readymades (section 7.2).
2. The refusal. No band name, no title, no type where the client would expect it; the sleeve trusts the buyer to know. This is the confidence design-taste calls "the sleeve with no name on it"; the mechanism is that withholding information signals that the audience is an insider.
3. Historical reference as system: the Fantin-Latour sleeve's colour code applied across New Order's releases, so the system, once learned, identified the label.

- Steal: the found image with the context changed, and the discipline to remove the caption.
- Trap: Saville's own admission that the Factory method was only possible because Factory was not run as a business; "Blue Monday" reportedly lost money per copy because of the die-cut. A luxury client can afford one refusal per campaign, chosen with care.

### 2.12 David Carson (born 1955)

Background: former professional surfer and sociology teacher; Transworld Skateboarding, Beach Culture (1989 to 1991), Surfer, Ray Gun (1992 to 1995); "The End of Print" (1995, with Lewis Blackwell). The Bryan Ferry interview set entirely in Zapf Dingbats (Ray Gun, 1994) because Carson found the interview dull is the emblematic act.

Method, stated in "The End of Print" and in his lectures: read the content, respond to it intuitively, let the type carry the emotional reading; the grid is used only where it helps the feeling. Carson's own position is that legibility and communication are different things and a reader who has to work remembers more.

- Steal: the idea that typography can carry the emotional reading of a text, which the house applies in a controlled dose (one broken line in a campaign, one overlapping headline).
- Trap: every Carson layout is a trained eye reacting at speed; the untrained imitator produces noise. Carson is in this file as the boundary of the method.

### 2.13 Stefan Sagmeister and Jessica Walsh

Background: Sagmeister Inc. (New York, 1993); the Lou Reed "Set the Twilight Reeling" cover (1996, lyrics written on Reed's face); the AIGA Detroit poster (1999, the text cut into Sagmeister's skin by Martin Woodtli); the sabbatical (one year off every seven, first taken in 2001, explained in the 2009 TED talk "The power of time off"); "Things I Have Learned in My Life So Far" (2008, twenty maxims from his diary built as public installations); "The Happy Film" (2016); "Beauty" (2018, book with Walsh and the MAK Vienna exhibition). Walsh joined in 2010, became partner in 2012 (Sagmeister & Walsh, 2012 to 2019), and founded &Walsh in 2019; "40 Days of Dating" (2013, with Timothy Goodman) and "12 Kinds of Kindness" (2016) are her method in public.

Method, documented in the books and talks:

1. Start from a sentence that is true for the designer personally (a maxim, a diary line), then find the physical form that makes a stranger feel it (sausages, coins, inflatable monkeys).
2. Make the making visible: the hand, the body, the labour are the content.
3. The sabbatical as research and development: Sagmeister's position is that the ideas from the year off fed the following seven years' work (the Bali year produced "The Happy Film" and much of the "Things I Have Learned" series).
4. "Beauty" (2018): the argument, with evidence from psychology and from public space (the exhibition at the MAK Vienna, 2018, and its later venues), that beauty is a functional property and the modernist suspicion of it is a mistake.

Walsh's addition: the project as a social experiment with a publishing plan built in from day one; "40 Days of Dating" was designed as a daily website, then a book, then film rights, before the first date.

- Steal: the personal sentence as the starting point; a campaign line the creative director would say about their own life carries a personal weight that an audience hears at once.
- Trap: shock as content. The skin poster worked once, in 1999, for an audience of designers.

### 2.14 Kenya Hara (born 1958)

Background: Nippon Design Center (president); art director of Muji from 2001; the Nagano 1998 Olympic programmes; "Re-Design" (2000), "Haptic" (2004) and "Senseware" (2007) exhibitions; "Designing Design" (2007), "White" (2010), "Ex-formation" (2015), all Lars Müller.

Method, documented in "Designing Design" and "Ex-formation":

1. Emptiness as a container (emptiness: Hara's term for a form left open to receive the viewer's meaning). Hara's distinction: simplicity (Western modernism) removes until the object is rational; emptiness (his reading of Japanese aesthetics) removes until the object can receive the viewer's meaning. Muji's 2003 "Horizon" campaign (the Uyuni salt flat in Bolivia, a single figure on an empty plane, photographed by Tamotsu Fujii) is the demonstration: an image with nothing in it and the brand's whole philosophy in it.
2. Ex-formation: his Musashino Art University seminar method, in which students take a known subject (a river, Shikoku, nudity) and make it unknown again, so the viewer sees it fresh; the opposite of information.
3. White as a condition: the capacity to be marked.
4. Haptic design (the 2004 exhibition's term for design that starts from touch): begin from how a thing feels in the hand, then let the look follow.

- Steal: the ex-formation exercise as a campaign method: take the category's most familiar object (the hotel pool, the diamond, the villa) and make it strange, which is design-taste's "tension" with a procedure attached.
- Trap: Muji-white as a palette. Emptiness is a philosophy of what the viewer brings; white cardboard is a material.

### 2.15 Irma Boom (born 1960)

Background: Amsterdam; the SHV Think Book 1996 to 1896 (2,136 pages, five years' work from 1991, read backwards through a century, no page numbers); the Chanel No 5 book (2013, 300 pages embossed without ink); the small-format monographs "Irma Boom: Biography in Books" (2010) and "Irma Boom: The Architecture of the Book" (2013); the Rijksmuseum identity (2013); the Gutenberg Prize (2001).

Method, documented in her lectures and the 2013 book:

1. Research first and at length; the SHV book came from five years in the company archive, and the content decided the form.
2. Make a tiny paper model of the book before any page is designed: the format, the thickness, the way it opens, the edge; Boom works in paper and at small scale before any computer is involved, and has said the small model decides the book.
3. Treat the book as an object with properties (weight, smell, edge colour, the sound of the page) that a screen cannot have; the Chanel book's unprinted embossing is the argument that the medium's property is the content.

- Steal: the paper model before the screen, for any physical deliverable (a hotel's in-room book, a jewellery house's catalogue, an invitation): decide the object in the hand first.
- Trap: thickness and no page numbers as a signifier of ambition; Boom's eccentricities each follow from the content of that book.

### 2.16 Experimental Jetset

Background: Amsterdam, founded 1997 by Marieke Stolk, Erwin Brinkers and Danny van den Dungen; the "John & Paul & Ringo & George" T-shirt (2001); the Stedelijk Museum CS identity (2004); the Whitney Museum identity (2013, the responsive W that stretches to fit the content); the 2007 "Helvetica" film; "Statement and Counter-Statement" (2015) is the primary text.

Method, documented in the 2015 book and their long written interviews: turn language into objects. A conceptual rule generates the system (the Whitney W is a zigzag whose angles change with the format), typeset in Helvetica because the typeface's neutrality keeps attention on the rule. They describe their work as a form of printed matter that refers to its own making (the printing, the folding, the grid).

- Steal: the identity whose logo is a rule; good for a cultural institution and for a media brand that lives in many formats.
- Trap: Helvetica-and-a-rule as a look; without a rule worth stating, it is a stationery set.

### 2.17 Mirko Borsche

Background: Munich; Bureau Borsche (2007); creative director of Zeit Magazin since its relaunch in the late 2000s; the Bayerische Staatsoper; the Balenciaga wordmark and identity under Demna (2017); work for Nike and Audi.

Method, reconstructed from the work and from Borsche's published interviews (Eye, It's Nice That): the editorial grid as brand. A strong grid with large type and generous white, then a weekly act of disobedience: one spread where the image or the headline breaks the system. The Zeit Magazin covers are a format (the masthead, the one image or one typographic statement, the line) that changes radically within fixed rules; the Balenciaga wordmark (a wide, plain sans in the manner of institutional signage) is the fashion application of the same neutrality-with-attitude.

- Steal: the weekly disobedience. In a content calendar (see 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 6.12, for the India and Gulf calendar), plan the one post per week that breaks the system on purpose.
- Trap: wide neutral sans as "fashion" in 2026 is the default; Borsche's reason was the contrast with Balenciaga's baroque past.

### 2.18 Brian Collins and COLLINS

Background: Ogilvy's Brand Integration Group (1998 to 2007), then COLLINS (New York, 2008, with Leland Maschmeyer); Spotify (2015, the duotone system, with Spotify's in-house team), Dropbox (2017), Mailchimp (2018), the San Francisco Symphony (2019), Twitch (2019).

Method, documented in COLLINS case studies and Collins's talks (99U, Design Matters): the studio sells "the future" as the deliverable. A project begins with a written and drawn picture of the client's world several years out (the brand's products, places and press as they would exist), then the identity is designed as the thing that world would have; the present identity is derived backwards from the future one. Collins's stated reason: a brand that only describes the present is already dated on launch.

- Steal: the future-first brief. For a real-estate developer in Dubai or a hospitality group in Goa, write the 2030 press release and design for it.
- Trap: futurism as slide-deck theatre; the method only works if the future is specific (products, prices, places), which is why COLLINS pairs it with intensive research.

### 2.19 Natasha Jen

Background: Taiwan-born; Pentagram partner since 2012; identities, environments and exhibitions for cultural and commercial clients; the 99U talk "Design Thinking is Bullshit" (2017).

Method and position: her 2017 critique is itself the method note. Jen's argument: the five-hexagon design-thinking diagram and its Post-it workshops had replaced craft with process theatre, and the test of any process should be "crit", the critique of the actual artefact by people who can make artefacts. She proposes that evidence of design ability is the work, judged in the open.

- Steal: the crit as the governance of a project (see 00-curriculum-and-method.md, sections 2.17 and 6.11, for the house critique format). Replace the client workshop that produces 400 Post-its with a wall of 20 finished artefacts and an argument.
- Trap: dismissing research. Jen's target was process without craft; research with craft (section 4.6) is the house position.

### 2.20 Erik Spiekermann (born 1947)

Background: Berlin; MetaDesign (1979, refounded 1990), FontShop (1989, with Joan Spiekermann and Neville Brody), FontFont; FF Meta (1991), the Berlin transit (BVG) system, Deutsche Bahn's DB Type (2005, with Christian Schwartz), Audi, Bosch; Edenspiekermann (2009); the letterpress workshop p98a in Berlin (2014) and the "post-digital printing" experiments; "Stop Stealing Sheep and Find Out How Type Works" (1993, with E. M. Ginger).

Method, documented in "Stop Stealing Sheep" and in "Hello, I am Erik" (2014): typography is the voice of the organisation; choose or draw a face for the organisation's tone (FF Meta began as a face for the Deutsche Bundespost that was rejected and then became the humanist sans of the 1990s); set the whole system (sizes, spaces, signage, forms) as one voice. The letterpress return: Spiekermann's position since 2014 is that printing from digital plates on letterpress produces an artefact with physical authority that offset cannot, and his p98a workshop prints with laser-cut and photopolymer plates.

- Steal: the voice brief for type: write the organisation's tone in three adjectives and choose the face against those words, with the signage and the forms in the same brief.
- Trap: humanist sans as "friendly" by default.

### 2.21 Neville Brody (born 1957)

Background: The Face (1981 to 1986), Arena; "The Graphic Language of Neville Brody" (1988); Fuse (1991, with Jon Wozencroft, the experimental type magazine issued with fonts on disk, 20 issues from 1991 to 2000); Research Studios (1994), Brody Associates; dean of the Royal College of Art School of Communication (2011 to 2018).

Method, documented in the 1988 and 1994 monographs: type as image and as a political signal; The Face's method was a new typographic device per issue (a stencil, a constructed alphabet, a rotated headline) so the magazine looked like the culture it reported. Fuse formalised this: each issue commissioned four designers to make a typeface and a poster from one theme, so the face was the argument.

- Steal: the issue-by-issue device; in a campaign calendar, each release gets one new typographic invention inside the identity's constants.
- Trap: 1980s constructed alphabets as nostalgia.

### 2.22 Tibor Kalman (1949 to 1999)

Background: Budapest-born; M&Co (1979, New York); Talking Heads covers ("Remain in Light", 1980, with the band); the Restaurant Florent identity; Colors magazine for Benetton (editor-in-chief 1991 to 1995, the "What if" race-swapped portraits, the issue with no text); "Tibor Kalman: Perverse Optimist" (1998) is the primary record.

Method and moral position, from "Perverse Optimist" and his essays: the designer's duty is to tell the truth and to refuse work that lies; the vernacular (shop signs, cheap printing) is more honest than the professional; design should make people uncomfortable enough to think. Colors was the method at magazine scale: one subject per issue, photography and data as the argument, the layout plain enough that the content had to carry it.

- Steal: the vernacular as a register (see 2.9) and the refusal as a studio policy, written down.
- Trap: provocation without a point; Kalman's shocks were arguments.

### 2.23 Alan Fletcher (1931 to 2006)

Background: Fletcher/Forbes/Gill (1962), Pentagram co-founder (1972); the Reuters logo (1965, built from punched-tape dots), the V&A logo (1989, the ampersand completing the A); "The Art of Looking Sideways" (2001, more than a thousand pages of visual thinking, the primary source), "Beware Wet Paint" (1996).

Method: Fletcher's book is itself the method, a commonplace book of quotations, images and puzzles arranged to train lateral seeing. The work shows the procedure: find the visual pun that is already in the material (Reuters was transmitted on punched tape; the V&A's A could lose a stroke and borrow it from the ampersand) and let it be the whole mark.

- Steal: keep the sideways book (the house's equivalent is the reference board in design-taste, section 4) and look for the pun that is already in the client's material.
- Trap: the visual pun for its own sake, which reads as a greetings card.

### 2.24 Ken Garland (1929 to 2021)

Background: London; Galt Toys; "First Things First" manifesto (1964), signed by 22 designers and photographers, republished as "First Things First 2000" in Adbusters, Emigre and Eye with 33 new signatories.

Method and position: the manifesto argued that the profession's skills were being spent on trivial consumer persuasion and should be redirected towards education, public information and culture. The 2000 version extended it to branding. Garland's own practice (Galt Toys, Connexions) was the demonstration: useful things, plainly designed.

- Steal: the written statement of what the studio will not do, which is the "what we are not doing" step in design-taste's process with a moral floor beneath it.
- Trap: signing the manifesto and taking the work anyway, which is what the 1964 and 2000 signatories were accused of.

### 2.25 Indian masters

- Sudarshan Dhir and Vikas Satwalekar (NID Ahmedabad): among the teachers who joined Swiss and Ulm method to Indian subjects; their individual identity credits are poorly documented, so no mark is attributed to either here (01-history-and-movements.md, section 10.2).
- D. Udaya Kumar's rupee sign (chosen on 15 July 2010 from a public competition): the Devanagari "ra" and the Latin "R" joined under a top bar that reads as the shirorekha (the Devanagari headline), the model of a bi-script mark that reads in both scripts at once (01-history-and-movements.md, section 10.2; 03-typography.md, section 8.5).
- Charles and Ray Eames, "The India Report" (April 1958): the founding document of NID and a principle set derived from observing Indian making (dissected in section 11.2).
- R. K. Joshi (1936 to 2008; Devanagari calligraphy and type research at the Industrial Design Centre, IIT Bombay) and Fiona Ross (Linotype's Indic and non-Latin type programme, later the University of Reading): the lineage behind the Indic process in section 3.8.
- Ishan Khosla (Delhi): the Typecraft Initiative, in which tribal and folk artists draw typefaces from their own craft vocabulary with a type designer's structure; the method, from the initiative's project pages, is co-design where the craft holds the authorship.
- Sabyasachi Mukherjee as a visual director: see section 7.9; the method is world building through objects and photography.
- Ek Type (Mumbai, a collaborative foundry whose founding group includes Sarang Kulkarni and Noopur Datye): multi-script families designed in parallel so Devanagari, Gujarati, Tamil and Latin share a rhythm (Mukta, 2015; Baloo, 2016; both on Google Fonts). ITF's launch post gives the reason as access for brands and individuals without a type budget; the strategic reading, reconstructed, is licence revenue traded for mindshare among the designers who will later buy the foundry's custom and retail work.
- Steal: the bi-script mark (the rupee sign) and the one-skeleton, many-scripts family (Ek Type) as the two models for any India or Gulf identity that must read in Devanagari or Arabic and in Latin.
- Trap: craft motifs (a paisley, a lotus) dropped onto an international grid, which is decoration; the rupee sign works because both scripts carry the structure.

## 3. Type designers and their process

### 3.1 Matthew Carter (born 1937)

Background: Bell Centennial (1975 to 1978, for AT&T directories), Galliard (1978), Bitstream co-founder (1981), Charter (1987), Carter & Cone (1992), Georgia and Verdana (1996, for Microsoft, hinted by Tom Rickner; hinting: instructions in the font that snap outlines to the pixel grid at small sizes), Tahoma (1994), Miller (1997), the Yale typeface (2004); MacArthur Fellow (2010).

Method, documented in his lectures (TED 2014, "My life in typefaces"), in the Bell Centennial papers and in Carter's many interviews: draw for the medium. Bell Centennial was designed for 6 point on newsprint at high press speed; the ink traps (notches cut into the junctions of strokes, so that ink spread on cheap paper fills them to a clean corner) are the medium's physics drawn into the letter, and the family's four weights (Name & Number, Address, Sub-Caption, Bold Listing) match the four lines of a directory entry. Verdana was drawn bitmap-first: Carter designed the pixel patterns at 8 to 12 pixels per em (the number of device pixels across the font's em square) and then drew outlines that would hint to those patterns, reversing the usual order. Charter (1987) was built with few curve points because the 300 dpi printers of the day could not store many.

- Steal: begin from the worst rendering condition the face will live in (an Instagram caption at 11 px, a dirham price on a receipt printer) and design to it.
- Trap: Georgia and Verdana as "web safe" in 2026; they were drawn for 96 ppi CRTs and look heavy on retina screens.

### 3.2 Tobias Frere-Jones and Jonathan Hoefler

Background: Hoefler Type Foundry (1989), Hoefler & Frere-Jones (1999 to 2014); Gotham (2000, Frere-Jones, for GQ, from the lettering of the Port Authority Bus Terminal and New York's mid-century signage; the Obama 2008 campaign face), Whitney, Mercury, Sentinel, Archer; the split: Frere-Jones sued in January 2014 claiming a 50/50 partnership agreed verbally in 1999 and seeking 20 million dollars; the case settled out of court on 28 September 2014 with undisclosed terms; Frere-Jones Type's first family, Mallory, followed in 2015; Hoefler sold Hoefler & Co. to Monotype in 2021.

Method, documented in Hoefler's typography.com essays and Frere-Jones's "Typeface Mechanics" series (frerejones.com, 2015 onward):

1. Research: photograph the historical sources (Frere-Jones walked Manhattan photographing lettering for two years before Gotham).
2. Draw the control characters first: H, O, n, o and a few more establish the stem, the round, the x-height and the proportions; every other glyph is derived from them.
3. Space before you draw more: sidebearings (the space built into each side of a glyph) are set on the control characters so the rhythm is tested in words at the earliest stage.
4. Test at size: the ScreenSmart (2013) and the optical-size masters (versions of a face drawn for a specific size range) came from printing and screening every face at the sizes it would be used, then correcting weight, spacing and detail per size.
5. "Typeface Mechanics" documents the optical corrections (overshoot, rounds drawn slightly past the baseline and x-height so they look as tall as flat letters; the thinning of horizontals, the narrowing of bold rounds) as rules with their reasons.

- Steal: control characters and spacing first, for any lettering or logotype: draw H, O, n, o in the proposed style and set them in a word before drawing the rest.
- Trap: Gotham as "trustworthy" because of 2008; it is the most imitated face of its decade.

### 3.3 Kris Sowersby and Klim

Background: Wellington, New Zealand; Klim Type Foundry (2005); National (2007), Founders Grotesk (2010), Tiempos (2010 onward), Untitled Sans and Serif (2017, from the Super Normal idea of Naoto Fukasawa and Jasper Morrison), Söhne (2019), Signifier (2020, from the Fell types), Epicene (2021).

Method, documented in the long design-information essays Klim publishes for each face. The Söhne essay (26 November 2019) is the model: Sowersby describes the face as the memory of Akzidenz-Grotesk framed through the reality of Helvetica; his source was the 1970 New York subway signage by Unimark (Vignelli and Noorda), which was set in Standard Medium, the American name for Akzidenz-Grotesk, and only officially replaced by Helvetica in 1989, so his own memory of "subway Helvetica" was wrong and the essay says so. He designed Söhne from the Halbfett (semibold) weight outward, taking family proportions from 36 pt Akzidenz-Grotesk Halbfett, against his usual practice of starting from the regular. Analogous sources (Blockschrift, Breede Halfvette Antieke for the alternate g; Enge Steinschrift for Söhne Schmal) are named per glyph. The method in general: a historical model studied as an artefact (printed specimens, signage, the physical conditions), a stated aim that is never a facsimile, a decision about which weight is the master, then the essay as the record so the buyer knows what they are buying.

- Steal: the published reasoning. Mask360's case studies should name the sources of every typographic decision the way a Klim essay does; the client who reads the reasoning stops asking for alternatives.
- Trap: Söhne set by reflex for every luxury brief; design-taste, references/type.md, recommends it for its idea (Akzidenz reconsidered for screens), so choose it only when that idea is the brand's.

### 3.4 Dinamo

Background: founded 2013 by Johannes Breyer and Fabian Harb (Basel and Berlin); ABC Favorit, ABC Diatype, ABC Monument Grotesk, ABC Arizona (a variable face that moves between serif and sans), ABC Walter; Font Gauntlet (a browser tool for testing variable fonts, 2019); the Darkroom and the many co-credited designers.

Method, documented in Dinamo's own project pages and in interviews: a collaborative foundry in which each face carries several named designers and a stated concept (Diatype's origin in the Diatype phototypesetter of the 1960s; Arizona's axis between the two historical categories), with variable-font (one font file holding a continuous design space along axes such as weight or width) experiments treated as the design problem itself. The foundry also builds custom faces for brands and publishes the custom work as case studies.

- Steal: the variable axis as the brand idea (one face that moves from display to text, or from editorial to signage).
- Trap: variable axes used as animation gimmicks in web headers.

### 3.5 Christian Schwartz and Paul Barnes, Commercial Type

Background: Commercial Type (2007, New York and London); Guardian Egyptian (2005, for the Guardian's Berliner-format redesign under creative director Mark Porter), Publico (2007), Graphik (2009), Druk (2014, Berton Hasebe), Canela (2016, Miguel Reyes), Marr Sans, Caslon Ionic.

Method, documented in Commercial Type's project notes and in Porter's own account of the Guardian redesign: a newspaper typeface is a system drawn against the paper's own grid and ink. Guardian Egyptian was drawn in text, headline and agate (the smallest newspaper size, about 5.5 point, for listings and tables) cuts, with the slab serifs sized to survive newsprint and the weights spaced to carry the Berliner page's many sizes; the sans companion came from the same skeleton so captions and headlines agreed.

- Steal: the companion drawn from the same skeleton for a bilingual or multi-register brand (a serif for the hotel's editorial voice, its sans for wayfinding, one skeleton).
- Trap: Graphik and Canela as the 2016 to 2022 luxury pairing, now everywhere.

### 3.6 Erik van Blokland and Just van Rossum, LettError

Background: LettError (1989, The Hague); Beowolf (1990, the first "random" font, whose outlines shifted at each print); FF Trixie, FF Kosmik; RoboFog (Fontographer with Python inside), RoboFab (2003, with Tal Leming), the UFO font source format (2004, with Leming); Superpolator (van Blokland), DrawBot (van Rossum, with Frederik Berlaen); RoboFont (2011) was built by their student Frederik Berlaen on their tool chain; both teach on the Type and Media master at KABK, The Hague. Just is the brother of Guido van Rossum, who created Python.

Method, documented in their talks and in the RoboFab and UFO documentation: treat the typeface as data and the design process as code. Beowolf's argument was that a digital letter has no fixed form; RoboFab's argument was that a designer who can script can test a thousand spacing variants in an afternoon. TypeCooker (van Blokland's teaching tool) generates random constraint sets for sketching, the type-design equivalent of design-taste's "tension".

- Steal: scripted variation for any system (icon sets, pattern libraries, testing Devanagari conjuncts, consonant clusters fused into one glyph): write the rule as code, generate every case, look at all of them.
- Trap: mistaking tool mastery for design; LettError's tools serve an eye trained by hand.

### 3.7 Adrian Frutiger (1928 to 2015) and Hermann Zapf (1918 to 2015)

Frutiger: Univers (1957, Deberny & Peignot), the first family planned as a system: 21 variants on a numbered grid (the tens digit for weight, the units for width and slope; 55 is the roman), so that a designer specifies a cut by number; Frutiger (1976, from the Charles de Gaulle airport signage designed 1970 to 1975 for Roissy), OCR-B (1968), Avenir (1988); "Signs and Symbols: Their Design and Meaning" (1978). Method, from his own writings: a face for signage is drawn for distance, angle and movement (the Roissy letters were tested on back-lit panels seen while walking), and a family is a matrix whose every weight and width is planned at once.

Zapf: Palatino (1949), Melior (1952), Optima (1958, sketched on a 1,000 lira note from the inscriptions in the floor of Santa Croce, Florence, 1950), Zapf Dingbats (1978), Zapfino (1998); the hz-program (with URW, 1990s), whose paragraph-level optimisation of spacing and glyph widths was licensed to Adobe and became the InDesign paragraph composer. Method: calligraphy as the ground truth (Zapf's "Manuale Typographicum", 1954 and 1968), then the machine taught to respect it.

- Steal: Frutiger's numbered matrix for any system with many variants (a hotel group's sub-brands, a jeweller's collections): name the axes, number the cells, design the matrix.
- Trap: Optima for "luxury" by reflex; it is on the Estée Lauder bottle and on a thousand spa menus.

### 3.8 The Indic type process

Universal Thirst (Gunnar Vilhjálmsson and Kalapi Gadhavi, India and Iceland) and Typotheque (Peter Biľak, The Hague; Fedra Hindi, the Typotheque Indic programme) publish their Indic process in detail, and the problem both describe is vertical metrics. Devanagari has a headline (the shirorekha), ascending matras (vowel signs written above the headline) and stacked conjuncts (consonant clusters fused or stacked into one glyph) below the base, so a Devanagari font needs far more vertical room than a Latin companion at the same point size. The OpenType OS/2 table carries two sets of vertical metrics, the Typo (sTypoAscender and sTypoDescender) and the Win (usWinAscent and usWinDescent); legacy Windows applications used the Win values for line spacing, so Indic fonts that set Win metrics tall enough to avoid clipping got loose Latin line spacing, and fonts that set them tight clipped the stacks. The fsSelection bit 7 flag (USE_TYPO_METRICS) tells applications to use the Typo values, and applications honour it unevenly (Tiro Typeworks's public issue tracker documents fonts where the Typo and Win sums deliberately differ for this reason). The process both foundries describe: design the Devanagari first or at least in parallel with the Latin (Biľak's stated regret about Fedra Hindi is that the Latin came first), set the vertical metrics for the tallest realistic Devanagari stack, then draw the Latin to sit well inside that line height. The lineage behind this practice runs through R. K. Joshi and Fiona Ross (section 2.25). See 03-typography.md, section 15, for the house settings.

- Steal: for every bilingual Mask360 deliverable, choose the Devanagari or Arabic face first and the Latin to match, and test line spacing with the tallest conjuncts in the copy.
- Trap: Latin-first pairing with a Devanagari "companion" picked at the end, which produces either clipped matras or a Latin set 40 percent looser than it should be.

### 3.9 The Arabic type process

The script facts (contextual forms, no case, kashida, harakat, Naskh and Kufi) are in 03-typography.md, section 15.2; this entry is the method. Each letter's contextual form is selected by the OpenType features isol, init, medi and fina (isolated, initial, medial, final), and the lam-alef ligature (the joined form of lam followed by alef) is required; kashida (tatweel, the lengthening of a connecting stroke) justifies the line by stretching joins; harakat (the optional vowel marks above and below the line) bring back the vertical-metrics problem of section 3.8, because a line set with full harakat needs more room than the same line bare.

- Huda Smitshuijzen AbiFarès, Khatt Foundation: the Typographic Matchmaking project (book, 2007) paired Arab and Dutch designers to draw bilingual families in parallel, each pair working on one family from both sides; the method is co-design across scripts, with neither script declared the master.
- Nadine Chahine: Arabic specialist at Linotype and Monotype from 2005 to 2015, where she drew the Arabic companions to Helvetica, Frutiger and Zapfino; her Koufiya was the first face with matching Arabic and Latin designed simultaneously; she led the Monotype team behind Dubai Font (2017, section 2.26).
- Pascal Zoghbi (29LT) and Kristyan Sarkis (TPTQ Arabic, with Peter Biľak): their project notes show the matching process: shared stroke contrast and weight steps, the Arabic tooth height and the Latin x-height tuned against each other, and the Arabic drawn from its own calligraphic logic.
- Steal: set the Arabic first, write the kashida rule (where a join may stretch, and by how much) into the type system, and test every layout with full harakat before sign-off.
- Trap: a Latin-first pairing with an Arabic "companion" chosen at the end, which clips the harakat or forces loose Latin line spacing to make room.

## 4. Product, UI and UX

### 4.1 Jony Ive and the Apple Industrial Design Group

Background: joined Apple 1992, head of industrial design 1997 to 2019; iMac (1998), iPod (2001), iPhone (2007), the unibody MacBook (2008), Apple Watch (2015), Apple Park; LoveFrom (2019, with Marc Newson): the LoveFrom Serif drawn from Baskerville for the Terra Carta seal (2021), Ferrari (from 2021), the King's coronation emblem (2023); io, the hardware company founded with former Apple designers, acquired by OpenAI in a deal announced on 21 May 2025 and closed on 9 July 2025, valued at about 6.5 billion dollars in equity including the 23 percent stake OpenAI already held (TechCrunch and the Altman and Ive joint letter); LoveFrom stayed independent and took on design across OpenAI, software included. A first device was expected in 2026; as of October 2026 none has shipped, and Wired reported in 2026 that OpenAI was dropping the "io" name for its hardware after a trademark suit and that the first device had slipped to 2027. Near-primary sources: Ive's interview in Hustwit's "Objectified" (2009), Leander Kahney's "Jony Ive" (2013), Walter Isaacson's "Steve Jobs" (2011), Tripp Mickle's "After Steve" (2022).

Method, from those sources:

1. Care for the unseen part. In "Objectified" Ive describes the attention given to the inside of a product and to the parts nobody sees, because the user senses the care even when they cannot point to it; Jobs's version is his father's rule about the back of the fence (Isaacson).
2. The studio as a closed room. Isaacson and Kahney describe Jobs visiting the design studio most days; the studio was off limits to most employees; the review was a conversation over models on tables, with the CEO and the designer deciding together, no presentations.
3. Models before drawings. CNC-milled prototypes in the real material at every stage; the design is judged in the hand. The hardware engineering side under Bob Mansfield (to 2012) and Dan Riccio (2012 to 2021) made the models manufacturable at volume; Mickle's "After Steve" documents the tension between the design group's demands and engineering's constraints after 2011.
4. Reduce to the one object: a single piece of aluminium, one button, no visible screws.

- Steal: the model in the hand and the closed review with the decision-maker present; for Mask360, the printed mock-up of the invitation or the packaging on the table with the founder, no slides.
- Trap: aluminium minimalism as a surface; the method's expense is in tooling and in the refusal to ship a compromise, which an identity cannot copy by looking bare.

### 4.2 The Rams to Ive lineage

The Braun T3 pocket radio (1958, Rams) and the first iPod (2001): a white rectangle, a circular control, the proportions and the placement of the wheel; the Braun ET66 calculator (1987, Rams and Lubs) and the iPhone's calculator app (2007): the same button colours and layout. Ive acknowledged the debt in his foreword to Sophie Lovell's "Dieter Rams: As Little Design as Possible" (Phaidon, 2011). The lineage is a method, which is to say that the ten principles (section 11.2) applied to software give the early iOS: the device explains itself, the chrome recedes, the detail is thorough. The lesson for a creative director is that a borrowed principle set can carry across media when the reasons carry too.

- Steal: carry a principle set across media by carrying its reasons: a hotel's print rules become its app rules when each rule's reason still holds on a screen.
- Trap: the Braun look (white plastic, the orange dot) applied to a product that does not explain itself, which quotes Rams and fails his fourth principle.

### 4.3 Susan Kare, Bill Atkinson and the Macintosh team

Susan Kare (born 1954) drew the Macintosh's icons and bitmap faces (Chicago, Geneva, Monaco) in 1983 and 1984 on graph paper, one square per pixel; the command key symbol came from a Swedish campground sign found in a symbol dictionary; the Happy Mac, the lasso, the paint bucket. Later: Windows 3.0 (the Solitaire card backs), NeXT, Pinterest (2015 onward), Niantic. Method, from her own talks and the 2015 book "Susan Kare: Icons": the pixel as the medium; draw every icon at its final size; test whether a stranger can name the icon in a second.

Bill Atkinson (1951 to 2025) wrote QuickDraw, MacPaint and HyperCard; Andy Hertzfeld's folklore.org (2004; printed as "Revolution in the Valley", 2004) documents the team's method: the rounded rectangle story (Jobs walked Atkinson around the block pointing at rounded rectangles until he agreed to implement them as a primitive), the pirate flag, the weekly demos, the sign-off where the team signed the inside of the case.

- Steal: draw at the final size for anything that will be seen small (app icons, favicons, the jeweller's monogram at 16 px).
- Trap: pixel art as retro flavour.

### 4.4 Alan Kay, Bret Victor and Bill Buxton

Alan Kay (born 1940): the Dynabook concept (1968; the 1972 paper "A Personal Computer for Children of All Ages"), Smalltalk, the overlapping-window interface at Xerox PARC; his 1971 line that the best way to predict the future is to invent it; his 1987 talk "Doing with Images Makes Symbols". Method: design the medium for the learner, prototype the future on hardware that does not yet exist, and judge by what children can do with it.

Bret Victor (born 1978): "Magic Ink" (2006), "Inventing on Principle" (CUSEC, January 2012), "Learnable Programming" (September 2012), "The Humane Representation of Thought" (2014), Dynamicland (Oakland, 2017 onward). Method, stated in "Inventing on Principle": find the principle you would fight for (his: creators need an immediate connection to what they create), then let every project be a test of it; the talks demonstrate working, coded prototypes, and the essays are built so the reader manipulates the examples. Dynamicland extends it to a building where computation is physical and communal.

Bill Buxton (born 1949): "Sketching User Experiences" (2007); "The Long Nose of Innovation" (BusinessWeek, 2008), the observation that technologies take about 20 years from invention to market (the mouse: 1965 to the Macintosh in 1984). Method: sketches are quick, cheap, plentiful and disposable; a prototype is the opposite; most design failures come from prototyping before sketching. His collection of input devices is the research method made physical.

- Steal: Victor's principle test (what would you fight for?) as the first question in a studio hire; Buxton's sketch count (dozens of sketches before one prototype) as a review gate.
- Trap: Victor's essays as aesthetics (the pastel diagrams); the content is the interactivity.

### 4.5 Don Norman, Jared Spool, Alan Cooper, Jesse James Garrett

Don Norman (born 1935): "The Psychology of Everyday Things" (1988, retitled "The Design of Everyday Things", revised 2013), Apple Fellow and vice president of the Advanced Technology Group (1993 to 1997, where he coined the title "user experience architect"), Nielsen Norman Group (1998, with Jakob Nielsen), "Emotional Design" (2004). Method: affordances (from Gibson: what an object permits), signifiers (what tells you where to act; added in 2013 to correct his own earlier misuse), mappings, feedback, conceptual models; the seven stages of action; the Norman door (a door whose form signals the wrong action, a pull handle on a push door) as the test.

Jared Spool: User Interface Engineering (1988), Center Centre (the Chattanooga UX school); the 300 million dollar button article (2009): removing a forced registration step before checkout raised an e-commerce client's annual revenue by about 300 million dollars by his account. Method: observe users doing real tasks, count the failures, change one thing.

Alan Cooper (born 1952): "father of Visual Basic", "About Face" (1995), "The Inmates Are Running the Asylum" (1999), Cooper (1992). Method: goal-directed design; personas, which he traces (in his essay "The Origin of Personas") to 1983, when he role-played a project manager named Kathy while designing a planning tool, and formalised in 1999 as a small set of archetypes with goals, built from interviews, to end arguments about "the user".

Jesse James Garrett: Adaptive Path co-founder (2001), "The Elements of User Experience" (the diagram 30 March 2000; the book 2002) with five planes from abstract to concrete: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface; the essay "Ajax: A New Approach to Web Applications" (18 February 2005) that named the technique. Method: decide each plane before the one above it; a surface decision that contradicts a strategy decision is a defect to be traced down the stack.

- Steal: Garrett's five planes as the order of critique (section 11.6) and Cooper's three personas with goals for every Mask360 product brief.
- Trap: personas as marketing segments with stock photos; Cooper's are behavioural and few.

### 4.6 Research and discovery: Indi Young, Erika Hall, Teresa Torres

Indi Young: Adaptive Path co-founder; "Mental Models" (2008), "Practical Empathy" (2015), "Time to Listen" (2022). Method: listening sessions with no agenda, then mental-model diagrams of the person's reasoning, with the product's features aligned beneath the towers of reasoning so gaps show; thinking styles as the unit of segmentation.

Erika Hall: Mule Design (2001, with Mike Monteiro); "Just Enough Research" (2013; second edition 2019), "Conversational Design" (2018). Method: research is asking questions you do not know the answer to, scoped to the decision in front of you; competitive research, interviews and usability tests sized to a week; the interface is a conversation and should be written as one (which 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md, section 2, builds on).

Teresa Torres: "Continuous Discovery Habits" (2021). Method: the product trio (product, design, engineering) talks to customers every week; the opportunity solution tree maps a desired outcome to opportunities to solutions to assumption tests, so the team's arguments are about the tree and the evidence on it.

- Steal: Hall's "just enough" scoping for an agency timeline: a week of research, written as the decisions it will inform.
- Trap: discovery as a permanent state.

### 4.7 Julie Zhuo and the Facebook critique culture

Julie Zhuo: Facebook 2006 to 2019 (vice president of product design), "The Making of a Manager" (2019), the "Year of the Looking Glass" essays; Sundial (co-founded after Facebook). Method, from the essays and the book: design critique as a scheduled ritual with rules (the presenter states the goal and the stage, the room critiques against the goal, feedback is a question before it is a verdict); managers judged on the team's output; the "looking glass" essays on how to tell good work from merely polished work.

- Trap: critique as consensus; Zhuo's model has a decider.

### 4.8 Brian Chesky and the design-led company

Airbnb (2008, Chesky, Joe Gebbia, Nathan Blecharczyk; Chesky and Gebbia are RISD graduates). The eleven-star experience (Reid Hoffman's "Masters of Scale", episode 1, 2017): design the five-star version, then the six, seven and so on to the absurd eleven-star (Elon Musk meets you at the airport), then work back to the version that is magical and feasible. - Steal: the storyboard of the whole customer experience, drawn, for every hospitality and real-estate client; the eleven-star exercise as a workshop format.
- Trap: founder-as-designer without the design training; Chesky's is from RISD.

### 4.9 Karri Saarinen: Airbnb DLS, Linear and the quality essay

Karri Saarinen: Airbnb principal designer; "Building a Visual Language" (airbnb.design, 2016), the essay behind Airbnb's Design Language System (see 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.10); Linear (2019, with Jori Lallo and Tuomas Artman). The Linear Method (linear.app/method) and the redesign posts are the primary sources. "How we redesigned the Linear UI (part II)" (28 March 2024, Saarinen with Yann-Edern Gillet, Andreas Eldh and Romain Cascino): the theme generator was rebuilt on LCH (lightness, chroma, hue, a perceptually uniform colour space) because LCH is uniform where HSL (hue, saturation, lightness, a simple cylindrical transform of RGB) is not, so a theme looks consistent across base colours; light and dark themes moved to the same generator; each theme takes three inputs, base colour, accent colour and contrast, replacing 98 separate variables; the contrast input produces very high-contrast themes for accessibility; chrome blue was reduced in the calculations and neutrals were darkened in light mode and lightened in dark. His ten rules as summarised by Figma's blog (2024): the best design is opinionated; a product should not reach customers before it passes the quality bar; Linear does not decide by A/B tests.

Contested: Linear's refusal of A/B tests (an opinionated quality bar, argued by Saarinen) against GOV.UK's "design with data" (section 11.5) and Booking.com's experimentation culture, which Stefan Thomke describes in "Experimentation Works" (2020) and in Harvard Business Review (March 2020) as some 25,000 tests a year. The house position: A/B tests are for conversion mechanics (the booking flow, form fields, the date picker), and the brand register (type, image, tone) stays with the creative director, because a short-run click win can erode the price a luxury brand can charge, and most luxury microsites lack the traffic for a valid test anyway (about 15,000 visitors per variant to detect a 20 percent lift on a 2 percent baseline; 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, section 12.3).

- Steal: three inputs for a theme (base, accent, contrast) generated in LCH or OKLCH (the polar form of the Oklab colour space, more uniform in hue than LCH); see 04-colour.md, sections 5.1 and 16.5, for authoring in OKLCH and the lightness bands for light and dark themes.
- Trap: "Linear style" (dark, glassy, gradient glow) as a look; the method is the generator and the quality bar.

### 4.11 The craft engineers: Rauno Freiberg, Emil Kowalski, Paco Coursey

## 6. Editing, cinematography, directing and photography

### 6.1 Walter Murch

Background: born 1943; sound and picture on "The Conversation" (1974), sound design on "Apocalypse Now" (1979, Oscar; he coined the credit "sound designer" for it), picture and sound on "The English Patient" (1996, Oscars for both, the first editing Oscar for a film cut on a digital system, the Avid); "In the Blink of an Eye" (1995, second edition 2001) is the primary source; Michael Ondaatje's "The Conversations" (2002) is the near-primary one.

Method, from the book and the Ondaatje conversations:

1. The Rule of Six. An ideal cut satisfies, in order of weight: emotion (51 percent), story (23), rhythm (10), eye-trace (7, where the viewer's eye is on the frame before and after the cut), the two-dimensional plane of the screen (5, screen direction and the 180-degree line, the axis between two subjects that the camera must not cross if screen direction is to hold) and three-dimensional space (4, continuity of position). When a cut cannot satisfy all six, give up from the bottom: a cut that breaks spatial continuity but lands the emotion is correct; the reverse is never correct. The percentages are deliberately lopsided, emotion outweighing the other five combined.
2. The blink. Murch observed that people blink at the moment a thought completes, and that a good cut falls where an attentive viewer would blink; the cut is punctuation of thought, so cutting on the wrong frame feels like a comma in the middle of a word.
3. Standing. He edits standing at a raised bench because he cuts as a surgeon or a conductor works, with the body engaged; the method point is that the edit has a physical rhythm.
4. Stills on the wall. He grabs representative frames from every set-up and pins them in grids beside the bench, so the whole film's visual vocabulary is visible at once (the reconstructed lesson: the storyboard after the shoot).
5. Distance from the material. He has argued, in the book and in interviews, for seeing the assembly fresh and for not leaning on temporary music, which masks a weak cut by supplying emotion the pictures have not earned.

- Steal: the Rule of Six as the critique order for every Mask360 film edit: ask "what does the viewer feel at this cut" before asking about continuity.
- Trap: quoting the percentages as science; Murch offers them as a weighting of priorities from his own practice.

### 6.2 The editor and director pairs

- Thelma Schoonmaker with Martin Scorsese: from "Who's That Knocking at My Door" (1967) and on every Scorsese feature since "Raging Bull" (1980, her first Oscar; then "The Aviator", 2004, and "The Departed", 2006). Method, from her interviews: the editor as the director's longest collaborator, in the room daily; "Raging Bull"'s fights cut for the subjective experience of the blow, slow motion and sound dropouts against the realist dialogue scenes, which is the Rule of Six's first item practised before it was written.
- Kirk Baxter and Angus Wall with David Fincher: Oscars for "The Social Network" (2010) and "The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo" (2011). The opening bar scene of "The Social Network" is widely reported to have taken 99 takes; Aaron Sorkin's dialogue was cut at a pace that made a nine-page conversation run about five minutes. Method: Fincher shoots many takes of locked, precise frames, and the edit chooses performance line by line across takes, so the rhythm is constructed in the cutting room.
- Paul Machliss with Edgar Wright: "Baby Driver" (2017, Oscar nomination). Machliss cut on set during the shoot, syncing takes to the song tracks in real time, because the film's action is choreographed to music and a take that drifted off the beat had to be reshot before the unit moved. Wright's method across his films is the full animatic (the storyboard frames cut to sound as a timed film) before the shoot (the seed of "Baby Driver" was his 2003 music video for Mint Royale's "Blue Song").
- Sally Menke with Quentin Tarantino: every Tarantino feature from "Reservoir Dogs" (1992) to "Inglourious Basterds" (2009); she died in 2010. Method, reconstructed from interviews and the films: chapter structure and long dialogue held past comfort, then a sudden cut to violence; the scene's tension is a duration decision.
- Dody Dorn with Christopher Nolan on "Memento" (2000, Oscar nomination): the reverse-chronology structure was built so the audience shares the protagonist's amnesia, the edit as the film's idea.
- Lee Smith with Nolan from "Batman Begins" (2005) to "Dunkirk" (2017, Oscar): three timelines (a week, a day, an hour) intercut so they converge; the method is structural cross-cutting with the score (Hans Zimmer's use of the Shepard tone, an auditory illusion of a pitch that seems to rise forever) carrying continuity.
- Vashi Nedomansky: editor and post-production workflow consultant whose Vashi Visuals blog publishes shot-length and pacing studies; the method lesson is the average shot length (ASL, the running time divided by the number of shots) as a measurable design value. A 30-second luxury film at an ASL of 3 seconds has 10 cuts; at 1.2 seconds it has 25 and reads as a sportswear ad.
- Steal: decide the ASL before the shoot. A Mask360 hotel film for Instagram Reels runs at an ASL chosen for the brand's tempo, then the shot list is written to that count (see 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 3.1).
- Trap: pace as energy; a fast ASL on a slow brand is the commonest category error in luxury video.

### 6.4 Directors as design thinkers

- David Fincher (source: Adam Nayman, "David Fincher: Mind Games", 2021): control and repetition: many takes (the 99-take report above), locked or motion-controlled camera moves that feel inevitable, previsualisation (a rough animated version of a sequence made before shooting) of complex sequences, capture at a larger resolution than delivery so the frame can be stabilised and reframed in post. The design lesson is that perfection is a process with a budget line.
- Jonathan Glazer: Guinness "Surfer" (1999, agency AMV BBDO, writers Walter Campbell and Tom Carty): black-and-white horses in the surf, the Moby Dick voiceover, built on the line that good things come to those who wait; "Under the Skin" (2013, hidden cameras in a van, non-actors, Mica Levi's score); "The Zone of Interest" (2023, ten fixed cameras hidden in the house, Johnnie Burn's sound design carrying what the image withholds). Method, reconstructed from the films and the crews' interviews: image and sound as equal authors, and the withheld image as the strongest image.
- Spike Jonze: music videos ("Sabotage", 1994; "Praise You", 1999), the Kenzo World fragrance film (2016), "Her" (2013). Method, reconstructed from the work: one human, one absurd premise, played completely straight.
- Wes Anderson (source: Matt Zoller Seitz, "The Wes Anderson Collection", 2013): the planimetric frame (the term is David Bordwell's: the camera perpendicular to a back wall, figures arranged like a frieze), symmetry, a fixed palette per film, the animatic voiced by Anderson himself for the stop-motion and live-action films alike. The Accidentally Wes Anderson problem: Wally Koval's Instagram account (2017) and book (2020) turned the style into a filter, and the 2023 AI "Wes Anderson trailer" meme made it the most cloned directorial look. The lesson: a look so legible that a machine can reproduce it is no longer a brand differentiator.
- Denis Villeneuve (source: Tanya Lapointe, "The Art and Soul of Dune", 2020): storyboard-led ("Arrival", "Blade Runner 2049", "Dune" with storyboard artist Sam Hudecki), monumental scale against small figures, silence.
- Paul Thomas Anderson, reconstructed from the films: film format as choice ("The Master" in 65 mm, 2012; "One Battle After Another", 2025, in VistaVision, section 6.3); the long take as performance capture.
- Chloé Zhao, reconstructed from the films: non-actors and real places, magic-hour light (the short window around sunset or sunrise when light is soft and warm; "Nomadland", 2020, Oscars for picture and director).
- Kogonada: video essays before features ("Kubrick // One-Point Perspective", "Wes Anderson // Centered", Ozu studies), then "Columbus" (2017) and "After Yang" (2021). The method: analyse the master's frame on screen, then make your own frame with that knowledge.
- Steal: Fincher's budget line for perfection (takes, rehearsal, and capture at a higher resolution than delivery so the frame can be reframed) written into the shoot plan for a hero film.
- Trap: a director's look (the Anderson symmetry, the Villeneuve haze) borrowed by a brand with no reason for it; the Accidentally Wes Anderson lesson above.

### 6.5 Commercial, fashion and music video directors

Commercial and fashion film is where Mask360's video work lives; the methods below are reconstructed from reels and from directors' interviews unless noted.

- Romain Gavras: Justice "Stress" (2008), M.I.A. "Born Free" (2010), Kanye West and Jay-Z "No Church in the Wild" (2012), the feature "Athena" (2022) built on long, choreographed takes. Method: the music video as a short film with political weight, crowds staged as architecture.
- Megaforce (Paris collective): co-directed Rihanna's "Bitch Better Have My Money" (2015) with the artist; graphic, narrative and cut tight.
- Nadia Lee Cohen: photographer and director of uncanny Americana; books "Women" (2020) and "Hello, My Name Is" (2022). Method: casting and styling as the whole idea, colour and wigs as character.
- Daniel Wolfe: The Shoes "Time to Dance" (2010), the feature "Catch Me Daddy" (2014, co-written with Matthew Wolfe). Method: social realism with one violent turn, cast from real places.
- Vincent Haycock: Calvin Harris "Feel So Close" (2011). Method: documentary casting and natural light inside a pop video.
- Prakash Varma (Nirvana Films, Bengaluru): the Vodafone ZooZoos (2009, for the IPL, with Ogilvy), actors in white bodysuits shot to look animated. Method: one absurdly simple character system that carries dozens of films.
- Prasoon Pandey (Corcoise Films, Mumbai): the long-running Fevicol and Fevikwik films with Ogilvy, comic timing built on one visual demonstration of the product.
- Music video as design school: Hype Williams (the fish-eye lens and letterboxed colour washes for Busta Rhymes and Missy Elliott, late 1990s); Michel Gondry (Daft Punk "Around the World", 1997, every dancer group mapped to one instrument; The Chemical Brothers "Star Guitar", 2002, the landscape passing the train window cut to the beat); Chris Cunningham (Aphex Twin "Come to Daddy", 1997; Björk "All Is Full of Love", 1999); Dave Meyers (Missy Elliott "Get Ur Freak On", 2001; Kendrick Lamar "Humble", 2017); Hiro Murai (Childish Gambino "This Is America", 2018, a long take with the violence staged in the edges of the frame). The lesson: each video is one visual rule pursued to its end, which is the single symbol method (section 2.7) in time.
- Steal: Gondry's mapping (every visual element mapped to one musical element) for any brand film cut to music.
- Trap: the music video vocabulary (fish-eye, strobe, fast whips) applied to a luxury brand whose tempo is slow.

### 6.6 Stills photographers as method

Dates and careers are in 01-history-and-movements.md, section 11.1; these are the methods a jewellery, fragrance or beauty shoot can borrow.

- Irving Penn: the corner portrait (1948), two flats pushed into a narrow angle so the sitter has nowhere to lean and the pose becomes gesture; still life as sculpture, from Clinique's products (from 1968) onward, single objects on plain grounds lit to describe the material. The model for jewellery and fragrance product shots.
- Richard Avedon: the white sweep (a continuous backdrop curving from wall to floor so no horizon shows), which removes place so the gesture carries the image.
- Peter Lindbergh: unretouched faces in black and white with a cinema reference; the five models on the British Vogue cover of January 1990 are the precedent for a no-retouch brief.
- Dayanita Singh (Delhi): photographs sequenced into "museums", wooden structures and book-objects she rearranges at each showing ("File Room", 2013; "Museum of Chance", 2014). Method, from her own books and talks: the edit and the sequence are the work, so the same pictures make new meanings in new orders.
- Steal: Penn's still-life discipline for product (one object, plain ground, light that describes the material) and Singh's re-sequencing for a campaign that must refresh monthly from one shoot.
- Trap: Lindbergh's unretouched look faked with a grain filter over retouched skin.

## 7. Fashion, luxury, hospitality and jewellery creative directors

The creative director of a luxury house is a design thinker whose medium is a world: collections, shows, stores, campaigns, objects and the house's image of itself. The methods below come from documented lectures, interviews and the houses' own communications, and are marked "reconstructed" where no such source exists; personnel are as of October 2026, with the dated roster in section 7.13.

### 7.1 The house-codes method

House codes are the fixed vocabulary a maison owns: materials, motifs, colours, silhouettes and objects (18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 8, lists the category-wide codes). The creative director's job is to recombine, exaggerate and transpose a house's own codes each season; every later entry in this section works inside them.

- Karl Lagerfeld at Chanel (1983 to 2019): took over a house widely seen as dormant and treated Coco Chanel's tweed, quilting, chain, camellia, two-tone slingback, black and white, pearls and interlocking CC as a vocabulary to recombine at the speed of daily sketching, while also designing Fendi (from 1965). Method reconstructed from the collections; Blazy (section 7.4) now works inside the same codes.
- Tom Ford at Gucci (creative director 1994 to 2004): turned a near-bankrupt house through total image control, with product, stores and advertising (with stylist Carine Roitfeld and photographer Mario Testino) under one hand; the Fall 1995 show is the usual turning point. Method reconstructed from the collections and the press of the period.
- Giorgio Armani (11 July 1934 to 4 September 2025): the unstructured jacket; the "American Gigolo" wardrobe (1980) as a launch; one hand over fashion, Armani/Casa, restaurants and the Armani Hotel Dubai in Burj Khalifa (opened 27 April 2010), the Gulf precedent for a fashion house running a hotel.
- Hermès: an annual theme carried through scarves, windows and campaigns, so a dozen product lines speak one idea a year (start year unconfirmed as of October 2026).
- Worksheet step for clients: list the client's ten codes before any campaign; each piece must use at least two and may break at most one, because two codes make the house recognisable and one break keeps it alive.
- Steal: the code list as the first page of every brief.
- Trap: codes copied from another house (the quilting, the chain) onto a client that never owned them.

### 7.2 Virgil Abloh (1980 to 2021)

Background: architecture degree (Illinois Institute of Technology), Kanye West's creative director, Off-White (2013), Louis Vuitton men's artistic director (2018 to 2021); "Figures of Speech" (Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago, 2019); died 28 November 2021.

Method, from the Harvard Graduate School of Design lecture of October 2017 (titled, by the Harvard Gazette's report, "Insert Complex Title Here", and closed with the retitle "Insert Yourself Here") and from his later talks:

1. The 3 percent approach: change an existing design by about 3 percent, enough to make it new and little enough to keep it recognisable (his example in the lecture: the Nike Air Force 1 edited by 3 percent).
2. Speak to the tourist and the purist simultaneously: every piece carries a reading for the newcomer and a reference for the expert.
3. The readymade: an existing object recontextualised, after Duchamp; the Off-White quotation marks ("SCULPTURE", "AIR") made the irony visible on the product.
4. Free Game (2020), his online guide for young people starting a brand, and the "cheat codes" he described in talks: shortcuts that let an outsider bypass the gatekeepers.

- Steal: the 3 percent rule for heritage work: a Rajasthan palace hotel's crest edited by 3 percent reads as renewed and keeps the equity.
- Trap: quotation marks and the readymade without the knowledge that made Abloh's edits pointed; the method needs the purist's library.

### 7.3 Jonathan Anderson

Background: JW Anderson (2008); Loewe creative director (2013 to 2025), where he founded the Loewe Foundation Craft Prize (first edition 2016); appointed to Dior men's in April 2025 and women's in June 2025, the first designer to lead all Dior lines since Christian Dior; Dior men's debut June 2025, women's ready-to-wear debut on 1 October 2025 in the Tuileries.

Method, from his interviews and the Loewe record: craft and absurdity together. Loewe's objects (the balloon heel, the pixelated garments, the egg and tomato motifs) are surreal gestures built on real craft (leather, weaving, ceramics), and the Craft Prize made the house the patron of the makers it borrowed from. Campaigns and catalogues are treated as editorial publications.

- Steal: one surreal gesture inside a classical frame, made with real craft (design-taste names Loewe in its canon; the method here is the craft patronage that legitimises it).
- Trap: surrealism as meme bait without the craft underneath.

### 7.4 Matthieu Blazy

Background: Raf Simons's studio, Maison Margiela Artisanal, Celine under Phoebe Philo, Calvin Klein; Bottega Veneta (creative director 2021 to 2024); Chanel creative director (appointed December 2024; debut ready-to-wear 6 October 2025 at the Grand Palais; couture debut January 2026).

Method, from the Bottega collections: material trompe l'oeil (one material made to pass for another), leather printed and finished to look like denim, flannel and cotton (first Bottega collection, Winter 2022), so the luxury is invisible until touched. At Chanel, by the reviews of the debut: the house codes (tweed, the suit, the blouse) altered in proportion and material without breaking them, which is Abloh's 3 percent practised by a craftsman.

- Steal: the luxury that reveals itself on contact: a jewellery box whose interior is finished better than its exterior.
- Trap: trompe l'oeil as a joke for the camera; Blazy's leather denim pays off only for the person who touches it.

### 7.5 Phoebe Philo, Miuccia Prada, Raf Simons

- Phoebe Philo: Chloé (2001 to 2006), Céline (2008 to 2017), her own label (from October 2023, sold in drops through its own site). Method, reconstructed from the work: the product as campaign (the Céline campaigns photographed by Juergen Teller, including Joan Didion in 2015), anti-marketing, no social media presence, the clothes for an adult woman's real life.
- Miuccia Prada: Prada from 1978; "ugly chic", the Spring/Summer 1996 collection (the "Banal" season) that made unglamorous colours and prints desirable; Fondazione Prada (1993; the Milan venue by OMA opened 2015); co-creative director with Raf Simons since 2020. Method, from her interviews and the show notes: an intellectual position (about women, about taste, about uniformity) stated in each collection and argued through deliberately uncomfortable choices.
- Raf Simons: his own label (1995 to 2022), Jil Sander (2005 to 2012), Dior (2012 to 2015; the documentary "Dior and I", 2014, records the flower-covered show walls), Calvin Klein (2016 to 2018, with Peter Saville on the identity), Prada (2020 onward). Method, reconstructed from the collections and "Dior and I": youth subculture and contemporary art as the references (the 2003 "Closer" collection with Saville's Joy Division artwork; Sterling Ruby at Calvin Klein).
- Steal: Philo's product-as-campaign for a jewellery launch: the piece photographed so plainly that the campaign is the product.
- Trap: anti-marketing without a product strong enough to carry it.

### 7.6 Demna, Hedi Slimane, Simon Porte Jacquemus

- Demna: Vetements (2014), Balenciaga (2015 to July 2025; revived Balenciaga couture in July 2021, the first since Cristóbal Balenciaga closed the house in 1968), Gucci artistic director from July 2025 (debut "La Famiglia" lookbook, September 2025). Method, from the Balenciaga show notes and the work: anti-design as strategy, the ugly and the banal (the IKEA bag, the Crocs collaboration, the mud show) used to comment on luxury from inside it; the couture return proved the craft beneath the provocation. Pierpaolo Piccioli succeeded him at Balenciaga.
- Hedi Slimane: Dior Homme (2000 to 2007), Saint Laurent (2012 to 2016, dropping "Yves" from the ready-to-wear name), Celine (2018 to 2024, dropping the accent). Method, from his own photography books (for example "Anthology of a Decade", 2011) and the work: total control, the silhouette (skinny, rock), and photographing his own campaigns; the identity edits (the name, the accent) as statements of a new regime.
- Simon Porte Jacquemus: label founded 2009; scale and sun: shows in a lavender field at Valensole (2019) and in wheat fields, the micro "Le Chiquito" bag, CGI giant bags on Paris streets (2023). Method, reconstructed from the work: Instagram-native world building, the south of France as the whole brand.
- Steal: Jacquemus's single place as the whole brand world, for a Goa or Jaipur label.
- Trap: Demna's provocation without the proof underneath; the banal objects were credible because the July 2021 couture show proved the craft.

### 7.7 Pharrell Williams, Alessandro Michele, Daniel Lee, Kim Jones, Nicolas Ghesquière

- Pharrell Williams: Louis Vuitton men's creative director (February 2023; debut June 2023 on the Pont Neuf). Method, reconstructed from the work: the creative director as cultural convenor, music, celebrity and the Damier reworked into camouflage ("Damoflage").
- Alessandro Michele: Gucci (2015 to 2022), Valentino (from April 2024, debut September 2024). Method, reconstructed from the collections: maximalism as an archive method, a collection assembled from historical and pop references until density becomes the identity.
- Daniel Lee: Bottega Veneta (2018 to 2021, the green and the intrecciato padded bags; intrecciato: the house's woven-leather technique), Burberry (from October 2022). The Burberry decision of February 2023: the Equestrian Knight Design returned with a serif wordmark, reversing the 2018 Saville sans; the reason given was heritage and Britishness, and the lesson is that an identity can be reversed in five years when the strategy changes.
- Kim Jones: Dior men's (2018 to 2025), Fendi women's and couture (2020 to 2024). Method, reconstructed from the collections: collaboration with artists (KAWS, Raymond Pettibon, Amoako Boafo) as the season's idea.
- Nicolas Ghesquière: Balenciaga (1997 to 2012), Louis Vuitton women's (from 2013). Method, reconstructed from the shows: futurism through archive, the Louvre and Fondation Louis Vuitton show sites as architecture.
- Steal: Kim Jones's artist collaboration as the season's idea, for a Mask360 fashion client's capsule with an Indian artist.
- Trap: the creative director as celebrity convenor without a product idea; Damoflage works because the Damier is the house's own code.

### 7.8 Rei Kawakubo, Yohji Yamamoto, Martin Margiela

- Rei Kawakubo: Comme des Garçons (1969, the company from 1973); "Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body" (Spring/Summer 1997, the padded "lumps and bumps"); Dover Street Market (2004, with Adrian Joffe); the Metropolitan Museum's "Art of the In-Between" (2017). Method: the refusal to explain; collections are given abstract titles and no interpretation, so the audience does the work. She also designs the shops, the graphics and the invitations: the brand as one authored world.
- Yohji Yamamoto: black as a position, asymmetry, the garment that is finished by the wearer's movement; Wim Wenders's "Notebook on Cities and Clothes" (1989) is the near-primary record.
- Martin Margiela: Maison Martin Margiela (1988 to 2009). Anonymity (the blank label held by four white stitches, no photographs of the designer), deconstruction (linings outside, garments made from found objects), the Tabi boot (1988). The documentary "Martin Margiela: In His Own Words" (2019) is the near-primary source. Glenn Martens became creative director of Maison Margiela in 2025.
- Steal: Kawakubo's authored world (shop, graphics, invitation by the same hand) for a founder-led Indian label.
- Trap: anonymity as a pose; Margiela's was a refusal of the cult of the designer that the house then sold.

### 7.9 Indian creative directors

- Sabyasachi Mukherjee: label founded 1999 in Kolkata. Method: world building through objects and photography; the Bengal vernacular (Kalighat painting, antique textiles, colonial interiors) arranged with an obsessive density in stores, campaigns and the jewellery line; the Mumbai flagship in Ador House, Kala Ghoda, designed as a house full of collected objects, where the shop is the campaign. His campaigns are cast and photographed like portraiture of an imagined Indian aristocracy. Steal: the flagship as the brand's world, which is the brief for any Mask360 retail client.
- Sanjay Garg, Raw Mango (2008): restraint and the textile: handloom Chanderi and Banarasi revived with modern colour, campaigns that treat the drape and the weave as the image. Steal: the textile as the hero shot.
- Rahul Mishra: Woolmark Prize (2014); the first Indian designer on the official Paris Haute Couture calendar (2020). Method: slow, hand-embroidered couture (nature motifs) as an employment model for artisans, stated as the reason for the work.
- Gaurav Gupta: sculptural couture, shown on the Paris couture calendar; red-carpet dressing (Cardi B at the 2023 Grammys) as the global launch mechanism.
- Trap: Sabyasachi's density without the collection behind it, which reads as a prop shop.

### 7.10 The creative director's week in a luxury house

Reconstructed from interviews and documentaries ("Dior and I", 2014; "The First Monday in May", 2016; "Martin Margiela: In His Own Words", 2019): a house that shows men's, women's, pre-collections, resort and couture runs six to eight collections a year, so the creative director's week has a fixed rhythm.

| Day | Work | Method point |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Studio review of boards and toiles (test garments in plain cotton) for the current collection | Decisions made on physical prototypes in the room |
| Tuesday | Fittings with the house model; atelier heads present | The premier (head of atelier) holds the craft authority; the designer holds the idea |
| Wednesday | Campaign and show set: photographer, set designer, casting director | The show set is the campaign's first frame |
| Thursday | Stores and products: leather goods, fragrance, eyewear reviews | The commercial lines fund the show; the creative director signs them off |
| Friday | Archive, references, the next collection's research | Research runs a season ahead |

- Steal: when a luxury client's creative director briefs the agency, the brief arrives in the gap between fittings and the campaign, so the agency's first deliverable is a physical board and one photograph, presented in the studio.
- Trap: a deck sent by email to a creative director who decides with objects on a table.

### 7.11 Hospitality, spatial and retail world-builders

Luxury hospitality is Mask360's lead vertical; these masters sit behind the hotel brief in section 12.3. Zumthor's principle set is dissected in section 11.2.

- Adrian Zecha, Aman (founded 1988; Amanpuri, Phuket, opened 1988, designed by Ed Tuttle): the resort as a private house, usually fewer than 55 rooms and a staff-to-guest ratio reported at about six to one, so service does the work of a lobby and signage. Aman-i-Khás, Ranthambore (2003, tents designed by Jean-Michel Gathy) is the India case. Method reconstructed from the properties.
- Kerry Hill (1943 to 2018): Amanusa (Bali, 1992), The Datai (Langkawi, 1994), Aman Tokyo (2014). Method, reconstructed from the buildings: the arrival designed as a sequence of shade, water and thresholds, so the guest has slowed down before reaching a room.
- Geoffrey Bawa (1919 to 2003): Kandalama (1991 to 1994), set into the rock and grown over with vegetation; the Lighthouse hotel at Galle (1995 to 1997); Lunuganga, his garden estate, reworked from 1948 to 1998. Method, from David Robson's "Geoffrey Bawa: The Complete Works" (2002): the site and the view decide the plan and the building steps aside, the model for Goa and Kerala briefs.
- Bill Bensley: Shinta Mani Wild (Cambodia, 2018), Capella Ubud (Bali, 2018). Method, reconstructed from the properties and bensley.com: a written back-story (at Capella Ubud, a nineteenth-century explorers' tented camp) governs every object, so each lamp and tent has a reason inside the story.
- Ilse Crawford (Studioilse, London, 2001): Soho House New York (2003), Ett Hem, Stockholm (2012), and the Man and Well-Being department she founded at Design Academy Eindhoven (2000); book "A Frame for Life: The Designs of StudioIlse" (2014). Method, from the book and her talks: start from behaviour and the senses (where people sit, what they touch, the light on the table) and let the look follow.
- Ian Schrager: Morgans (1984, interiors by Andrée Putman) and the Royalton (1988, Philippe Starck) made the lobby a social room; EDITION, his partnership with Marriott International, includes The Abu Dhabi EDITION. Method reconstructed from the hotels.
- Bijoy Jain, Studio Mumbai: Palmyra House (Nandgaon, near Alibag, 2007). Method, from the studio's exhibitions: its own craftsmen build full-scale mock-ups and material samples before the drawings are fixed, so the building is designed in the material.
- Dennis Paphitis, Aesop (Melbourne, 1987): no two stores alike, each designed for its street, often by a different architect, with the same products and the same restraint; acquired by L'Oréal in 2023 for about 2.5 billion dollars.
- Jean Nouvel, Louvre Abu Dhabi (inaugurated 8 November 2017, open to the public from 11 November): a dome about 180 metres across of 7,850 aluminium stars in eight layers, producing the "rain of light" that also gave the wayfinding pictograms their shapes.
- Steal: decide the key count, the arrival sequence and the light before any visual identity; for a suite shoot, build Jain's full-scale mock-up of the one corner the campaign will show.
- Trap: beige minimalism and teak sold as "Aman", a look without the key count and the service ratio that produce the calm.

### 8.2 Awwwards jury thinking

Awwwards publishes its system at awwwards.com/about-evaluation (as of October 2026): design 40 percent, usability 30, creativity 20, content 10. Every approved site goes to at least 18 jury members, and the 3 scores furthest from the average are removed automatically; 6.5 or more earns an Honourable Mention. Site of the Day (SOTD) goes to the highest jury score, and a site with a high jury score and at least 10 PRO (validated professional) users can win before its 5-day period ends. Only SOTD winners go to the developer jury, and a developer score above 7 earns the Developer Award. The eight highest-scoring sites each month are nominated for Site of the Month, and every Site of the Month winner is nominated for Site of the Year, announced the following February.

The arithmetic: design and usability carry 70 percent, so a creative site that fails on navigation, performance or accessibility loses on the weights. No named juror's published comment is cited here; the working criteria are the weights read as questions: does the layout hold at every breakpoint (design); can a first-time visitor reach the booking with a keyboard on a mid-range phone (usability); is there one idea no other site has (creativity); does the copy say something specific (content). For a Mask360 luxury microsite this is the house rule as well (21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, section 1.4): the experiment sits on a page that loads fast and can be navigated with a keyboard.

- Steal: score the site against the four weights before submission, with usability tested on a mid-range Android phone.
- Trap: the WebGL experiment that wins creativity points and loses the 30 usability points.

## 9. Creative technologists and generative artists

### 9.1 The code-as-sketch lineage

- The 1960s origin: Vera Molnar (1924 to 2023), Frieder Nake and Manfred Mohr made algorithmic drawings on plotters from the 1960s; Molnar's "machine imaginaire" (rules written as if for a machine and executed by hand before she had computer access) is the ancestor of every generative identity.
- Casey Reas and Ben Fry: Processing (2001, begun at the MIT Media Lab's Aesthetics and Computation Group under John Maeda); the file unit is called a "sketch", and the naming is the method: code as drawing, run often, kept small. The Processing Foundation (2012) and p5.js (2013, started by Lauren Lee McCarthy) carried it to the browser.
- Zach Lieberman: openFrameworks (with Theo Watson and Arturo Castro); a daily sketch posted publicly since January 2016; co-founder of the School for Poetic Computation (New York, 2013). Method: daily output as research, the small poetic experiment that later becomes a commission.
- Golan Levin: Carnegie Mellon's STUDIO for Creative Inquiry; "Messa di Voce" (2003, with Lieberman, voice made visible). Method: the interactive piece as a question about perception.
- Lauren Lee McCarthy: p5.js; "LAUREN" (2017), in which she acted as a human smart-home assistant in people's houses. Method: performance that tests a technology's social premise.
- Tyler Hobbs: "Fidenza" (Art Blocks, 2021), built on flow fields (a grid of angles across the canvas that curves follow step by step), which his essay "Flow Fields" (tylerxhobbs.com, February 2020) documents; the long-form generative method (one algorithm, hundreds of outputs, every output acceptable) puts the designer's work into the range of the rules. Production is in 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md, section 10.
- Steal: the long-form test for any generative identity: run the generator 500 times and reject the system if any output is unusable.
- Trap: the sketch left as a sketch; Processing's naming invites unfinished work presented as finished.

### 9.2 The AI and data artists

- Refik Anadol: "Machine Hallucinations" (from 2019), "Unsupervised" at MoMA (2022 to 2023), the data-as-pigment method: a large dataset (an institution's archive, weather, the museum's collection) trained into a model whose latent space is rendered as moving fluid images on architectural screens. His studio announced Dataland, a museum of AI art at The Grand LA in Los Angeles; opening date unconfirmed as of October 2026.
- Mario Klingemann: "Memories of Passersby I" (sold at Sotheby's in 2019), a machine generating portraits in real time. Method: neural networks as a material with its own errors, the error kept.
- Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst: "PROTO" (2019), made with an AI "baby" called Spawn trained on their ensemble's voices; Holly+ (2021), a voice model others can use, with governance through a collective; "xhairymutantx" at the 2024 Whitney Biennial and "The Call" at the Serpentine (2024 to 2025). Method: consent and ownership designed into the model as part of the art.
- Claire Silver: AI-collaborative artist; method: the artist's taste and editing as the authorship, the model as collaborator, stated in her writing.
- Sougwen Chung: drawing in duet with robotic arms (the D.O.U.G. series, from 2015) trained on her own drawing; the method makes the human and machine marks visibly distinct.
- Steal: Herndon and Dryhurst's consent design for any AI campaign that uses a person's likeness or voice (see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 7.4, for the house rules on likeness and voice).
- Trap: the Anadol fluid as a look; without a dataset that means something to the client, it is a screensaver.

### 9.3 Installation and identity studios

- Ryoji Ikeda: "datamatics" (from 2006), "test pattern": data and sound at the limit of perception, black and white and sine tones. Method: reduce the material to its numerical substrate.
- teamLab (Tokyo, founded 2001 by Toshiyuki Inoko): borderless immersive rooms (teamLab Borderless, Tokyo, 2018, reopened at Azabudai Hills in 2024); teamLab Phenomena Abu Dhabi opened on Saadiyat Island in 2025, which makes it a Gulf reference a Mask360 client will have visited.
- Random International: "Rain Room" (Barbican, 2012), with a permanent Rain Room at the Sharjah Art Foundation since 2018. Method: one physical paradox (rain that does not wet you) built with sensing.
- Universal Everything (Sheffield, Matt Pyke): "Walking City" (2014, Golden Nica at Ars Electronica); method: a behaviour system for figures, applied across brand films and installations.
- Field.io (London) and Onformative (Berlin): generative brand systems and data sculptures; method: the brand's data as the source of form.
- Generative identity studios: Studio Dumbar (section 5.2), Moving Brands (London, identities designed to move from the start), Oddfellows (motion systems for product brands).
- Steal: one physical paradox (Rain Room) as the centrepiece of a launch event in Dubai or Mumbai.
- Trap: the immersive room as a projection-mapped selfie backdrop; teamLab's rooms respond to the visitor.

## 10. Design leaders, studio builders and advertising masters

### 10.1 How a creative organisation thinks

- Ed Catmull: "Creativity, Inc." (2014, with Amy Wallace). The braintrust (section 5.3); the "ugly baby" (early ideas are ugly and must be protected from the organisation's appetite for the finished); Notes Day (2013), when Pixar shut for a day so employees could fix the company's own problems; post-mortems after every film. Method: build mechanisms for candour, then protect the new from them until it is strong.
- Steve Jobs on design: Walter Isaacson's "Steve Jobs" (2011) and Ken Segall's "Insanely Simple" (2012). Segall, the ad man who named the iMac, describes the "simple stick": Jobs ended complexity in meetings, kept meetings small, and pushed one message per ad. Isaacson records the father's fence (care for the unseen part) and the weekly design reviews.
- Tony Fadell: "Build" (2022). iPod, then Nest (2010). Method: the story first: the press release, the packaging and the customer's pain written before the product, and the product judged against that story, because a product that cannot explain why it needs to exist will not be bought.
- John Maeda: "The Laws of Simplicity" (2006), ten laws: reduce, organise, time, learn, differences, context, emotion, trust, failure, the one; the tenth states that simplicity is about subtracting the obvious and adding the meaningful. The Design in Tech reports (from 2015, presented at SXSW) document design's place in technology companies year by year.
- Yves Béhar: fuseproject (1999); the One Laptop per Child XO (2007), Jawbone, August. Method: designers take equity alongside fees, so the studio is invested in the product's life.
- Tim Brown: IDEO (chief executive 2000 to 2019); "Design Thinking" (Harvard Business Review, June 2008), "Change by Design" (2009). Natasha Jen's critique (section 2.19) is the counterweight the house reads with it.
- Marty Neumeier: "The Brand Gap" (2003), "Zag" (2006), "The Designful Company" (2008): the brand defined as a person's gut feeling about a product, and the onliness statement ("our brand is the only ___ that ___") as the positioning test.
- Debbie Millman: "Design Matters" (podcast since 2005), the largest archive of designers describing their own methods; co-founder of the School of Visual Arts Masters in Branding (2009). Use it as the near-primary source for many entries in this file.
- Jessica Hische: lettering (Penguin Drop Caps, 2013); procrastiworking, her observation that the work you do while avoiding the work you should be doing is a clue to the work you should do for life.
- Aaron Draplin: Draplin Design Co. (2004), Field Notes (2007, with Coudal Partners), "Pretty Much Everything" (2016). Logo process, from his talks: pencil roughs, then thick, simple vector shapes built from circles and straight lines, tested in one colour at small size, presented with real applications.
- Steal: Catmull's candour mechanisms (braintrust, Notes Day, post-mortems) as the agency's quarterly rhythm, and Fadell's press release written before the product for every Mask360 launch.
- Trap: candour imported without the protection of the new, so notes aimed at a week-old idea kill it.

### 10.2 The design systems leaders

- Dan Mall: SuperFriendly; "Design That Scales" (2023). Method: the design system run as a product with customers (the product teams), a roadmap and adoption metrics.
- Brad Frost: Atomic Design (blog post 2013, book 2016), five stages named atoms, molecules, organisms, templates and pages; method: build the interface from the smallest parts upward and test in context.
- Jina Anne: with Jon Levine at Salesforce, the origin of design tokens (named, platform-neutral values that implement design decisions in code) in the Lightning Design System (2014); founder of the Clarity conference; a founding co-chair of the W3C Design Tokens Community Group. See 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md, section 4.1, for the token specification as of October 2026.
- Nathan Curtis (EightShapes): the essays that framed a design system as a product serving products; method: governance, versioning and team models written down.
- Alla Kholmatova: "Design Systems" (Smashing Magazine, 2017). Functional patterns (components) and perceptual patterns (the feel: colour, type, motion, voice); systems placed on three axes, strict to loose, modular to integrated, centralised to distributed. Method: diagnose the organisation before choosing the system's shape.
- Steal: Kholmatova's three axes (strict or loose, modular or integrated, centralised or distributed) as the diagnosis before choosing a client's system shape.
- Trap: a component library shipped as "the system" with no owner, roadmap or versioning, which Curtis's essays warn against.

## 11. The great principle sets and systems dissected

### 11.1 How to dissect a set

27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7, gives the creation cycle and short dissections of Rams, Vignelli, the Pixar rules, the HIG, Material, Gerstner, Alexander, Airbnb, Linear, Stripe, Polaris and GOV.UK; this section adds the forbids, enforcement and ageing columns. Seven questions per set: origin (what problem produced it), author, the exact principles, what each forbids (a principle that forbids nothing is a slogan), the enforcement mechanism (who asks which question, when), how it aged (revisions, failures), and what to borrow for a client. The comparison table is in section 13.2.

### 11.2 Objects, type and print

- Dieter Rams, ten principles (late 1970s; exact names in section 2.6). Origin: Rams asking whether his own work was good design, after twenty years of Braun products. Forbids: decoration without function (principle 3 read with 10), overclaim (6), fashion (7), the unfinished hidden part (8). Enforcement: Rams's own review in the factory studio, and today Vitsoe's product decisions. Aged well because each principle is a test on the object; the ninth (environment) reads as prophecy. Borrow: principle 6 (honesty) as the luxury client's anti-overclaim rule.
- The Vignelli Canon (2010). Origin: fifty years of practice written for students. Principles grouped as the intangibles (semantics, syntactics, pragmatics, discipline, appropriateness, ambiguity, design is one, visual power, intellectual elegance, timelessness, responsibility, equity) and the tangibles (paper sizes, grids, margins, typefaces, type sizes, colour, layout, sequence, scale, texture, white space, identity). Forbids: more than a handful of typefaces, arbitrary sizes, trend. Enforcement: the designer's discipline; the canon has no reviewer. Aged: the method holds, the typeface list dates it. Borrow: the tangibles list as a production checklist.
- Bauhaus and Ulm pedagogy. Bauhaus (Weimar 1919, Dessau 1925, Berlin 1932, closed 1933): Gropius's manifesto unifying art and craft; the Vorkurs, the preliminary course (Johannes Itten 1919 to 1923, then László Moholy-Nagy and Josef Albers), in which every student learned material, form and colour from first principles before a specialism. HfG Ulm (1953 to 1968; Max Bill, then Tomás Maldonado and Otl Aicher): design as a discipline with science, method and ergonomics; teamwork with industry (Braun, Lufthansa). Forbids: ornament without reason, the lone genius (Ulm), art for its own sake. Enforcement: the curriculum and the critique. Aged: the Ulm method lives on in design schools and in NID Ahmedabad (section 2.25); its scientism was criticised from the 1960s. Borrow: a preliminary course for every new Mask360 hire, a week of material and colour studies before client work. History in 01-history-and-movements.md, sections 3.3 and 4.2.
- Swiss style (Müller-Brockmann, Emil Ruder, Armin Hofmann; the journal "Neue Grafik", 1958 to 1965). Rules: a mathematical grid; asymmetric layout; one sans serif (Akzidenz-Grotesk, then Helvetica and Univers, both 1957); flush left, ragged right; objective photography as the image. Forbids: centred symmetry, decorative type, subjective imagery. Enforcement: the grid itself. Aged: absorbed into every interface and every corporate system; its universal claim was answered by postmodern and vernacular work from the 1970s. Borrow: the grid as the system's constitution (section 2.3; history in 01-history-and-movements.md, section 4.1).
- The NASA Graphics Standards Manual (Danne & Blackburn, 1975; the "worm" logotype). Origin: the US Federal Design Improvement Program (from 1972) asked agencies to modernise; Richard Danne and Bruce Blackburn delivered a manual specifying the worm, Helvetica, the colours and every application from vehicles to forms. Forbids: improvised use. Aged: the worm was retired in 1992 when the administration restored the 1959 "meatball", and returned in 2020 as a secondary mark; the manual's 2015 Kickstarter reissue (Jesse Reed and Hamish Smyth, about 8,800 backers by press reports) made it a design-world bestseller. Lesson: a system can outlive its political sponsor and return.
- The British Rail Corporate Identity Manual (from 1965, Design Research Unit; the double arrow by Gerald Barney; the Rail Alphabet by Jock Kinneir and Margaret Calvert). Issued as loose-leaf binders updated by replacement sheets, which is the version control of the analogue era. Forbids: local variation in signage, liveries and print. Borrow: a manual built to be updated page by page, the ancestor of a documentation site.
- Karl Gerstner, "Designing Programmes" (1964): the designer designs the rule set and the rule set produces the pieces; dissected in 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.8, and the ancestor of section 11.9.
- Charles and Ray Eames, "The India Report" (April 1958). Origin: the Government of India's invitation, through Pupul Jayakar, to recommend a design institute; the Eameses travelled the country and wrote what they observed. Principles: design rooted in Indian values and problems, learned from objects perfected by use, with the lota (the brass water vessel: form, material, balance and ritual in one) as the emblem. Forbids: imported style without a local problem. Enforcement: the National Institute of Design (opened 1961) as the institution built on it. Aged: the founding text of Indian design education (01-history-and-movements.md, section 10.2). Borrow: the lota test, asking which object in the client's own culture has been perfected by use before designing the new one.
- Peter Zumthor, "Atmospheres" (2006). Nine points: the body of architecture; material compatibility; the sound of a space; the temperature of a space; surrounding objects; between composure and seduction; tension between interior and exterior; levels of intimacy; the light on things; then three codas: architecture as surroundings, coherence, the beautiful form. Forbids: a space judged as an image. Enforcement: none formal; the architect's walk-through. Borrow: the nine points as the review checklist for a suite shoot or a hotel film (does the frame carry sound, temperature and intimacy?).

### 11.3 Story, motion and editing

- The Pixar 22 rules and the braintrust. The rules were tweeted in 2011 by story artist Emma Coats as what she had learned, then circulated as "Pixar's rules". Examples: rule 4 is the story spine (once upon a time, every day, one day, because of that, because of that, until finally); rule 7, come up with the ending before the middle; rule 9, when stuck, list what would not happen next; rule 12, discard the first idea and the next several; rule 19, coincidences that get characters into trouble are fine, coincidences that get them out are cheating. Forbids: the easy rescue, the first idea, the unfinished middle. Enforcement: none formal; the braintrust (section 5.3) is Pixar's mechanism for story quality in general. Borrow: the story spine for every campaign film, and the no-authority note for every Mask360 critique.
- The twelve principles of animation (Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, "The Illusion of Life", 1981, codifying Disney practice of the 1930s): squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead action and pose to pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal. Forbids: linear motion, unclear staging, weightless movement. Enforcement: dailies (the daily screening of the previous day's work) and the supervising animator. Aged: the default vocabulary of UI motion (see 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md, section 2); exaggeration and squash need a light hand in luxury interfaces.
- The Rule of Six (section 6.1): a ranked priority list, enforced by the editor's order of questions.
- Bruce Block, "The Visual Story" (2001; third edition 2020): seven basic visual components (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm) controlled by the principle of contrast and affinity, plotted against the story's intensity curve. Forbids: visual intensity that does not track the story. Borrow: plot contrast against the brand film's emotional peak before the shoot.

### 11.4 Platform design languages

- Apple Human Interface Guidelines. The first Macintosh HIG (1987) set direct manipulation, see-and-point, consistency, feedback and forgiveness. iOS 7 (2013) reduced the HIG to three themes: clarity (legible text, precise icons), deference (the interface recedes behind content) and depth (layers and motion convey hierarchy). Liquid Glass (announced 9 June 2025 for iOS 26, iPadOS 26, macOS Tahoe 26, watchOS 26, tvOS 26 and visionOS 26) is a translucent material that reflects and refracts what sits beneath it, adapts its colour to content and reacts to movement with specular highlights; Apple's newsroom described it as inspired by visionOS, and Alan Dye called it Apple's broadest software design update. The WWDC25 session "Get to know the new design system" frames the foundations as hierarchy, harmony and consistency. Forbids: chrome that competes with content, controls that ignore the concentric geometry of the hardware. Enforcement: App Review, the system components developers get for free, and the annual WWDC sessions. Aged: the material drew legibility and accessibility criticism (Wired and UX Collective among others); Apple added a transparency toggle in iOS 26.1 (October 2025), then at WWDC on 8 June 2026 reduced the default transparency and added a slider from clear to tinted and frosted in iOS 27, iPadOS 27 and macOS 27, alongside a reintroduced Design principles page in the HIG (27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.6). Dye left on 5 December 2025 to become chief design officer of Meta Reality Labs, and Stephen Lemay succeeded him (Reuters, December 2025). Borrow: the content-first hierarchy; the glass itself is on design-taste's peaking list.
- Material Design. 2014 (Matías Duarte's team, Google I/O): three principles, material is the metaphor (paper and ink with real shadows), bold graphic intentional, motion provides meaning. Material Theming (2018) answered the complaint that every app looked like Google. Material You, the third version (2021, Android 12), derived colour from the user's wallpaper. M3 Expressive (announced May 2025; the research figures are in section 4.14): spring-based motion physics, an expanded shape library, emphasised type and new components. Enforcement: Jetpack Compose components and Google's own apps. Borrow: the research-backed argument for expression, which a luxury client can use to justify a bolder type scale.
- Microsoft Metro and Fluent. Metro's roots are the Zune player (2006) and Windows Media Center, formalised in Windows Phone 7 (2010): typography-led, flat, inspired by transit wayfinding and Swiss style, "authentically digital"; the name was dropped in 2012 after a trademark dispute. Fluent (Build, May 2017) added light, depth, motion, material and scale; Fluent 2 (2023) states four principles: natural on every platform, built for focus, one for all and all for one, unmistakably Microsoft. Aged: Metro's purity failed commercially on phones and lived on in Windows tiles and in flat design generally. Borrow: Metro's typographic confidence for a brand with few images.

### 11.5 Public service and product systems

- GOV.UK design principles (first published 3 April 2012 by the Government Digital Service; last updated 2 April 2025). Eleven principles: "Start with user needs"; "Do less"; "Design with data"; "Do the hard work to make it simple"; "Iterate. Then iterate again"; "This is for everyone"; "Understand context"; "Build digital services, not websites"; "Be consistent, not uniform"; "Make things open: it makes things better"; "Minimise environmental impact" (the eleventh, added in 2025). Forbids: building what users did not need, duplicating what another department does, decisions by opinion. Enforcement: service assessments against the 14-point Service Standard before a service can go live; the GOV.UK Design System supplies the components. GOV.UK won the Design Museum's Design of the Year in 2013. Borrow: "do less" and "design with data" for a hotel group's booking flow, within the testing limits stated in section 4.9.
- Shopify Polaris (2017): began as a published design system with content guidelines; as of October 2026 shopify.dev describes it as Shopify's unified UI framework built on web components, used by every app surface so third-party apps look native in the admin. The earlier experience values survive only in archived copies (27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.11), so the house quotes no principle list. Enforcement is technical: the framework is the rule.
- IBM: Eliot Noyes as consultant director of design (1956), Paul Rand's logo (1956; the eight-bar version 1972), Thomas Watson Jr's lecture "Good Design Is Good Business" (Wharton, 1973). The modern IBM Design Language, IBM Plex (2017) and Carbon, IBM's open-source design system, continue the Rand lineage; IBM's design programme under Phil Gilbert in the 2010s built Enterprise Design Thinking (ibm.com/design/thinking): three principles (a focus on user outcomes, restless reinvention, diverse empowered teams), the Loop (observe, reflect, make) and three Keys (Hills, the outcome statements that frame a release; Playbacks, the scheduled reviews; Sponsor Users, real users on the team), taught to thousands of employees as one process. Forbids: outputs judged without a user outcome. Enforcement: Playbacks and the chief executive's sponsorship. Borrow: Hills as the format for a client's campaign objectives.
- Airbnb Design Language System (Karri Saarinen, "Building a Visual Language", 2016): the team printed every screen and pinned them up to see the inconsistency, then built a component library around four principles: unified, universal, iconic, conversational. Descendants: react-sketchapp and Lottie (both 2017); see 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.10. Borrow: the wall of printed screens as the audit that starts any system.
- Stripe: the documentation as product (section 4.13); six operating principles, as stripe.com/jobs/culture lists them: Users first; Create with craft and beauty; Move with urgency and focus; Collaborate egolessly; Stay curious; Obsess over talent. Forbids: ego, slowness, uncrafted work. Enforcement: hiring and review (Katie Dill's weekly product reviews, section 4.10). Borrow: "Create with craft and beauty" as a written review criterion. Stripe Press is the brand's long-form voice.
- Linear: the Linear Method's eight principles: build for the creators; purpose-built; create momentum, don't sprint; meaningful direction; aim for clarity; say no to busy work; simple first, then powerful; decide and move on. The quality bar and the theme generator are in section 4.9. Forbids: busy work, A/B-test indecision, the unfinished release. Enforcement: the quality bar, held by the founders.
- Vercel Geist (section 4.13). Forbids: off-system colour and type. Enforcement: the components themselves.
- Atlassian: the Atlassian Design System (atlassian.design) with a brand personality stated as three traits: "Bold", "Optimistic" and "Practical, with a wink". Forbids: a voice that is timid, gloomy or ornamental. Enforcement: tokens and components across Jira and Confluence.
- Uber Base: after the 2016 "bits and atoms" rebrand was widely criticised, the 2018 rebrand (with Wolff Olins) returned to a plain wordmark set in the custom Uber Move typeface and organised everything under Base, a design system whose React library (Base Web) was open-sourced. No published principle list is known as of October 2026. Lesson: a system can repair an identity mistake within two years.
- Spotify Encore: a "family" of design systems with shared foundations and local systems per platform and team, described on spotify.design; no published principle list is known as of October 2026. The method answers the failure of one monolithic system across a large company.

### 11.6 Frameworks for thinking

- Christopher Alexander, "A Pattern Language" (1977, with Sara Ishikawa, Murray Silverstein and others): 253 patterns from regions to window seats, each with a name, a context, a problem, a solution and links to larger and smaller patterns. Software descendants: "Design Patterns" (Gamma, Helm, Johnson and Vlissides, 1994), Ward Cunningham's WikiWikiWeb (1995, built to host the Portland Pattern Repository), Jenifer Tidwell's "Designing Interfaces" (2005), and every component library since. Alexander's OOPSLA keynote (1996) told programmers they had borrowed the format and missed the moral aim, the quality without a name. Borrow: write every Mask360 pattern with context and problem first.
- Jesse James Garrett, the five planes (section 4.5): strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface. Forbids: surface decisions that contradict strategy. Enforcement: critique runs bottom-up.
- Jakob Nielsen's ten heuristics (developed with Rolf Molich in 1990, refined in 1994 from a factor analysis of 249 usability problems, wording updated 2020): visibility of system status; match between the system and the real world; user control and freedom; consistency and standards; error prevention; "recognition rather than recall"; flexibility and efficiency of use; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors; help and documentation. Enforcement: heuristic evaluation by three to five reviewers. Borrow: run them on every client site before launch.

### 11.7 The Mask360 rule set as a worked example

The design-taste skill is itself a principle set and can be dissected with the same seven questions without restating it (27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.13, treats it as a live set). Origin: Kaavish Ramchandani's house taste written down for every build. Form: a taste layer (design-taste sections 1 to 6), a house layer of tokens and prohibitions (section 7), and an enforcement layer: the seven tests at the end of the process (section 4, step 7) and the one-line verdict format (section 8). Forbids: each cheap tell (section 3) is a prohibition with a reason. Enforcement: the reviewer asks the tests in order; a single failure returns the work. Aged: the currency list (references/currency-2026.md) is the revision mechanism, dated and rewritten. What a client can borrow from its structure: tokens plus prohibitions plus tests, which is the shape section 12 produces.

### 11.8 What the durable sets share

Every set in this section that lasted was derived from observing finished work: Rams after twenty years of Braun products, Nielsen from 249 observed problems, Thomas and Johnston from fifty years of Disney practice, Murch from decades of cutting, Coats from what she learned on Pixar films, GOV.UK from an alpha and then thirteen years of revision. The sets that failed (Metro on phones, the 2016 Uber identity) were declared ahead of the work. The lesson drives section 12: derive the client's principles from twenty artefacts that already feel right.

### 11.9 Living and editorial identity systems

- Studio Dumbar's living identity (section 5.2; 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.12). Origin: identities that must move on screens before they are printed. Principles, reconstructed from the studio's case studies: the mark is a behaviour; the behaviour is coded as a tool; the client receives the tool. Forbids: motion without a stated rule. Enforcement: the tool's parameters, which bound what anyone can make with it. Aged: DEMO (from 2019) shows the method scaling to a city's screens. Borrow: hand a hotel group a generator (the mark's behaviour, the type scale, two colours, one motion) that its own team can run for years.
- Bureau Borsche's editorial system for Zeit Magazin (section 2.17). Principles, reconstructed: a fixed format (masthead, one image or one typographic statement, one line) plus one planned break per issue. Forbids: drift outside the format between breaks. Enforcement: the art director's weekly cover. Aged: the format has carried the magazine for more than a decade. Borrow: the content calendar as a format with one scheduled break a week (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 6.12).
- Both are Gerstner's programme (section 11.2) in production.

## 12. Deriving a principle set and a system for a client in a day

### 12.1 The day, step by step

The 90-minute version is 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, section 7.14. Inputs: the founder or brand head for two sessions (09:00 for step 1, 16:30 for step 8), a wall, a printer, the client's existing assets, and the reference board method from design-taste (section 4).

1. 09:00 to 10:30, collect twenty finished artefacts that feel right, with the founder choosing the ten from the client's own history (a menu, a suite, a necklace, a campaign image, a letter) and ten from outside the category (design-taste, references/worlds.md, for the category's references). Printed, pinned, numbered 1 to 20. Finished artefacts only, because principles derived from mood boards describe wishes (section 11.8).
2. 10:30 to 11:30, name what they share. For each artefact write three observed properties in plain words ("one material per surface", "no people in the frame", "type smaller than expected"). Count the repeats; a property seen in eight or more artefacts is a candidate.
3. 11:30 to 12:30, write the candidates as prohibitions. "Never show more than one material per surface" forbids something and can be checked; "authentic materials" cannot. Aim for twelve to fifteen.
4. 13:30 to 14:30, test each against a counterexample. Find an artefact the client admires that breaks the candidate. If the counterexample is better than the rule, the rule is wrong or needs a stated exception; delete or rewrite.
5. 14:30 to 15:00, cut to five to seven. Seven is the ceiling because a reviewer must hold the set in mind during a review (George Miller's 1956 "seven, plus or minus two" is the usual reference; Nelson Cowan's 2001 review puts working memory nearer four chunks, which argues for five).
6. 15:00 to 16:00, write each principle three ways: the one line (the prohibition), the three lines (the prohibition, the reason, the exception), and attach a reference image (the strongest of the twenty) and a token (the measurable value that implements it: a hex, a ratio, a duration, a type size, a crop rule).
7. 16:00 to 16:30, write the enforcement test for each: the single yes or no question a reviewer asks of any new artefact.
8. 16:30 to 17:30, present to the founder with the wall; the founder may cut but not add (additions on the day are aspiration).
9. 17:30 to 18:00, publish: one page, five to seven principles, each with its image, token and test; file it with the brand guidelines (18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 6) and the token set (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md).
10. Day 90, revisit: list every artefact rejected under each principle in ninety days. A principle that rejected nothing is dead weight; one that rejected good work needs its exception rewritten.

Facilitation rules: the founder breaks ties; anything proposed as an addition after step 3 is parked as a day-90 candidate; a principle that two people in the room cannot test the same way is rewritten on the spot.

## Terms introduced

- 180-degree line: the axis between two subjects that the camera must not cross if screen direction is to hold.
- 3 percent approach: Abloh's rule of changing an existing design by about 3 percent so it reads as new and stays recognisable.
- Affordance: what an object's form permits a person to do (Gibson, popularised by Norman).
- Agate: the smallest newspaper text size, about 5.5 point, used for listings and tables.
- Animatic: storyboard frames cut to sound as a timed film before the shoot.
- Arrival sequence: the ordered thresholds of shade, water and light a hotel guest passes before reaching a room (Kerry Hill).
- Atomic design: Brad Frost's method of building interfaces from atoms, molecules and organisms up to pages.
- Average shot length (ASL): a film's running time divided by its number of shots; a measurable pace value.
- Bi-script mark: a symbol built to read in two scripts at once, as the rupee sign joins Devanagari "ra" and Latin "R".
- Braintrust: Pixar's group of peers who give a director candid notes that carry no authority.
- Conjunct: a Devanagari consonant cluster fused or stacked into one glyph.
- Contextual forms: the isolated, initial, medial and final shapes of an Arabic letter, selected by the OpenType features isol, init, medi and fina.
- Contrast and affinity: Bruce Block's principle that visual components gain intensity through contrast and calm through similarity.
- Control characters: the few glyphs (H, O, n, o) drawn first to fix a typeface's stems, rounds and proportions.
- Corner portrait: Irving Penn's 1948 set-up of two flats in a narrow angle that leaves the sitter nowhere to lean.
- Crit: the critique of a finished artefact by people who can make artefacts (Natasha Jen).
- Dailies: the daily screening of the previous day's footage or animation for review.
- Day-90 review: the revisit of a client principle set after ninety days, counting what each principle rejected.
- Design token: a named, platform-neutral value (colour, size, duration) that implements a design decision in code.
- Display mode: one of the surfaces (inline card or carousel, fullscreen, picture-in-picture) in which an app renders inside ChatGPT.
- E-konte: the Japanese storyboard; Miyazaki draws his as the film's script.
- Eleven-star experience: Chesky's exercise of designing past five stars to the absurd, then working back to the feasible.
- Emptiness: Kenya Hara's term for a form left open to receive the viewer's meaning.
- Enforcement test: the single yes or no question a reviewer asks to check a principle on a new artefact.
- Ex-formation: Kenya Hara's method of making a familiar subject unknown again so it is seen fresh.
- Eye-trace: where the viewer's eye sits on the frame either side of a cut.
- Five planes: Garrett's strategy, scope, structure, skeleton and surface, decided in that order.
- Flow field: a grid of angles across a canvas that curves follow step by step (Tyler Hobbs).
- Found image: an existing image recontextualised as the work, as in Saville's "Unknown Pleasures".
- Functional and perceptual patterns: Kholmatova's split between components and the system's felt qualities.
- Haptic design: design that starts from how a thing feels in the hand (Hara's 2004 exhibition).
- Harakat: the optional Arabic vowel marks written above and below the line.
- Heuristic evaluation: an inspection in which several reviewers judge an interface against a set of heuristics.
- Hinting: instructions in a font that snap outlines to the pixel grid at small sizes.
- Hit area: the touchable or clickable region of a control, often larger than its visible shape.
- House codes: the fixed vocabulary a maison owns (materials, motifs, colours, silhouettes, objects) that each creative director recombines.
- HSL: hue, saturation and lightness, a simple cylindrical transform of RGB that is not perceptually uniform.
- Ink trap: a notch cut into a letter's stroke junction so ink spread fills it to a clean corner.
- Intrecciato: Bottega Veneta's woven-leather technique.
- Kashida: the lengthening of a connecting stroke in Arabic (tatweel), used to justify a line.
- Keys (IBM): Hills (outcome statements that frame a release), Playbacks (scheduled reviews) and Sponsor Users (real users on the team).
- Kundan: the setting of uncut stones in refined gold foil, a Rajasthani court technique.
- Lam-alef: the required Arabic ligature formed when lam is followed by alef.
- Latent space: a model's internal map of meaning, in which nearby points produce similar outputs.
- LCH: a colour space of lightness, chroma and hue designed to be perceptually uniform.
- LED volume: a stage surrounded by LED walls showing real-time backgrounds for in-camera visual effects.
- Living identity: an identity defined as coded behaviours (motion, response, variation) handed to the client as a tool.
- Long Nose of Innovation: Buxton's observation that technologies take about 20 years from invention to market.
- Long-form generative art: an algorithm released to produce hundreds of outputs, each of which must be acceptable.
- Lota test: asking which object in the client's culture has been perfected by use, after the Eameses' "India Report".
- Ma: the Japanese idea of meaningful interval; in Miyazaki, the pause in which the audience feels the world.
- Machine imaginaire: Vera Molnar's practice of writing rules as if for a machine and executing them by hand.
- Magic hour: the short window around sunrise or sunset when light is soft and warm.
- Match cut: a cut that joins two shots through a shared shape or movement.
- Matra: a Devanagari vowel sign, some of which are written above the headline.
- Meenakari: enamel work on gold, a Rajasthani jewellery technique.
- Mental model diagram: Indi Young's map of a person's reasoning with product features aligned beneath it.
- Motivated key light: a main light justified by a visible source in the scene, such as a window or lamp.
- Norman door: a door whose form signals the wrong action, used as the test of poor signifiers.
- Notes Day: Pixar's 2013 day of closure in which employees worked on fixing the company's own problems.
- OKLCH: the polar form of the Oklab colour space, more uniform in hue than LCH.
- One-solution rule: Rand's practice of presenting a single answer with its reasoning.
- Onliness statement: Neumeier's positioning test, "our brand is the only ___ that ___".
- Opportunity solution tree: Torres's map from an outcome to opportunities, solutions and assumption tests.
- Optical size: a version of a typeface drawn for a specific size range.
- Optimistic update: an interface that shows the result of an action before the server confirms it.
- Overshoot: the amount by which round letters extend past the baseline and x-height so they look as tall as flat ones.
- Pattern language: Alexander's format of named patterns, each with context, problem, solution and links.
- Persona: Cooper's behavioural archetype with goals, built from interviews to settle arguments about the user.
- Pixels per em: the number of device pixels across a font's em square at a given size.
- Planimetric frame: Bordwell's term for a camera square to a back wall with figures arranged like a frieze.
- Play principle: Rand's idea, after Huizinga, that work made as a game inside rules carries life.
- Polki: uncut diamonds, traditionally set in kundan.
- Preliminary course (Vorkurs): the Bauhaus foundation year in material, form and colour before specialism.
- Premier: the head of a couture atelier, who holds the craft authority in fittings.
- Previsualisation: a rough animated version of a sequence made before it is shot.
- Procrastiworking: Hische's term for the work done while avoiding assigned work, read as a clue to a vocation.
- Product trio: Torres's product manager, designer and engineer who interview customers together weekly.
- Readymade: an existing object made into a work by recontextualising it (Duchamp, then Abloh).
- Rostrum camera: a camera fixed above a flat table for frame-by-frame filming of artwork.
- Rule of Six: Murch's ranked, weighted criteria for a cut: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, two-dimensional plane, three-dimensional space.
- Serious play: Scher's working state, in which play is pursued with full seriousness of intent (2008 TED talk).
- Shepard tone: an auditory illusion of a pitch that seems to rise forever.
- Shirorekha: the headline from which Devanagari letters hang.
- Sidebearing: the space built into each side of a glyph.
- Signifier: Norman's term for the cue that shows where and how to act.
- Silver retention: a photochemical process that keeps silver in the print for deeper blacks and lower saturation.
- Single symbol method: Bass's discipline of carrying a film or company in one device, then varying it.
- Site of the Day (SOTD): Awwwards' daily award for the highest jury score, which can close early with at least 10 PRO (validated professional) votes.
- Sketch: Processing's name for a program, framing code as drawing.
- State machine: in Rive, a graph of animation states and the input-driven transitions between them.
- Story spine: Pixar rule 4's structure: once upon a time, every day, one day, because of that, until finally.
- Styleframe: a still frame finished to final quality and signed off before any animation.
- Three-level check: Vignelli's semantics, syntactics and pragmatics (meaning, structure, use) run in that order.
- Toile: a test garment in plain cotton used to fit a design before it is cut in the final cloth.
- Trompe l'oeil: a material or image made to pass for another; Blazy's material version is leather finished as denim.
- Ugly baby: Catmull's term for an early, unformed idea the organisation must protect until it is strong.
- Unigrid: Vignelli's single grid for all National Park Service publications (1977).
- Variable font: one font file holding a continuous design space along axes such as weight or width.
- Vernacular amplification: Scher's method of drawing a client's existing local visual language with authority.
- Vertical metrics: a font's ascender, descender and line-gap values (Typo and Win sets in the OS/2 table), with the USE_TYPO_METRICS flag telling applications which set to use.
- VistaVision: 35 mm film run horizontally through the camera so each frame spans eight perforations, about twice the standard negative area.
- Weekly disobedience: Borsche's one planned break of a fixed editorial format per issue.
- White sweep: a continuous backdrop curving from wall to floor so no horizon shows (Avedon).
