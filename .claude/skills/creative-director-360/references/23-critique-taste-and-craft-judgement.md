## 1. How taste forms

### 1.1 The four components

Taste in practice is four capacities, and a critique can fail at any of them.

| Component | What it is | How it fails in a crit | Trained by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure | The stock of filed references the eye compares against | The reviewer has no reference for the category and judges by personal habit | 00, section 3.1; section 1.2 here |
| Discrimination | Telling better from worse in a pair in about two seconds | "They are both fine" | Pair drills (00, section 3.2; 1.3 here) |
| Articulation | Naming the property, the value and the reference behind a judgement | "It feels off" | The articulation ladder (1.4) and the vocabulary (4.7) |
| Nerve | Saying the verdict aloud and removing what fails | Thirty soft notes in place of one hard one | Running crits; killing work (section 8) |

### 1.2 Exposure: volume and quality

Volume targets live in 00, section 3.1 (about 200 references a week, 10,000 a year). Quality matters as much as volume, because the eye calibrates to its diet.

| Rank | Source | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The object in hand or the space in person: a printed Gentlewoman, an Aesop bottle, a room at The Postcard Goa, the Louvre Abu Dhabi dome | Scale, material, light and sound survive; a screen flattens all four |
| 2 | The live primary artefact at full quality: the site on a calibrated display at 100 percent, the film at full resolution with sound | Motion and interaction only exist live; a GIF of a site is a different object |
| 3 | The maker's own case study (Pentagram, Collins, Koto, Studio Dumbar pages) | Shows the system, often the rejected routes and the constraints |
| 4 | Edited aggregators: Fonts In Use, Brand New, It's Nice That, Awwwards Sites of the Day (its daily award to the top jury-scored site), D&AD archive | An editor or a jury filtered it; the verdict is attached |
| 5 | Algorithmic feeds: Pinterest, Instagram Explore, Dribbble Popular, Behance | Ranked by engagement, which rewards the peaking wave (design-taste, references/currency-2026.md) |
| 6 | Screenshots of screenshots and generated mood images | No provenance, compressed, and trains the eye on the model default (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 5) |

House rule: at least 70 percent of weekly exposure from ranks 1 to 4, logged. Reason: ranks 5 and 6 teach what is popular this month, and a critique built on them can only say "on trend" or "off trend".

### 1.3 Discrimination: pairs that isolate one variable

A pair teaches only if one variable differs; two unrelated posters teach preference. Pair construction for the weekly 100 (target and logging in 00, section 3.2):

| Pair type | Variable isolated | Free source |
|---|---|---|
| Before and after identities | Mark, wordmark, system | Brand New's before and after images |
| Jury and crowd | Craft against spectacle | Awwwards jury score against the community score on the same site (12.2) |
| Pencil and shortlist | Excellence against merit | D&AD archive, same category and year |
| Same headline, two kerning passes | Spacing | Self-made in Figma: metric against optical against manual |
| Same shot, two grades | Skin, black level, saturation | Self-made in DaVinci Resolve from one clip |
| Same scene, two cuts | Cut point, hold length | Self-made from one stringout (every take of a scene laid end to end; 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 5.4) |
| Same copy, two edits | Adjectives, length, rhythm | Self-made: a brand's About page against its sentence-by-sentence edit |

### 1.4 Articulation: the four-rung ladder

Every judgement can be said at four levels. Direction a team can act on starts at rung 3.

1. Feeling: "it feels off". Useless to the maker.
2. Location: "the headline". Tells the maker where and leaves the what unsaid.
3. Mechanism: "the headline and the subhead are 32 and 28px, too close to read as two levels". Names the property.
4. Fix with reference: "take the subhead to 18px at weight 400; see the size jumps on The Gentlewoman's contents page". Names the value and the evidence.

The drill: every note the CD (creative director) writes for a week is tagged with its rung; any note below 3 is rewritten before it is sent.

### 1.5 The Ira Glass gap in a team

The gap itself (taste ahead of ability, closed by volume under deadline) is described in 00, section 3.4. In a studio the CD meets it in two directions, and the cure differs.

| Condition | How it shows in work | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| Taste ahead of hands | Strong references, weak execution; the maker is the harshest critic of their own work; slow output | Volume with short deadlines; copywork to measurement (00, section 3.6); fix one craft variable per week |
| Hands ahead of taste | Fluent software, fast, polished, generic; every project looks like the last good Dribbble shot | Exposure from ranks 1 to 4; pair drills; a reference board approved before any build |

The second condition is more dangerous in 2026, because generative tools give everyone hands. A CD who praises polish without asking for the idea and the reference trains the second condition.

### 1.6 Six thinkers and the critique question each gives

| Thinker | Work | The claim | The question it adds to a crit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paul Graham | "Taste for Makers", February 2002 | Taste can be studied because good and bad design exist; he lists 14 properties, from "Good design is simple" and "Good design solves the right problem" to "Good design is redesign", "Good design can copy" and "Good design is often daring" | Which of the 14 does this fail? Is this solving the brief's problem or a more comfortable one? |
| Dieter Rams | Ten principles of good design (formulated from the late 1970s; published by Vitsoe), Less but Better (1995) | Good design is innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough down to the last detail, environmentally friendly, and "as little design as possible" | Is anything here present for the designer's benefit? Is the last detail as considered as the first? |
| Michael Bierut | Pentagram partner from 1990, part-time consulting partner from early 2025 (as of October 2026); Seventy-Nine Short Essays on Design (2007); How to (2015); co-founded Design Observer in 2003 with Jessica Helfand, Rick Poynor and William Drenttel | Design writing as public criticism; work shown with its brief, constraints and rejected options | Can the maker explain this decision in plain words to a non-designer, and does the explanation come from the content? |
| Stefan Sagmeister | Beauty, with Jessica Walsh (2018), shown at the MAK Vienna and the Museum Angewandte Kunst Frankfurt between 2018 and 2020 | Beautiful forms "are function themselves"; beauty makes objects, buildings and strategies more effective | Did "clean" leave beauty on the table? Is this merely correct? |
| Kenya Hara | Art director of Muji since 2001 (as of October 2026); Designing Design (2007); White (2010); Ex-formation (Lars Müller, 2015) | Emptiness is a receptive state that the viewer fills, distinct from simplicity, which is a reduction | Does the empty space receive the viewer, or is it content that is missing? |
| John Berger | Ways of Seeing, BBC Two, four 30-minute films, January 1972, produced by Mike Dibb; book, Penguin, 1972 | "Seeing comes before words"; publicity sells the spectator an envied future self; the nude and the gaze | Whom is this image asking the viewer to envy, and is that the brand's register? Who is looking at whom? |

The house reads Graham and Rams for structure, Sagmeister as the counterweight when restraint turns sterile, Hara for the reason empty space reads as luxury, and Berger before every luxury campaign, because the episode on publicity describes the mechanism the campaign uses.

Graham's 14 as crit questions: simple (what can go?); timeless (will it hold in 2036?); solves the right problem (is this the brief's problem?); suggestive (does it leave the viewer room to complete it?); often slightly funny (is there wit anywhere?); hard (where did the effort go?); looks easy (is the effort hidden?); uses symmetry (is repetition doing structural work?); resembles nature (does it borrow a model that already works in the world?); is redesign (how many versions came before this one?); can copy (whose solution did it learn from, openly?); often strange (is anything here unexpected?); happens in chunks (which moment or movement does it belong to?); often daring (what risk did it take?).

Hara inherited a Muji whose art direction Ikko Tanaka set at its founding in 1980. Muji aims at the customer's rational "this will do" (kore de ii) over the craving "this is what I want" (kore ga ii); the Horizon campaign (2003, shot at the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia and on the Mongolian grassland) makes that emptiness an image; his exhibitions RE-DESIGN (2000) and HAPTIC (2004) treat everyday objects as subjects for redesign and touch. Ex-formation (Hara's term for making the familiar unknown again, the reverse of adding information) gives a crit question: does the work make the viewer see a familiar thing as if for the first time?

Rams's set and the other durable principle sets, dissected: 28-masters-methods-and-principle-systems.md, section 11. The cognition behind expert judgement: 27-how-the-best-designers-think.md, sections 2 and 11.

### 1.7 History as a critique instrument

01-history-and-movements.md gives the lineages. In a crit, history does three jobs:

- Naming the lineage: every piece descends from something. A maker who can name it ("Müller-Brockmann's Musica Viva posters via Experimental Jetset") is borrowing; a maker who cannot is copying the latest copy.
- Separating homage from pastiche: homage takes one principle and applies it to new content; pastiche takes the surface. The test: remove the borrowed surface; if nothing of the idea remains, it was pastiche.
- Dating the work: knowing when a look first appeared tells you whether it is rising, peaking or a revival (section 9).

### 1.8 Copywork in critique

Copywork method and targets: 00, section 3.6. Its critique use: when a piece fails and the cause is unclear, rebuild the reference it borrowed from at the same size, overlay at 50 percent opacity, and measure. The deviation is the note. A maker who has rebuilt a Gentlewoman spread to within 2px never again sets captions 4px from an image edge.

### 1.9 The reference library discipline

Tools (Are.na for connected thinking, Savee for fast visual boards, Cosmos for discovery with an AI-image filter), their plans and the house filing schema are in 00, section 3.7. The annotation rule is what makes a library usable in critique. Every block carries four lines: Reference (source, page, date viewed, device and width); Borrowed (the exact property and its value, for example "outer margin ratio; photographs cropped at the horizon line; small captions set below images"); Not borrowed (what to avoid and why, for example "the palette, too close to the client's competitor"); For (client, project, page).

Folder structure, whatever the tool: `clients/<client>/<project>/` for project boards; `worlds/<category>/` mirroring design-taste references/worlds.md; `craft/<discipline>/<technique>/` (for example `craft/type/hanging-punctuation/`); `dead/` for looks that have peaked; `pairs/` for discrimination drills with the verdict recorded. A reference with no "Borrowed" line is a mood and cannot be briefed; the CD returns it.

### 1.10 The daily eye practice

Twenty minutes, before email, every working day:

1. Five minutes: one pair, two-second call, then the reason in one sentence at rung 3 (1.4).
2. Five minutes: one reference filed with the full annotation (1.9).
3. Five minutes: one crit of something in the wild in the house format (a hoarding on the Western Express Highway, a Sheikh Zayed Road billboard, a restaurant menu, an app onboarding). Verdict, one thing working, one thing not, the fix.
4. Five minutes: one craft detail rebuilt (kern one word, match one easing, grade one frame).

Weekly: the Friday crit (00, section 3.8). Monthly: one visit to primary artefacts (in Mumbai, the CSMVS museum (Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya) and the Kala Ghoda galleries; in Dubai, Alserkal Avenue and Jameel Arts Centre; in Abu Dhabi, the Louvre Abu Dhabi), with five annotated references from it.

### 1.12 When to trust the two-second call

Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, "Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree" (American Psychologist, 2009), agree that intuition is valid only where the environment is regular enough to predict and gives the expert fast, clear feedback. Applied: the CD's two-second call is reliable on craft and register (kerning, grade, hierarchy, tone), where the rules are stable and feedback arrives in every crit and press check. It is unreliable on predicting virality, sales or the winner of a test, where feedback is slow and noisy; those calls go to data (7.6). The evidence that trained eyes see differently: Calvin Nodine, Paul Locher and Elizabeth Krupinski (Leonardo, 1993) tracked eye movements and found art-trained viewers scanning the relations between compositional elements while untrained viewers fixated on individual objects.

### 1.13 The standard of taste

The client line "taste is subjective" has two classic answers. David Hume, "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757), holds that some judges are better than others: the true judge has "strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice", which maps onto the four components in 1.1 (comparison is exposure, delicacy is discrimination, practice builds articulation and nerve). His model of discrimination is the story from Don Quixote of Sancho Panza's two kinsmen, who taste a hogshead of wine and report, one a taste of leather, the other of iron; they are mocked until the cask is emptied and an old key on a leather thong lies at the bottom. A judgement is proved when its hidden cause is found, which is what a rung 3 note (1.4) does. Pierre Bourdieu, Distinction (1979; English 1984), answers from sociology: "taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier"; luxury taste is a social signal, so the CD designs to the audience's distinction codes (an old Delhi family, a first-generation Mumbai buyer, an Emirati family) whatever the CD personally prefers. House position: both hold, Hume for the craft verdict and Bourdieu for the register. Client script: "Taste here is a prediction about how your buyer will judge this, and we can test it", then run a test from 4.8.

## 2. What the expert eye sees

The novice fixates on objects and reacts to the whole; the expert scans the relations between them (Nodine, Locher and Krupinski, 1993; 1.12). The expert sees the whole in two seconds, forms a verdict, then runs a checking pass at three distances: the full piece at arm's length (hierarchy, weight, idea), 100 percent (alignment, spacing, colour), and 200 to 400 percent (kerning, edges, artefacts). Each item below is something the expert checks on that pass, with the threshold that separates fine from defect. The fixes and references are in section 3.

### 2.1 Typography

Detail in 03-typography.md, sections 4, 5, 6 and 8.
- Optical alignment: round letters (O, C, G, S) and pointed ones (A, V) placed on a mathematical edge look inset, because the eye judges an edge by its mass. On display type the left edge of a headline is checked against the body margin; round and pointed letters should hang slightly past it, and opening quotes hang fully.
- Kerning pairs: spacing adjustments between specific letter pairs (AV, To, Ty, LT, P full stop, r comma). Font kerning tables are drawn for text sizes; above about 32px the pairs need an optical or manual pass at final size.
- Rag shape: the profile of the ragged right edge. A good rag moves in and out softly; defects are the staircase (each line shorter than the last), the wedge, a single very short line, and more than two consecutive hyphenated lines.
- Rivers: channels of white running vertically through justified text where word spaces align. Seen best upside down or squinting.
- Leading mismatches: two blocks of the same role with different line spacing, display type set at body leading (1.4 and above on a 96px headline), or columns whose baselines do not align across a gutter.
- Faux styles: bold or italic synthesised by the software because the real weight or style is not loaded (outlines thickened, counters clogged), a slanted roman passed as italic, small caps made by shrinking capitals (strokes visibly thinner than the caps beside them).
- Wrong figures: proportional figures in price columns, old-style figures in UI tables, a hyphen where a minus sign belongs, the letter x for a multiplication sign, straight quotes, and inch marks used as apostrophes.
- Tracking on lowercase: positive letter-spacing on lowercase running text breaks word shapes. Capitals and small caps take positive tracking at small sizes; lowercase display usually wants negative.
- Runts, widows and orphans (03-typography.md, section 6.4, in Bringhurst's senses): a runt is a single word or part of a word alone on the last line; a widow is a paragraph's last line stranded at the top of a column, page or slide; an orphan is its first line stranded at the bottom.
- Measure (the length of a line of text, counted in characters): body text outside 45 to 75 characters per line, about 66 being ideal for a single column (Bringhurst, chapter 2), loses the eye at the return or breaks the rhythm. The weekly case is a desktop luxury site running paragraphs 120 to 140 characters wide; the fix is `max-width` in `ch` units, about 60 to 70ch.
- Size steps too close: two roles 2 to 4px apart (16 and 18px, 28 and 32px) read as a mistake. The house working rule is a ratio of at least 1.25 between adjacent levels, or a difference carried by weight, case or colour instead of size.

### 2.2 Colour

Detail in 04-colour.md.
- Temperature mismatch: a warm photograph on a cool grey interface, or neutrals with a blue cast beside a tungsten-lit interior. Checked with the eyedropper in OKLCH (a perceptually uniform colour space of lightness, chroma and hue): true neutrals sit at chroma near 0; tinted neutrals share one hue across the palette.
- Muddy mids: midtones that go grey-brown, usually from a gradient interpolated in sRGB (the standard web RGB colour space) between complementary hues, or from darkening a colour by overlaying black.
- Banding: visible steps in a smooth gradient, from 8-bit output across large areas or heavy compression.
- Contrast fails: text under 4.5:1 or large text and interface parts under 3:1 (WCAG 2.2, the W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines; numbers in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 8; method in 11-accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md, section 2). Pale grey captions for elegance fail first.
- Saturation creep: each round adds a little saturation because each version is judged against the last. Version 7 beside version 1 shows it.
- The accent used twice: an accent works because it is scarce. A second use halves the signal of the first. The house flame (#F53901) appears once per view.

### 2.3 Layout

Detail in 05-layout-grids-and-composition.md, sections 6, 7 and 8.
- Grid drift: elements 2 to 8px off their columns, usually from nudging. Checked with the column overlay on.
- Margin inconsistency: outer margins that differ between slides, pages or breakpoints; the eye compares margins across a turn of the page.
- Unintended alignment: two edges that almost align (1 to 4px apart) or touch tangentially. Near-misses read as error; edges either align exactly or separate by at least one grid unit.
- Visual weight imbalance: the darkest, largest or most saturated mass all on one side with nothing answering it. Seen with the squint test (4.8).
- The medium sizes: elements at 40 to 70 percent of the largest element's size, competing with it; design-taste, section 2, gives the scale-contrast principle.
- Three focal points: three elements of similar weight competing for the first look. One primary, at most one secondary.

### 2.4 UI

Detail in 06-ui-design-craft.md, sections 3, 13 and 22, and 09-interaction-design-and-ux-patterns.md, section 12.
- Radius mismatch: nested corners that are not concentric. Inner radius equals outer radius minus the padding between them (06, section 13).
- Inconsistent hover: some cards change background, some underline, some do nothing.
- 1px misalignment: icons a pixel off-centre in their buttons from odd-sized bounding boxes; elements blurred by a half-pixel transform.
- Focus ring absent: the outline removed with nothing in its place, failing WCAG 2.4.7 Focus Visible (Level AA; 11, section 5).
- Icon weight against type weight: a 2px icon stroke beside 300-weight text looks bolder than the word it labels; match stroke to the text's stem.
- State missing: no empty, loading, error, disabled or partial state designed; the developer invents them.
- Shadow stacking: shadows with different light directions, or a card with a shadow inside a card with a shadow.
- Density mismatch: one screen on a 4px rhythm with 32px rows, the next on 56px rows; the product feels assembled from two kits.

### 2.5 Motion

Detail in 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md, sections 3, 4 and 16.
- Timing inconsistency: durations chosen per element (180, 230, 310ms) in place of a scale of three or four tokens.
- Linear easing: constant velocity on a positional move reads mechanical; objects in the world accelerate and decelerate.
- Bounce and overshoot on text: letters that overshoot and settle disturb reading and read as toy-like on a luxury surface.
- Everything moving: more than one considered motion per view (design-taste, section 2, slowness).
- Parallax wobble: scroll-linked layers jittering by a pixel against each other, common when a smooth-scroll library and native scroll both drive the transform.
- Cuts off the beat: picture changes landing 2 to 4 frames from the musical beat. Editors often cut a frame or two before the beat so the cut is perceived on it.

### 2.6 Video

Detail in 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, 15-lighting-and-sound.md and 14-video-preproduction-and-cinematography.md, sections 8, 10 and 12.
- Light direction continuity breaks: the key light on the left in the wide and on the right in the close-up of the same moment.
- Eyeline errors: a character looks frame left in the single when the other person is frame right; the 180-degree line (the imaginary line between two subjects that the camera stays on one side of) crossed without motivation.
- The 30-degree jump: a cut between two shots of the same subject with less than about 30 degrees of camera angle change and similar size, read as a jump cut.
- Warp wobble: the rubbery, rolling distortion that Premiere's Warp Stabilizer adds when pushed too far.
- Orange skin: skin pushed off the skin tone indication line on the vectorscope (16, section 8.5), usually by a warm look applied to the whole frame.
- Crushed blacks: shadows clipped at 0 on the waveform, losing hair, suit weave and jewellery setting detail.
- Dissolve abuse: dissolves used as a default join. A dissolve means time passing or a connection of ideas; used everywhere it means nothing.
- Music at frame one: no breath before the score, which signals a template edit; on social, sound is often off anyway, so the first frame must carry the picture.
- Caption font: platform or editor default captions with heavy stroke and pop-in animation, inside a film with a typeface system.

### 2.7 Photography

Detail in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, sections 2 to 6.
- Flat light: no direction and no shadow modelling, from on-camera flash or open shade with no negative fill (a black flag that removes bounce light to deepen shadow).
- HDR (high dynamic range tone-mapping) and over-retouching are on design-taste's cheap list; the expert sees the mechanism: halos along high-contrast edges, grey highlights, bent door frames near a liquified waist, pores replaced by blur.
- Stock pose: hand on chin, pointing at a screen, laughing at nothing, the arms-crossed team photo.
- Wrong lens for the subject: a 24mm lens close to a face swells the nose; faces want about 85 to 135mm full-frame equivalent at a distance; architecture shot without a shift lens or correction shows converging verticals.

### 2.8 Print

Detail in 04-colour.md, section 10, 20-print-packaging-ooh-and-physical-production.md, sections 2, 3, 5 and 11, and 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 6.
- Wrong paper: gloss-coated stock for a hotel's letter, or a heavy uncoated sheet that swallows a photograph's shadows.
- Gloss: gloss lamination on a luxury object, which reflects light and shows fingerprints.
- Rich black mismatch: two rich blacks (black built from K plus other inks for density) side by side (K 100 beside C60 M40 Y40 K100) so one panel looks grey.
- Foil as default: gold foil on every element, and foil on hairlines finer than the finisher's stated minimum, which fills in.
- Misregistration: coloured fringes around small type knocked out of a four-colour background.

### 2.9 Web

Detail in 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, sections 3 and 5.
- Font flash: text rendering in a fallback face and then jumping when the web font arrives, shifting the layout.
- Layout shift: Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) above 0.1 (web.dev, Core Web Vitals, Google's loading, responsiveness and stability measures, as of October 2026).
- Slow hero: Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) above 2.5 seconds at the 75th percentile, usually an unoptimised hero video or image.
- Scroll jank: dropped frames while scrolling, and interactions slower than the 200ms Interaction to Next Paint (INP) threshold.
- The preloader: a percentage counter or logo animation that delays content the visitor came for.
- Cursor effects: custom cursors that lag behind the pointer, trailing blobs, and magnetic buttons that move away from the click.

### 2.10 Copy

Design-taste lists the copy tells (design-taste, section 3, copy); 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md gives the craft. The expert also sees the mechanism under them: claims with no fact attached; every sentence the same length; the brand describing itself in the third person; a promise in the headline with no proof in the next line; and lines that could sit under any competitor's logo.

### 2.12 Sound

Detail in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, sections 10 to 13, and 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md. Sound carries half of every film the CD approves; the ear runs the same three-distance pass (the whole mix, each stem, each cut).
- Loudness off target: integrated loudness in LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale, measured per ITU-R BS.1770) away from the platform target: EBU R 128 sets minus 23 LUFS for broadcast, and streaming platforms normalise to about minus 14 LUFS (values per platform in 15, section 13.4, and 26). A hot mix is turned down and loses its punch; a quiet one sounds thin beside the ad before it.
- Sync drift: sound against picture is detectable from about 45ms with sound leading and 125ms with sound lagging (ITU-R BT.1359-1; 02-perception-and-psychology.md, section 16). Check lips and hard sounds (a door, a clasp) in the last minute, where drift accumulates.
- Music cut mid-phrase: a music edit off a 4- or 8-bar boundary, so the ear hears the join.
- Dead air: room tone (the location's ambient sound with nobody speaking) missing under cuts, so the background drops to digital silence.
- Ambience jumping at picture cuts: the background changing exactly on the cut; the fix is a J cut or an L cut (sound leading or trailing the picture across the cut).
- Breaths and sibilance: voice-over breaths stripped entirely (robotic) or left loud (nervous); harsh s sounds left without a de-esser (a processor that tames sibilance).
- Recognisable library music: a track the audience has heard under ten other advertisers.
- No Foley (sound effects performed and recorded to picture) on product handling: without the clasp click, the cork, the fabric and the key card, the product feels weightless.

### 2.13 CGI and archviz

Real estate is a core Mask360 category and renders are critiqued weekly. 2.11 and 22 cover generated material; CGI (computer-generated imagery) and archviz (architectural visualisation) come from a 3D pipeline (13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md, section 8).
- Light that contradicts the place: sun direction disagreeing with the shadows, the stated time or the site's latitude (Mumbai about 19°N, Dubai about 25°N; compute the angle with a sun-path tool such as SunCalc); interior lights glowing at noon; a replaced sky that disagrees with the reflections in the glazing.
- Surfaces too perfect: hard 90-degree edges with no bevel to catch light; uniform roughness with no smudge or wear; repeated plant instances.
- Missing contact: no contact shadows or ambient occlusion (the soft darkening where surfaces meet), so furniture floats.
- Entourage (the people, cars and props added for scale and life) cloned: the same cut-out person on two balconies.
- Lens: ultra-wide interiors at 14 to 16mm that make a 2BHK read as a ballroom; the house keeps about 24mm full-frame equivalent or longer at about 1.2 to 1.6m eye height.
- Regulation: section 12 of RERA makes the promoter liable to buyers who suffer loss from a false statement in an advertisement or prospectus, so a render shows no amenity absent from the sanctioned plan, and the artist's impression line stays on every render.

References for light and restraint: MIR (Bergen) and Brick Visual (Budapest).

## 3. The craft tells catalogue

## 4. Critique methods

### 4.1 The studio crit structure

Six stages, in order, for a 30-minute crit of one piece. The order matters because judgement offered before observation anchors the room on the first opinion voiced. The lineage is Edmund Burke Feldman's model of art criticism (describe, analyse, interpret, judge), extended at both ends for a working studio.

| Stage | Minutes | Who speaks | What happens | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Silent look | 2 | Nobody | Work full size on the wall or screen; everyone forms a two-second verdict privately (00, section 3.8) | Explanation shaping perception |
| 1. Present | 4 | Maker | Brief in one sentence; the single idea; the three to five references and what was borrowed; the stage (concept, development, finish); the feedback wanted | Reviewers critiquing a different brief |
| 2. Clarify | 3 | Reviewers ask, maker answers | Questions of fact only: audience, size, medium, budget, mandatories | Opinions disguised as questions |
| 3. Observe | 5 | Reviewers | What the eye does: "first the image, then the price, then the logo"; no adjectives of value | Skipping to taste before agreeing what is there |
| 4. Interpret | 5 | Reviewers | What it means and feels like; the register; whether it carries the idea | Craft notes on an idea nobody has located |
| 5. Evaluate | 6 | Reviewers, CD last | Against the brief and the rubric (section 13); idea first, craft second; each problem with a severity (4.5) | A heap of equal-weight nitpicks |
| 6. Suggest and decide | 5 | Reviewers suggest directions with references; CD decides aloud | Actions written with owner and date | Leaving with three versions of the decision |

The CD speaks last in stages 3 to 5, because a senior's first remark ends independent observation in the room (anchoring: 02-perception-and-psychology.md, section 12).

### 4.2 The Pixar braintrust rules

Ed Catmull's braintrust (described in "How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity", Harvard Business Review, September 2008, and in Creativity, Inc., 2014, with Amy Wallace) is a group of experienced filmmakers who watch a film in progress and give notes. The rules that make it work:

1. No authority. The braintrust advises; the director decides whether and how to act on a note. Removing power from the room is what makes candour safe.
2. Candour about the work. Notes address the film and its problems, and the people in the room are peers who have made films themselves.
3. Notes diagnose. A good note says what is wrong, missing, unclear or unbelievable; the solution belongs to the maker, who knows the film better than any reviewer.
4. Early work is expected to be weak. Catmull's line "early on, all of our movies suck" gives permission to show work before it is defensible.
5. Repeated screenings. Inside Out went through seven to eight story screenings for the braintrust (12.4).

Mask360's adaptation (monthly, two biggest live jobs, one outside practitioner) is in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 10.3.

### 4.3 Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process

Devised by the choreographer Liz Lerman in 1990 (lizlerman.com, read October 2026). Three roles: the artist (maker), the responders, and a facilitator who runs each step and helps people frame questions. Four steps:

| Step | What happens | Craft of the step |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Statements of meaning | Responders say what was meaningful, evocative, striking or exciting | Specific: "the 4-second hold on the empty courtyard told me this hotel is quiet" |
| 2. Artist as questioner | The maker asks the questions they want answered; responders answer on topic and may give opinions in answer | The maker controls the agenda: "does the price slide feel abrupt?" |
| 3. Neutral questions | Responders ask questions with no opinion inside | "What led you to the 4:5 crop?" is neutral; "why is it so tight?" carries a verdict |
| 4. Permissioned opinions | Responders name the topic and ask permission: "I have an opinion about the ending; would you like it?" | The maker may decline |

Optional add-ons on the same page: "What's your next step?", a separate subject-matter discussion, and "working the work" (trying changes live). When the house uses it: early-stage work, juniors' first major pieces, cross-discipline reviews (a film shown to designers), and any maker who has had a bruising crit. When it does not: final-stage QA (quality assurance), where the rubric and the checklists (section 5) are faster.

### 4.4 I like, I wish, what if

Three sentence-starters used in design-thinking teaching: "I like" (what works and should stay), "I wish" (what is missing or not working), "What if" (a possibility, offered as a question). Use with groups of six or more and with clients, because the form gives everyone a safe way in. Two rules: write the cards silently before anyone speaks, so the loudest voice does not set the agenda; and the CD converts every "I wish" into a problem statement before it becomes a task, because "I wish it were blue" is a solution looking for its problem.

### 4.5 Severity ratings

Jakob Nielsen's usability severity scale (Nielsen Norman Group, November 1994) rates problems 0 to 4 by frequency, impact and persistence: 0 not a problem, 1 cosmetic, 2 minor, 3 major, 4 catastrophe. The house craft adaptation:

| Rating | Meaning in creative work | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| S4 | Ship-blocker | Idea absent or off-brief; legal or regulatory miss (the RERA registration number, required in Indian project advertising by section 11(2) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016, missing; the Trakheesi permit, the Dubai property advertising permit, missing on a listing; 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 14.7); wrong brand mark; accessibility failure on a core path | Fix before anyone outside the studio sees it |
| S3 | Major | Hierarchy broken; the hero image wrong; a grade that turns skin orange; LCP over 2.5 seconds | Fix this round |
| S2 | Minor | Kerning pairs, a near-miss alignment, a caption font | Fix this round if time, else next |
| S1 | Polish | A hold two frames long, a rag that could be smoother | Batch into the finishing pass |
| S0 | Preference | "I would have used green" | Logged and labelled as preference; no action |

Every crit note carries its rating. The maker works S4 down; a round with an open S4 is not presented.

### 4.6 The idea problem before the craft problem

Design-taste, section 8, sets the order. The diagnostic, in sequence:

1. Can the maker say the idea in one sentence, as a feeling or a proposition?
2. Is it the brief's idea, answering the brief's problem (Graham: "solves the right problem")?
3. Is it specific: does it survive the cover-the-logo test (4.8)?
4. Would it survive a different execution? An idea that dies when the typeface changes was an execution.
5. Only now: craft, from section 2 and section 3.

The reason for the order is cost and sunk cost: polish applied to a dead idea makes it harder to kill (section 8.4), and an hour of kerning notes on a route that will be dropped wastes the maker's next day.

### 4.7 Critique vocabulary

| Term | Definition | How you see it | In a note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hierarchy | The order in which the eye takes in elements, set by size, weight, colour, position and space | Squint and list what you see first, second, third | "Hierarchy reads price, image, name; the brief wants name first" |
| Rhythm | The pattern of repetition and interval across a page, sequence or edit | Flick through pages or watch at double speed; feel the beats | "The rhythm is all quarter notes; give it one long hold" |
| Tension | A deliberate imbalance that holds attention: scale, crop, silence, an unexpected pairing (design-taste, section 1) | The one element you keep looking back at | "There is no tension; everything is correctly placed" |
| Resolution | The sense that the piece arrives and ends; tension released | The last frame, slide or element feels final | "The film stops without resolving; the last shot needs to land the idea" |
| Specificity | The degree to which a choice could belong only to this brand | Cover the logo | "This could be any Dubai tower; find the one fact only this one has" |
| Restraint | Choosing fewer elements, effects and words than the maker could | Count the devices in use | "Seven devices; restraint would be two" |
| Inevitability | The sense that no other solution was possible, which comes from the idea fitting the content | You cannot imagine it changed | "It feels arbitrary; nothing ties the colour to the place" |
| Currency | How current the work feels, read against the wave (section 9) | Where else you have seen the look this year | "Glass panels are peaking; this will date by 2027" |
| Coherence | Consistency of one system across touchpoints | Lay all pieces side by side | "The film and the site belong to two brands" |
| Register | The level of formality and the cultural tone the work speaks in | Read it aloud as the client's best customer would hear it | "The register is a fintech app; the brand is a 90-year-old jeweller" |
| Weight | Visual mass: how strongly an element pulls the eye, from size, darkness, saturation and density | Squint; blur the screen | "The logo carries more weight than the product" |
| Pace | Speed of information or cuts over time | Count cuts per 10 seconds or elements per scroll | "Pace is frantic for a hotel; halve the cuts" |
| Contrast | Difference between elements in size, weight, value, colour or texture | The grey test (4.8) for value | "Not enough size contrast between levels" |
| Negative space | The unoccupied area that shapes and isolates elements | Look at the gaps as shapes | "The negative space is leftover; draw the gaps as shapes" |

### 4.8 The perceptual tests

| Test | How | What it reveals | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-second test | Show the piece for two seconds, hide it, ask what was seen and what it was | Hierarchy and the single idea | The viewer names the primary element and the subject |
| Squint test | Squint or apply a 10 to 20px Gaussian blur | Weight distribution, focal points, balance | One dominant mass, an intended path |
| Cover the logo | Hide every brand mark | Specificity (design-taste, section 4, step 7) | A stranger names the brand or at least its world |
| Flip test | Mirror horizontally or turn upside down | Balance and alignment, freed from reading | Still balanced; misalignments jump out |
| Grey test | Desaturate to greyscale | Value contrast and hierarchy independent of hue; colour-blind legibility | Hierarchy survives in grey |
| Print it out | Print at real size (A4 for decks, 1:1 for posters, a crop at 100 percent for OOH, out-of-home advertising) and pin up | Scale, type size, margins, colour on paper | Body type readable at arm's length; nothing feels too big or too small |
| Phone at arm's length | View on a phone held at full arm's length, about 60cm, and at 1m from a wall-mounted screen | Mobile legibility; social thumbnails | Headline and product legible; idea reads in under 2 seconds |
| Thumbnail test | Shrink to about 120px wide | Whether the piece survives as a grid tile or search result | Shape and primary element still readable |
| Mute test (film) | Watch with sound off, then listen with picture off | Whether picture and sound each carry the story | Picture alone tells it; sound alone sets mood |
| Overnight test | Leave it; look first thing next morning before email | Fatigue blindness | The verdict holds |
| Other-room test | View from 3 to 5m away | OOH and signage legibility; poster scale | Headline and mark read at distance; for OOH, cap height at least the viewing distance divided by 360, and about the distance divided by 120 for impact (20, section 7.4) |
| In-feed test | Drop the post into a screen recording of the target customer's feed between 10 real posts, and check the 3:4 profile grid tile (the grid shape since January 2025; 26, section 2) | Stopping power and brand recognition in context | It stops the eye and reads as the brand before the handle is seen |
| Shelf test | Place the pack among the three nearest competitors at the distance a shopper stands from the shelf | Findability and price tier | Found first; read as the right price tier |
| In-situ test | Composite the OOH artwork onto a photograph of the actual site, by day and by night | Scale, surround, glare and legibility on site | Reads at the site's viewing distance in both lights |
| Competitor wall | The piece beside the three best category competitors and one best-in-world reference | Distinctiveness and relative craft | Distinct from all three and at least equal in craft to the best of them |
| Swap test (copy) | Replace the brand name in the line with a competitor's | Specificity of the claim | The line becomes false or absurd; if it is still true, it is rewritten |

### 4.9 Choosing the method

| Situation | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly studio review of live jobs | Studio crit (4.1) with severity | Fast, decisive, builds the eye |
| A large job stuck in development | Braintrust (4.2) | Peers without authority find structural problems |
| Junior's first big piece; cross-discipline review | Critical Response Process (4.3) | Keeps the maker in control and motivated |
| Workshop with a client team of 8 to 15 | I like, I wish, what if (4.4) | Everyone speaks; CD converts wishes into problems |
| Final QA before delivery | Checklists (section 5), rubric (section 13) | Completeness matters more than conversation |
| Mid-process check on one maker's work | Desk crit: one to one at the maker's screen, 10 to 15 minutes | Fast and private; keeps momentum |
| Group review of work in progress | Pin-up: work printed or projected at real size on the wall | Scale and sequence visible; everyone sees the same object |
| Final review before a client or an award entry | Jury: reviewers who did not see the process, scoring with the rubric | Fresh eyes counter habituation (4.11) |
| Many pieces, many reviewers | Gallery walk: silent written notes on cards beside each piece | A volume of notes without the loudest voice |
| Team or client in another city | Remote crit: shared at 100 percent, reviewers open the link on their own device, cameras on; for async rounds a recorded 3 to 5 minute walk-through | Everyone sees true size and colour; the recording is the record |

### 4.10 The review environment

Colour, type and motion verdicts made on an uncalibrated laptop, a projector or an iPhone with True Tone on are a weekly source of wrong notes and disputed sign-offs.

| Medium | Review condition | Reason and standard |
|---|---|---|
| Screen colour | Display calibrated to D65 (the daylight white point, about 6500K) at about 120 cd/m²; True Tone and Night Shift (Apple's features that shift the white point with ambient light or time of day) off | 04-colour.md, sections 4.4 and 4.5 |
| Print | ISO 3664:2009 viewing booth at D50 (about 5000K) and 2000 lux; office LED and window light excluded | The condition printers proof to; most print disputes start under the wrong light (04, section 10; 20, section 5) |
| Film | The grading reference monitor (Rec.709, the HD video colour standard, gamma 2.4, dim surround), then a phone at about 50 percent brightness | The monitor shows the master; the phone shows what most viewers see (16, section 8) |
| Web | The house test Android and an iPhone, with campaign links also opened inside the Instagram and WhatsApp WebViews | 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, section 14 |
| Social | Posted to a private test account and seen in the real app | The export window has no interface, no compression and no feed |
| OOH | Composited onto a photograph of the actual site at true scale | 20, section 7 |

Rule: every sign-off in the log names the device and the viewing condition.

### 4.11 Biases in the room

02-perception-and-psychology.md, sections 8.10 and 12.2, defines these biases from the user's side; in a crit they distort the reviewers.

| Bias | How it shows in a crit | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| Halo and the aesthetic-usability effect (Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura, CHI 1995) | The most polished route also wins on idea and usability | Show routes at equal fidelity |
| Order effects | The first or last route shown wins | Vary the order between sessions and say why each route sits where it does |
| Mere exposure and habituation (Robert Zajonc, 1968) | After 12 rounds the team likes what it has lived with and stops seeing defects | A cold reader who has not seen earlier rounds |
| IKEA effect (Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely, 2012) | Makers overvalue what they built | Makers speak last in the evaluate stage (4.1) |
| Anchoring | The first opinion or number voiced sets the range | Silent look and written verdicts before discussion (4.1, 4.4) |
| HiPPO (highest paid person's opinion; popularised by Avinash Kaushik) | The room converges on the most senior view | The CD speaks last; the client's senior speaks after the team has presented |
| Groupthink (Irving Janis, Victims of Groupthink, 1972) | Nobody voices the doubt everyone holds | One person holds the devil's advocate role, rotated per crit |
| Law of triviality (C. Northcote Parkinson, Parkinson's Law, 1957) | The committee approves the reactor in minutes and argues about the bike shed: 40 minutes on logo colour, two on the idea | An agenda that puts the idea first and timeboxes craft notes |

### 4.12 Critique upstream: brief crit, tissue session, pre-mortem

- Brief crit, six questions (the brief's anatomy is in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 2): one audience named as a person; one measurable objective; a single-minded proposition in one sentence; an insight stated as a tension; mandatories listed; budget and dates. A brief that fails two goes back to the client before work starts.
- Tissue session: rough routes (a scamp, a headline and a reference each) shown to the client before production, at equal fidelity, to kill cheaply.
- Pre-mortem (Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007): imagine it is six months after launch and the campaign has failed in public; everyone writes the reasons silently for 3 minutes, then reads them out. Seed the Mask360 failure modes: a festival or religious misread, an ASCI or UAE permit breach, meme risk, an AI disclosure backlash.

## 5. Self-critique checklists per deliverable

Run before any piece goes to the CD, and again before it goes to the client. Questions are numbered so a reviewer can cite them ("identity 14 fails"). The CD's own final gate is 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 17; the UI polish audit is 06-ui-design-craft.md, section 22; web launch QA is 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, section 15.

### 5.1 Identity

- Idea: 1. Can I say the idea behind the mark in one sentence? 2. Does it come from something true about the client (place, founder, material, method)? 3. Does it survive cover-the-name: would a stranger guess the category and the register? 4. Which three references did it borrow from, and what exactly?
- Mark: 5. Does it hold at 16px, in one colour, reversed, embroidered and etched? 6. Is there a small-size version where detail closes? 7. Are curves drawn, with no leftover nodes or wobble at 1,600 percent? 8. Is it optically centred in its own clear space?
- Wordmark and type: 9. Is the wordmark kerned by hand? 10. Do symbol and wordmark balance in weight? 11. Is the type family licensed for logo, web, app and broadcast use (03, section 16)? 12. Do the Arabic and Devanagari versions exist, designed together with the Latin?
- System: 13. Can the system make 20 different applications without new rules? 14. Is the colour specified in hex, RGB, CMYK per stock, Pantone and paint? 15. Is there one owned colour? 16. Is the grid or graphic device generative, or decoration?
- Applications: 17. Have I shown the worst case (a favicon, a cheque, a WhatsApp avatar, a hoarding at 40m)? 18. Do the mock-ups use real content? 19. Does it work on the client's real channels (Instagram grid, website header, signage)?
- Time: 20. Will this hold in 2029 (section 9.3)? 21. Is any element a trend that should live in campaigns instead? 22. What did I remove, and should one more thing go?

### 5.2 Deck

- Story: 1. Can the deck's argument be read from the slide titles alone? 2. Is there one idea per slide? 3. Is the client the hero, with their real hex on a #0A0A0A ground (design-taste, section 7)? 4. Does the first slide earn attention and the last slide ask for the decision?
- Content: 5. Is every claim backed by a number, a reference or an image? 6. Is the ratio about 70 visual to 30 text? 7. Have I cut every slide that exists to show effort? 8. Does each chart make one point, with the point as its title?
- Type and layout: 9. Are margins identical on every slide? 10. Are there no more than three type sizes in use? 11. Are there any runts, orphan bullets or text boxes that touch an edge? 12. Do images share one treatment and crop logic?
- Craft: 13. Do all images hold up at 100 percent on a 4K screen? 14. Are video embeds playing offline? 15. Is the PDF export under the client's email limit (often 10 to 25MB) with fonts embedded? 16. Have I checked spelling of the client's names, products and people?
- Delivery: 17. Can it be presented in 20 minutes with the remaining slides as appendix? 18. Does it pass the flick test at speed, with nothing jumping? 19. Would the client's CEO forward it without editing? 20. What is the single sentence the client should repeat after the meeting?

### 5.3 Website or microsite

- Idea and narrative: 1. Is there one idea, stated in the first viewport? 2. Is there one considered motion moment and everything else still? 3. Does the scroll have a beginning, a peak and an ending? 4. Does the page end with the same care as it starts?
- Performance: 5. LCP 2.5 seconds or less, INP 200ms or less, CLS 0.1 or less at the 75th percentile on a mid-range Android on 4G? 6. Hero media compressed (AVIF or WebP; video with a poster)? 7. Fonts subset and preloaded, with no layout jump on swap?
- Accessibility: 8. Text contrast 4.5:1, large text and UI 3:1? 9. Keyboard path complete with visible focus? 10. `prefers-reduced-motion` honoured? 11. Alt text written by a person, describing what matters?
- Craft: 12. Grid holds at 360, 768, 1280, 1440 and 1920px? 13. No near-miss alignments or drifting margins? 14. Real content at real lengths, including the longest Arabic or Hindi string? 15. Hover, focus, active and visited states consistent?
- Function: 16. Anchor links, back button, find-in-page and right-click work? 17. Forms validate inline and confirm success? 18. Open Graph image, favicon and 404 page designed? 19. Analytics events firing for the one action that matters? 20. Does it work with JavaScript slow or failing?
- Taste: 21. Cover the logo: whose site is this? 22. Is any word selling? 23. What would Awwwards' usability jurors mark down (12.2)?

### 5.4 App screen

- Purpose: 1. What is the one job of this screen, and is it the most visible thing? 2. Is the primary action reachable by thumb? 3. Can a first-time user explain the screen after five seconds?
- States: 4. Empty, loading, partial, error, offline, disabled and success all designed? 5. What happens with zero items, one item and 1,000? 6. With a 40 percent longer translated label? 7. At 200 percent text size?
- System: 8. Every value from a token (colour, type, space, radius, motion)? 9. Radii concentric? 10. One icon family, stroke matched to text? 11. Density consistent with sibling screens?
- Platform: 12. Respects iOS and Android conventions where users expect them (06, section 2)? 13. Safe areas, notch and home indicator clear? 14. Touch targets at least 24px, primary actions 44pt? 15. Haptics and motion only as feedback?
- Craft: 16. Pixel-aligned at 1x, 2x and 3x? 17. Dark mode designed in its own right and checked? 18. Copy: verbs on buttons, no jargon, sentence case? 19. Does the screen compare cleanly against the build at 2px tolerance (06, section 20)? 20. What can be removed?

### 5.5 Film

- Idea and structure: 1. Is the idea clear with sound off? 2. Does the first second earn the second (16, section 3.4)? 3. Is there one image the audience will remember tomorrow? 4. Does it resolve? 5. Is every shot doing work, and which one would I cut first?
- Picture: 6. Light direction consistent within scenes? 7. Eyelines and the 180-degree line respected? 8. No cuts between near-identical angles? 9. Skin on the skin tone line; blacks and highlights holding detail? 10. Product in focus wherever it is the subject?
- Edit: 11. Cuts land on or a frame before the beat where cut to music? 12. Dissolves only where they mean time or connection? 13. Holds long enough for the luxury pace (16, section 3.3)? 14. Cutdowns (30, 15, 6 seconds) each work on their own?
- Sound: 15. Opening breath before the score? 16. Dialogue clear over music? 17. Loudness on target per platform (15, section 13)? 18. Music licensed for every territory and term (15, section 12)?
- Delivery: 19. Captions in the brand face, inside safe zones, in every required language? 20. Every aspect ratio reframed by hand? 21. End frame on the master logo artwork, legible on a phone? 22. Generated shots checked frame by frame at the cuts (22, section 5.4)?

## 6. Giving feedback

### 6.1 The four rules

1. Specific: location, mechanism, value (rung 3 or 4 of the articulation ladder, 1.4). "The caption sits 24px from the image; take it to 8px so they pair."
2. Reference based: the reference opened on screen, with the thing to take named. References turn a taste argument into evidence (19, section 10.2).
3. One fix at a time: per round, the top three notes by severity, in order. Changes interact; thirty simultaneous notes produce thirty compromises and a maker who can no longer see the piece.
4. The why: every note carries its reason, so the maker can apply the principle next time without the CD.

The note format: `Where | What I see | Why it matters | Direction | Reference | Severity`.

### 6.2 To designers

- Name the level of the note first: "idea note", "system note", "craft note". The designer then knows how much to change.
- Ask before you tell when the designer is mid-level or above: "What were you going for with the second typeface?" often surfaces a reason worth keeping.
- Draw over the work only with permission and only to show a direction; never take the mouse. The designer's hand must make the fix, or the next file repeats the error.
- For juniors, one craft variable per week (kerning this week, margins next), with a reference rebuilt to measurement (1.8).
- Close with what to keep, specifically, so the redraw does not lose the good part.

### 6.3 To copywriters

- Read the line aloud in the room; ear catches what the eye forgives.
- Mark the line that could run under any competitor's logo and ask for the one fact only this client has.
- Ask for 20 versions of the line that matters, then choose together; never rewrite the writer's line in front of them, because the room then owns the line and the writer stops writing.
- Notes on register (too formal, too eager, wrong for an 80-year-old family jeweller) before notes on words.
- Word budgets as numbers: "the hero has seven words; the subline twelve".
- For Hindi, Arabic and Hinglish (Hindi and English mixed, in Latin script): notes come from a native-speaking reviewer, with the CD's note limited to rhythm and length (10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md).

### 6.4 To editors and colourists

The vocabulary is in 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md (sections 1, 3 and 8). Rules for notes:

- Structure before shots: the first pass of notes is about order, length and what the film says (16, section 1.6). Shot notes on a cut whose structure will change are wasted.
- Timecode on every note, written in the review tool against the frame (16, section 5.9).
- To the colourist, name the quality and the direction: skin (warmer, cleaner, less magenta), black level (lift to read the suit weave), highlight roll-off (softer on the marble), saturation (less in the greens), contrast (flatter mids). Point at the reference frame and the scope reading; "make it pop" is on the banned list (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 13.4).
- Watch the first assembly without music, then with (Murch's practice, 16, section 1.6).
- Respect the session: notes are given at the end of a pass or at agreed checkpoints, through one voice; several people talking at the grading monitor stall the session.

### 6.5 To photographers and directors of photography on set

The chain of voice on set is client to CD to photographer or director to talent. Breaking it confuses the talent and undermines the person running the floor.

| Say | Never say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The ring needs to read first in this frame; what would you change?" | "Use a longer lens" (unless asked) | Describe the outcome; the craft choice belongs to the photographer |
| "This frame is the one; let's do five more and move on" | "Just a few more" with no reason | Time on set costs light and money |
| "Can we see one with the light coming from the window side, as in the reference?" | A note shouted across the set to the model | Talent takes direction from one person |
| Notes to the photographer quietly at the monitor | Critique in front of the client or the crew | Authority on set is a working tool |
| "We have it. Thank you." when it is there | Continuing to shoot "for safety" past the point | Over-shooting dilutes the edit and tires talent |

Video village (the monitor station where director, CD and client watch takes) etiquette: one CD voice at the monitor, the client briefed before the day on when and how to give notes (between takes, to the CD), and the shot list as the arbiter of time.

### 6.6 To 3D and motion artists

- Notes by frame number and timecode, with the curve named: "the ease-out on the bottle lands hard at frame 46; soften the last 8 frames" or "hold 12 frames longer on the reveal at 24fps".
- Easing given as values when it matters (`cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1)`), or as a reference clip with its timecode (12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md, section 15).
- Approve at the cheapest stage: animatic (timed storyboard frames with sound), then playblast (a fast viewport render) or viewport capture, then lighting stills, then the final render. A note on timing after a final render costs render hours (13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md).
- Materials and light notes are given on stills, where they can be judged at rest (13 owns the pipeline).
- Ask for the graph editor view when a move feels wrong and nobody can say why; the curve shows it.

### 6.7 To AI tools: prompt revision as feedback

Prompting is art direction in text (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 3). Feedback to a model follows the same rules as feedback to a person, adapted:

- Change one variable per iteration (light, lens, crop, palette, surface) and keep the seed or reference fixed, so the effect of the change is visible.
- Describe the problem in the model's vocabulary: name the light setup, the lens and the film stock or grade; adjectives give the model nothing to hold.
- Feed references as images (style or omni references) where the tool allows, because a picture carries more than any sentence.
- Keep a log: prompt, seed, model and version, what changed, verdict. The log is the critique record and the disclosure record.
- Stop at three failed iterations on the same problem and change method (shoot a plate, composite, or redraw), because the model has reached the limit of what it knows (22, section 6).

When the CD reviews a team member's generated work, the review covers the tells (22, section 5) and the disclosure: which tool, which prompt, which rights position (22, section 7).

### 6.8 To clients: educating without condescension

- Lead with their business: "Your buyer at ₹12 crore has seen every render; the material close-ups are what will feel new to them."
- Show the reference and the reason in one breath; never "trust us" alone.
- Give the client the test and let them reach the verdict: hand them the cover-the-logo test or the phone at arm's length and let them see it.
- Use "we" and their vocabulary; drop studio jargon (kerning, LCP) unless they use it.
- One teaching point per meeting. The taste education is a programme over months (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 8.4).

### 6.9 Written feedback formats

- Frame.io (Adobe; V4 as of October 2026): frame-accurate comments and on-frame annotations, version stacking for side-by-side comparison, and a Premiere panel so comments land as markers in the edit (16, section 5.9). One consolidated round from one named approver.
- Figma comments: pinned to the frame, threaded, resolved when fixed, @-mentions to the owner; behaviour notes (timing, focus order, validation) go in Dev Mode (Figma's developer inspection mode) annotations, which travel with the frame to engineering (06-ui-design-craft.md, section 20).
- The feedback doc, for rounds that cover several deliverables:

```
Project | Round 2 | 2026-10-29 | Approver: <name> | Due: 2026-11-03
No Deliverable      Where              Note (what, why)                         Direction / reference          Sev  Owner  Status
1  Hero film 30s   00:00:04:12        Logo appears before the product; ...     Product first, see ref A        S3   Edit   Open
```

- WhatsApp, voice notes and red circles: in India and the Gulf most team and client feedback arrives this way. Colour, sharpness and type are never judged from a WhatsApp-forwarded file, because WhatsApp recompresses images and video even in its HD setting; masters travel as a Document or as a versioned link (Frame.io, Figma, Drive) with the version in the filename (`_v07`), and an approval counts only against a versioned link. Voice notes are transcribed into the feedback doc the same day, because a voice note is neither searchable nor a record; red-circle screenshots are rewritten in the note format with a severity. One thread per project, and every reply states the decision ("changed in v08, link").

### 6.10 The feedback sandwich problem

The sandwich (praise, criticism, praise) was popularised in management practice by Mary Kay Ash in the 1980s. Roger Schwarz, "The 'Sandwich Approach' Undermines Your Feedback" (Harvard Business Review, 19 April 2013), argues that it weakens both the feedback and the working relationship; the house follows him. House observation of the mechanism: the recipient learns that praise signals bad news and discounts all of it; the criticism, buried between two softer statements, leaves the room diluted; and generic praise ("love the energy") carries no information. Kim Scott's Radical Candor (2017) supplies the positive frame, care personally and challenge directly, and names the failures: ruinous empathy (care without challenge), obnoxious aggression (challenge without care) and manipulative insincerity (neither). The house format: one specific thing working (information about what to keep), the problem with its why, the fix with its reference. Praise is given when it is earned and specific, and on its own when the work is good.

### 6.11 How to say it is not working

1. Say it early. The cost of a kill rises every day (section 8.4).
2. Say it to the maker first, privately, before the room or the client.
3. Say it plainly: "This route is not working." Then the reason in one sentence, at rung 3.
4. Separate the person from the work, and name what the maker did well in the process, specifically.
5. Name what survives: the reference, the type choice, the insight.
6. Give the next step and the time: "Two new routes by Thursday from the insight; drop the gold."
7. Never say "it's fine" about work that is not, because juniors learn the standard from what the CD lets through.

### 6.12 To developers: reviewing the build

For web and app work the build is the week's feedback stream; the QA gates are 06-ui-design-craft.md, section 20, and 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md, section 15.
- Review the staging URL on the house test devices: a 2023 or 2024 Android sold at about ₹12,000 to 18,000 and an iPhone (21, section 14), with campaign links also opened inside the Instagram and WhatsApp WebViews (the browsers embedded in those apps). The Figma file is the spec; the build is the thing reviewed.
- Every note carries the URL, viewport width, browser, a screenshot, the expected value and its token name: "space-6 is 24px; the build shows 20px at 390px wide in Chrome on Android". A note that says only "match the design" goes back to its writer.
- Compare with a 50 percent opacity overlay (the PerfectPixel extension, or Dev Mode compare) at the 2px tolerance of 06, section 20. Judge motion in the Chrome DevTools Animations panel at 25 and 10 percent speed, and give easing as the token or the `cubic-bezier()` value.
- Visual regression testing (automated screenshot comparison against approved baselines: Chromatic for Storybook, Percy, Playwright's `toHaveScreenshot`) catches drift between rounds (07-design-systems-and-tokens.md, section 9.2).
- Each note is marked design defect (the spec was wrong or missing) or build defect (the build departs from a correct spec) with its S-rating, one ticket per screen in the tracker (Linear or Jira), and comments go on Vercel preview deployments where the stack has them.

### 6.13 Feedback across cultures in a Mumbai and Dubai studio

Section 10 covers culture in the work; this covers culture in the room, in a studio that mixes Indian, Emirati, Lebanese, British and Filipino staff and presents to hierarchical clients.
- Erin Meyer, The Culture Map (2014), places countries on an evaluating scale of how negative feedback is given: American managers wrap it in positives, while French, Dutch, Israeli and German managers state it directly. Upgraders (absolutely, totally) and downgraders (kind of, slightly; British understatement is the classic case) change how strong a note sounds, so "a few small things to look at" from a British reviewer can mean the route is in trouble. The house reads the book for each nationality in the team and calibrates per person.
- Power distance (Geert Hofstede's measure of how far less powerful people accept unequal power) scores high for India (77), the UAE (74) and Saudi Arabia (72) on The Culture Factor's comparison tool (read October 2026). In the room, juniors rarely contradict the CD, and "yes" can mean "I heard you".
- Counters: silent written cards before discussion (4.4); the Critical Response Process for juniors (4.3); ask "what would you change?" before giving your note; confirm each action in writing after the crit; correct a senior client person only in a one-to-one, never in front of their juniors.

### 6.14 To printers, creators, illustrators and stylists

- Printers at press check: notes against the signed contract proof and the colour bar, as density or hue ("magenta down in the skin, hold the black"). An ink-key change in one zone moves every image in that column of the sheet, so trade-offs are agreed before the press runs (20, sections 5.4 and 5.5).
- Creators and influencers: brief the outcome, the mandatories and the disclosure (ASCI influencer guidelines in India; the UAE Advertiser Permit, 19, section 16); give them the facts and leave the lines to them, because their voice is what the brand pays for; one round of notes, on the first cut.
- Illustrators and lettering artists: notes at the sketch stage, because redrawing after inking or vectorising costs days.
- Stylists and set designers: approval on a lit test frame seen through the camera, because a mood board shows intent only; notes route through the photographer (6.5).

## 7. Receiving feedback and decision rights

### 7.1 The CD decides

Decision rights are written into the brief at kickoff (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 12.4). Inside the studio:

| Decision | Decides | Consulted | Final approval |
|---|---|---|---|
| The idea and the route | CD | Team, strategist | Client's named decision maker |
| Craft standard and execution | CD (or the associate creative director, ACD, by delegation) | Maker | CD |
| Brand guardrails (mark, colour, legal lines) | Client brand owner | CD | Client |
| Regulatory content (RERA, Trakheesi, ASCI, pricing claims) | Client legal | Account lead, CD | Client legal |
| Cultural and religious sensitivity | CD with a native reviewer | Client | Client |

A crit informs; the CD decides in the room and says the decision aloud.

### 7.2 Listening for the problem behind the note

Clients give solutions; the CD's job is to recover the problem (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 8.4 for the client-handling method).

| The note | The problem it often hides | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "Make the logo bigger" | The brand is not recognised fast enough, or the founder does not feel seen | "When someone sees this for two seconds, what should they remember?" |
| "Can we try it in blue?" | The palette feels cold, off-category, or like a competitor | "What does the current colour make you feel?" |
| "It feels empty" | The value is unclear; the client fears the price is not justified | "What does a buyer need to know before they call?" |
| "Add the amenities" | Sales team pressure; fear of missing a buyer's checklist | "Where will the buyer look for detail: here, or the brochure?" |
| "Make it pop" | Low contrast, slow pace, or no single focal point | "Brighter, faster, bigger, or warmer?" |
| "My wife does not like it" | Politics, or a real reaction from the target buyer | "Is she the customer we are designing for?" |
| "Can we add a festive touch?" | A real calendar need (Diwali, Eid, National Day) | "Which moment, which market, and for how long?" |

### 7.3 The client note taxonomy

| Type | Tells | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Taste | First person, no reason: "I don't like green" | Return to the brief and the references; offer the test; give way on non-idea elements |
| Fear | Risk words: "what if", "the board", "competitors do", "safe" | Name the risk, show precedent (a reference that took the same risk and worked), offer a measured test |
| Politics | A third party's view relayed, a late joiner, a change of tone after an internal meeting | Ask who the decision maker is (19, section 12.4); request the stakeholder in the next meeting |
| Real | A fact: legibility, regulation, a cultural misread, a wrong product detail, a sales insight | Thank, fix fast, and record the learning in the brief |

### 7.4 The brief as arbiter

The signed brief (19, section 2) is the referee: objective, audience, single-minded proposition, mandatories, measures. When a note conflicts with it, the CD asks one question: "Has the brief changed?" If yes, the brief is reissued and the change is scoped and priced (24, section 3). If no, the note is answered against the brief, in writing.

### 7.5 When to hold the line and when to let go

Hold when the note breaks the idea, damages long-term brand equity, breaches law or ethics, or fails accessibility. Let go when the note is taste on an element that does not carry the idea, when the client is right, or when the fight costs more trust than the element is worth. House rules: hold on at most one thing per round, because a CD who fights every note is heard on none; hold with evidence: a reference, a test or data; and after two refusals of the same route by the decision maker, stop presenting it (19, section 10.4).

### 7.6 When the data disagrees with the eye

Performance creative makes "the ugly ad won the test" a weekly note. Two bounds. The cautionary case: Doug Bowman left Google in March 2009 ("Goodbye, Google", stopdesign.com), citing a team that could not decide between two blues and tested 41 shades between them. The evidence for brand building: Les Binet and Peter Field, The Long and the Short of It (IPA, 2013), about 60:40 brand to activation as the starting balance (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 4.5).

House rule: the CD decides the idea, the distinctive assets and anything that builds memory; data chooses between executions of one idea at the activation end (hook, first frame, offer, call to action, format). A winning "ugly" variant is read for its mechanism (the product clearer, the hook faster, the price visible), and that mechanism is then crafted well; the ugliness is never the lesson. The success metric is written down before launch; tests run at least 7 days to cover weekday cycles; brand metrics are never judged from a 3-day test. Derived metrics: hook rate (3-second video views divided by impressions) and hold rate (ThruPlays, Meta's count of plays to completion or to 15 seconds, divided by 3-second views).

### 7.7 When the public hates it

Reverted: Gap's new logo (4 October 2010, withdrawn about a week later); Tropicana Pure Premium's redesign by Arnell Group (January 2009, reverted after about two months, with sales reported down about 20 percent because shoppers could not find the product); Cracker Barrel's removal of its Old Timer figure (August 2025, reversed within about a week; 01-history-and-movements.md). Held: the London 2012 logo by Wolff Olins (June 2007), ridiculed at launch and used through the Games; Airbnb's Bélo (July 2014; 12.1), remixed online and kept. Jaguar's November 2024 rebrand is in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 7.

Rule: separate reaction to change, which decays in weeks, from functional failure (shoppers cannot find the product, as with Tropicana) and from betrayal of core customers (Cracker Barrel). Judge on behaviour (sales, share of search; 18); comment sentiment measures only the reaction to change. A holding window of about 8 to 12 weeks is agreed with the client before launch and broken early only by behaviour data.

### 7.8 Receiving feedback as the CD

Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen, Thanks for the Feedback (2014), separate three kinds of feedback (appreciation, coaching, evaluation) and three triggers that block hearing it (truth: "this is wrong"; relationship: "who are you to say"; identity: "I am not good at this"). House protocol for any note the CD receives: thank, restate it in your own words, ask "what is the one thing?", hold any defence in the room, and decide after the overnight test (4.8). Upward feedback: a quarterly anonymous survey asks makers to rate how useful the CD's crits were (1 to 5) and the one thing to change; the CD reads the results to the team.

## 8. Killing work and the edit

### 8.1 The courage to remove

Arthur Quiller-Couch's advice to writers, in lectures given at Cambridge in 1913 and 1914 and published as On the Art of Writing (1916), was to "murder your darlings": the passage the writer loves most for its own sake is the one to suspect. In design the darling is the effect the maker learned last week. Rams's tenth principle states the goal: as little design as possible.

### 8.2 The edit as the final skill

Design-taste names the edit as the final skill (section 4, step 6, and section 5). Walter Murch treats the edit as the last rewrite of a film (16, section 1.6). The CD's edit runs at three scales: the campaign (which executions to drop), the piece (which elements), and the detail (which word, which frame).

### 8.3 Remove until it breaks: the procedure

1. List every element: each word, image, rule, colour, motion, logo instance, shot.
2. Hide one at a time (Figma: toggle visibility; Resolve or Premiere: disable the clip; copy: delete the sentence).
3. Ask: does the piece still carry the idea and do its job? If yes, it stays hidden.
4. Continue until a removal breaks the idea or the function.
5. Restore the last removed element, then remove one more decorative element: the one accessory rule, after the dressing advice commonly attributed to Coco Chanel (look in the mirror before leaving and take one thing off; design-taste, section 4). If the piece survives the overnight test (4.8), it stays off.

### 8.4 Sunk cost in creative work

The sunk-cost fallacy is letting past, unrecoverable investment drive a future decision. In studios it shows as "we spent three weeks on this route", "the shoot cost ₹40 lakh", or 600 generated images from which one must be used. The only question: knowing what we know today, starting fresh, would we choose this? Clients carry sunk cost too (a logo the founder drew, a render already paid for); acknowledge the investment, then decide on the future.

### 8.5 Saving the idea for later

Killed routes are kept. Every one goes into the idea bank with: the brief it answered, the one-sentence idea, the boards, the reason it was killed, and tags (category, mechanism, tone). The bank is read quarterly against live briefs; a route killed for a jeweller's budget may be right for a hotel's launch. Ownership rules for unused concepts are in the contract (24, section 4).

## 9. Judging currency

### 9.1 The reading rule

The rule (where a look lives tells its age: fashion houses and award nominees for rising, SaaS sites and Dribbble for peaking, templates and AI defaults for dead) and the current read are in design-taste, references/currency-2026.md, sections 1 to 5. This section applies it to critique and hiring.

### 9.2 Rising or peaking in a portfolio

| Signal | Suggests rising | Suggests peaking |
|---|---|---|
| Where the maker found it | A foundry release, a fashion campaign, a studio case study, an archive | A Dribbble or Behance feed, a SaaS homepage, a tutorial |
| Execution | The look adapted to the content and the brand | The look applied whole, the same on every project |
| Dating | The maker used it before it appeared in templates | The maker's newest work matches this quarter's popular shots |
| Explanation | Can name the lineage and the reason | "It's what clients want right now" |

### 9.3 The three-year test

Ask of each element: will this embarrass the client in three years? Sort elements into structure (grid, typeface, photography approach, proportion, colour logic) and surface (effects, trend type, trend colour, motion fads). House rule: at most one surface trend per piece, placed in a layer that is cheap to replace (a campaign asset, a social template); the mark and the core system carry none. Identity work is judged at ten years: Rand's IBM stripes (1972) and the Hermès box carry no surface trend.

Two dials sharpen the test. MAYA (Raymond Loewy's "most advanced, yet acceptable"; 02-perception-and-psychology.md, section 10.4) sets how far to push a client: name where the client's buyer sits, then push to the edge of what that buyer accepts. The Lindy effect (Albert Goldman, "Lindy's Law", The New Republic, 1964; extended by Benoit Mandelbrot and by Nassim Nicholas Taleb in Antifragile, 2012) holds that for non-perishable things expected remaining life grows with age: a typeface or grid with 60 years of use is likelier to hold to 2036 than a 2024 look, so the three-year test asks how old each element's lineage is.

### 9.4 Timelessness markers

Proportion systems that can be described (a grid, a ratio); typefaces with decades of use; real photography of real places and people; colour taken from materials; function before effect; content-led form; a single owned element. Benchmark: the New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual (Unimark International, Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda, 1970), whose principles still structure the subway's signage.

## 10. Taste across cultures

### 10.1 Indian and Gulf luxury codes beside Western minimalism

Design-taste's physics of expensive (section 2) was formed mostly on European and Japanese reference. It holds in India and the Gulf for most premium work, and it changes at specific moments.

| Code | Western minimalist luxury | Indian luxury | Gulf luxury |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Black, white, one owned colour | Red, marigold, rani pink, antique gold, indigo; colour as celebration | Gold, black, deep green, sand; white in dress codes |
| Ornament | Absent or a single gesture | Craft as proof of value: zardozi (metal-thread embroidery), block print, meenakari (enamel), kundan (stones set in gold foil), jaali (pierced screens) | Geometry (girih, interlaced strapwork), arabesque, calligraphy |
| Abundance | Emptiness reads as confidence | Abundance and family read as generosity | Hospitality (the majlis, the welcome) reads as status |
| Image | One object in real light | People, family, lineage, the wedding | Discretion in depicting people; architecture and craft |
| Script | Latin only | Devanagari and regional scripts beside Latin; Hinglish in voice | Arabic first or co-equal; right-to-left layouts |
| Calendar | Seasons and fashion weeks | Wedding season (roughly November to February), Diwali, regional festivals | Ramadan and Eid, UAE National Day (2 December), Saudi National Day (23 September), Saudi Founding Day (22 February) |

### 10.2 Where restraint fails locally

- Weddings: an Indian wedding is maximal by design; a white minimalist invitation can read as cold and, in Hindu tradition, white carries associations of mourning. The taste move is maximalism with control, which is a system with stricter rules.
- Festivals: Diwali gifting, Eid hampers and Ramadan iftar settings call for warmth, light and generosity; a stark grid with one line of copy can look stingy.
- The maximal Sabyasachi world: Sabyasachi Mukherjee's label builds abundance with total control: a narrow palette of deep reds, antique gold and ivory, heritage textiles, layered rooms photographed like old portraits, and a consistency that runs from stores to campaigns to the jewellery. Maximalism there is curation. Raw Mango and Nicobar show Indian restraint works too (design-taste, references/worlds.md).

### 10.3 Ornament with precision and ornament as noise

| Test | Ornament with precision | Ornament as noise |
|---|---|---|
| Source | A named craft or tradition (zardozi, Mughal jaali, Kufic geometry) | Clip-art paisley, generic arabesque brushes |
| Drawing | Drawn at the resolution of the medium, with consistent line weight | Scaled, stretched, blurred, mixed line weights |
| Scale and repeat | One scale, a true repeat, a deliberate border | Many scales, broken repeats |
| Ground | A quiet field around it | Ornament on every surface |
| Meaning | Carries something (place, lineage, occasion) | Fills space |

Precision benchmark in the Gulf: the Museum of the Future in Dubai (opened February 2022), whose façade windows are Arabic calligraphy by the Emirati artist Mattar bin Lahej; the ornament is the architecture.

### 10.4 Bilingual craft as a taste marker

A second script set as an afterthought (smaller, a different weight, stuck at the bottom, machine-translated) is the fastest tell that a Gulf or Indian brand is not premium. Bilingual craft: both scripts designed together from the first sketch; Latin cap height matched to the Arabic tooth height or the Devanagari headline (03-typography.md, section 15); right-to-left layouts mirrored with intent (direction-dependent icons flipped, numerals and logos kept unmirrored); type families with matched Arabic and Latin (Dubai Font, 2017; 29LT families); copy transcreated by a native writer. Reviewing it needs a native reader in the room, every time.

### 10.5 Local reference worlds

Category benchmarks with their India and GCC (Gulf Cooperation Council) notes are in design-taste, references/worlds.md. Critique questions to add for these markets: would this read as luxury to a first-generation buyer in Mumbai and to an old family in Delhi? Would an Emirati family find the imagery respectful? Does the Arabic look designed or translated? Is the festival treatment specific to this brand, or the market's default gold-and-lamps?

### 10.6 Regional codes inside India and the Gulf

"Indian luxury" and "Gulf luxury" are each several codes. A brief names the region before the moodboard does.

| Region | Codes | Critique note |
|---|---|---|
| South Indian bridal | Kanjeevaram silk, temple jewellery, jasmine in the hair | Gold heavier and more sculptural than the North Indian kundan look |
| Bengali | The red-bordered white sari, shankha-pola (conch and coral bangles) | Red and white is the bridal and festival pair |
| Punjabi | Chooda bangles, phulkari ("flower work") embroidery | Saturated colour; celebration is loud by design |
| Parsi | Gara embroidery with motifs from the China trade | Fine needlework; restraint inside ornament |
| Goan | Lime-washed Indo-Portuguese façades, azulejo-like tile | A Mediterranean register inside India |
| Dubai | Cosmopolitan and expatriate global luxury | Arabic often co-equal; the buyer may be from anywhere |
| Abu Dhabi | Cultural and institutional: the Saadiyat Cultural District, the Louvre Abu Dhabi | Heritage and culture read as status |
| Saudi Arabia | Heritage revival: Najdi mud-brick, Diriyah; the Saudi Architecture Characters Map, launched by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman on 17 March 2025 with 19 regional architectural styles | A Riyadh brief checks the region's style before any architectural motif |

Charged colours and symbols: saffron carries religious meaning and contemporary party-political association in India, so saffron-led work is reviewed by a native reviewer; the Saudi flag carries the shahada (the Muslim declaration of faith), so it is never altered, never printed on disposable items and never flown at half-mast.

### 10.7 Rasa as critique vocabulary

The Natya Shastra (attributed to Bharata) names eight rasas (the aesthetic emotions an audience tastes): shringara (love), hasya (comedy), karuna (compassion), raudra (fury), vira (heroism), bhayanaka (fear), bibhatsa (disgust) and adbhuta (wonder). Later theorists added shanta (peace), defended by Abhinavagupta (about the 10th to 11th century). Use: name the intended dominant rasa of a film before the crit (bridal jewellery: shringara, with karuna at the vidaai, the bride's farewell; a heritage hotel: shanta; a launch: adbhuta) and check that every shot serves it. A film whose rasas mix with no dominant one reads as muddled, and Indian clients recognise the vocabulary.

## 11. Portfolio and work review for hiring

### 11.1 The 15-minute read

The first screen is five minutes per portfolio (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 11.1). Shortlisted portfolios get the 15-minute read:

| Minutes | What | Looking for |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | The index or homepage, two-second test on each project thumbnail | Range, edit, whether the selection itself shows taste |
| 2 to 7 | One project in depth, chosen by the reviewer (often the third, because the first is the most polished) | Brief, idea, rejected routes, system, applications, outcome |
| 7 to 11 | A second project in a different register | Range without loss of craft |
| 11 to 13 | Process evidence: sketches, references, versions, writing | Judgement visible in decisions |
| 13 to 15 | Write the verdict (expensive, competent, cheap), three strengths, three questions | A record comparable across candidates |

### 11.2 What to look for

- Decisions: each can be explained from the brief and the content.
- Restraint: what was left out, and how few devices carry the work.
- Range: at least two registers, for example a luxury hotel and a cultural institution.
- Craft under constraint: a real client, a real budget, a regulatory line, a bilingual requirement handled well.
- Systems: the work shown across 20 applications as well as the hero.
- Writing: case studies written plainly, with numbers.

| Role | Ask for | Read it for |
|---|---|---|
| Copywriter | Headlines in a campaign sequence, one long-copy piece, a script | Three read aloud: voice, range, one fact per line |
| Motion designer | A 60 to 90 second reel, breakdowns and one project file | The first 10 seconds; timing and easing; the candidate's own share in the breakdowns |
| Photographer or DP (director of photography) | The contact sheet or raw selects from one shoot | The eye and the edit, which finals hide |
| UI/UX designer | A case study with research, edge states and a sourced metric | States designed; the metric's source |
| 3D artist | Clay and wireframe passes beside the final | Modelling and lighting, judged apart from the render |

Bias controls: names, schools and agencies stripped from the first screen; two reviewers score independently on the same rubric; the shortlist decision is written down before any interview.

### 11.3 The questions

1. What was the brief, in one sentence, and what did you think the real problem was?
2. Which routes did you kill, and why?
3. What did you borrow, and from whom?
4. What did the client change, and were they right?
5. What would you change now?
6. Which part was yours, and who else worked on it?
7. Where did AI tools enter the process, and what did you do with their output?
8. Show me something you are not proud of and tell me why.
9. Whose work do you study right now?
10. Critique this (hand over a piece) in five minutes.

### 11.4 Red flags

- Dribbble shots without context: no brief, no client, no system, no outcome.
- Purchased or modified templates (Framer, Webflow, Squarespace, Envato) presented as original work.
- AI-generated imagery presented as photography or illustration without disclosure; check for the tells (22, section 5) and for Content Credentials (the C2PA provenance standard) where the file carries them.
- Team work with no credit split; work for an agency's client shown in breach of an NDA (non-disclosure agreement).
- Every project in the same style, whatever the client.
- Concepts only, nothing shipped, after three or more years of practice.
- Metrics without a source ("increased engagement 300 percent").

### 11.5 The test brief

Paid at the candidate's day rate, capped at 4 to 6 hours, with a real-shaped brief and one hard constraint (a bilingual lockup, a 9:16 cutdown, a RERA line on a hoarding). Deliverables: a reference board with the borrowed thing named for each, the idea in one sentence, one finished piece, and a 150-word rationale. Second part, 30 minutes: a written critique of a weak piece in the house format (verdict, what works, what does not, the fix). Scored with the rubric (section 13) by two reviewers independently. The brief is one the house has already answered, because a live client brief would make the test spec work by another name (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 11.1).

## 12. Case studies of critique

### 12.1 Brand New: the review as an argument

Brand New (UnderConsideration, Armin Vit and Bryony Gomez-Palacio, reviewing identities since 2006) is the longest-running public identity critique. As of October 2026 the review text, polls and comments are for subscribers; the title, subtitle, before-and-after images, byline, industry and tags are public. Reviews are bylined by Armin and by other writers (Adam and Josh in late October 2026). The form, move by move:

| Move | What it does | What to take into a Mask360 critique |
|---|---|---|
| Subtitle as thesis | A few words frame the argument ("Coming Full Circle(s)", "Legally Bold") | State the verdict's angle before the evidence |
| Before and after | The evidence leads | Show the work and its predecessor or competitor first |
| Context | Who the client is and what changed, from public sources | Judge against the client's real situation and constraints |
| Stated intent | The designers' or press statements, quoted and kept apart from the critic's voice | Judge the work against what it set out to do |
| The system in order | Mark, then type, then colour and devices, then applications, each paragraph tied to an image | One claim per image; no claim without evidence |
| Overall | A closing verdict paragraph | The house verdict line (expensive, competent, cheap) |
| Readers' polls and comments | The crowd's verdict beside the critic's | Separate expert and audience reactions |

Three reviews, read for the question the form forced (the verdicts are subscriber text and are not reproduced here):

1. Airbnb by DesignStudio, reviewed by Armin on 17 July 2014 (tags: colourful, icon, loops, red). The identity launched with a story: the Bélo symbol and the "Belong Anywhere" positioning, and the internet remixed the symbol quickly. The question: does the mark stand without its launch narrative? Lesson for critique: judge a mark first with the story covered, then with it, and say which one the audience will meet.
2. Mastercard by Pentagram, reviewed by Armin on 14 July 2016, subtitle "Coming Full Circle(s)" (tags: circles, geometric, overlap, overlay, sans serif). An evolution: the interlocking circles simplified and the wordmark set in lowercase outside them. The question: how much equity was kept against how much change was gained. In January 2019 Mastercard dropped the name from the symbol in most uses, which shows how much recognition the circles carried. Lesson: an evolution is judged on equity kept, and the critic must say whether the change was visible enough to justify its cost.
3. White & Case by Uncommon Creative Studio, reviewed by Armin on 26 October 2026, subtitle "Legally Bold" (tags: ampersand, blue, law firm, ligature, proprietary type, serif; 109 comments by 29 October). Law firms default to serifs and navy. The question: can a proprietary serif and an ampersand ligature distinguish a partnership inside its category's own codes? Lesson: in conservative categories, critique judges differentiation within convention, which is the situation of most Mask360 real estate and jewellery clients.

Argument mapping: Stephen Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958), breaks an argument into six slots: claim; grounds (the evidence, here the image); warrant (the principle linking grounds to claim); backing (the reference or standard behind the warrant); qualifier (how strongly the claim holds); rebuttal (the strongest counter-reading). Mastercard mapped from public evidence: claim, the evolution kept equity; grounds, the before and after; warrant, recognition rests on the overlapping circles; backing, the January 2019 dropping of the name; qualifier, in most uses; rebuttal, the change was too small to justify its cost. Airbnb's rebuttal slot holds the remixes (did the story carry the mark?); White & Case's warrant is differentiation within convention, and its rebuttal is that serif and blue are what every law firm uses. Every house long-form review (1.11) fills all six slots and reports Brand New's reader poll (Great, Fine, Bad; 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md, section 7.6) as the audience verdict, apart from the critic's.

### 12.2 An Awwwards jury: the score profile as the comment

Awwwards weights Design 40 percent, Usability 30, Creativity 20 and Content 10; each site goes to at least 18 jurors; the three scores furthest from the average are removed; Honorable Mentions (Awwwards' spelling) go to sites scoring 6.5 or more, and the Developer Award to sites scoring above 7 with the developer jury (awwwards.com/about-evaluation, read October 2026). Site of the Day pages publish scores per juror and per category, without written comments; the profile is the critique.

A24 by Ravi Klaassens, Site of the Day, 26 October 2026 (tags include GSAP, Three.js and Barba.js):

| Measure | Score |
|---|---|
| Overall (jury) | 7.65 |
| Design (40%) | 7.65 |
| Usability (30%) | 7.31 |
| Creativity (20%) | 8.01 |
| Content (10%) | 7.95 |
| Jurors | 18; individual overall scores from 7.00 to 9.00, untrimmed mean 7.57 |
| Developer Award score | 7.41 (5 jurors): animations and transitions 8.00, responsive 7.60, semantics and SEO 7.40, performance 7.40, markup 7.00, accessibility 6.80 |
| Community score | 9.21 |

Reading it: the weighted categories reproduce the overall exactly (0.4 by 7.65 plus 0.3 by 7.31 plus 0.2 by 8.01 plus 0.1 by 7.95 equals 7.65). The jury paid for creativity and content and marked usability lowest; the developer jury's lowest mark is accessibility. The community scored it 1.56 points above the jury, the gap between spectacle and craft judgement. Lesson for Mask360 microsites: the score that keeps a luxury scroll site from the top is usability, and accessibility is the developer jury's weak point; both are fixable before submission (section 5.3).

### 12.3 A D&AD jury: reading a pencil

Juries have approximately seven judges led by a Jury President and judge "ideas, craft and impact" (dandad.org/awards/judging). The levels (dandad.org/awards/pencils; both read October 2026):

| Level | D&AD wording | House reading |
|---|---|---|
| Black Pencil | "for ground-breaking work"; a handful or none in a year | Changed what the category thought possible |
| Yellow Pencil | "true creative excellence" | The standard to study |
| Graphite Pencil | Work "worthy of consideration for a Yellow Pencil" | Study it for the step it missed |
| Wood Pencil | "the best of the year" | Excellent in its year |
| Shortlist | Merit | Well made and worth filing |

Example in the 2026 archive: Bottega, a family-run Italian café and sandwich shop in Brooklyn; identity and environmental graphics by Pentagram New York (partner Andrea Trabucco-Campos); Wood Pencil, New Brand Identity. The archive page publishes no jury rationale. The house therefore writes its own jury note when it judges or pre-judges an entry, in three sentences mapped to D&AD's three words, plus the level. Idea: what the work proposes and whether it is new to the category. Craft: the execution at its strongest and weakest point. Impact: what it changed for the client or the category, with the number if there is one. Level: Shortlist, Wood, Graphite, Yellow or Black, and the one thing that would have moved it up a level.

### 12.4 Pixar notes

- The note that broke a film. On 19 November 1993 Pixar screened half of Toy Story for Disney executives. Disney's notes had pushed for "more edginess" in Woody and Buzz; Tom Hanks said Woody had become "a real jerk". Peter Schneider halted production (Pixar later called it the "Black Friday" incident). John Lasseter's team rewrote the story in about three months, turning Woody from a tyrant into a wise leader; production resumed by February 1994. Lesson: "edgier" is a solution with no problem attached; the problem (audiences need a reason to like the hero) would have pointed elsewhere.
- The note that found a film. Inside Out's early version followed Joy and Fear lost together. More than two years in, Pete Docter told John Lasseter the film was not working and proposed swapping Fear for Sadness; the storyboards went through seven to eight braintrust screenings before the story locked. Lesson: the braintrust diagnoses, the director finds the structural fix, and a late restructure is cheaper than a weak film.

### 12.5 Three Mask360 critiques in full

Illustrative composites written to show the house format (design-taste, section 8); the projects are invented, the values are the kind the CD writes.

Critique 1: hotel microsite, a 22-room heritage hotel in Jaipur.
- Verdict: not presentable, with an open S4 (no idea yet); rubric 2.25 on the site profile (13.1), cheap, with the idea gate and the function cap both applying. The photography is expensive.
- What works: the courtyard photographs at first light. Real sandstone, real shadow, nobody in frame; they carry the place better than any line on the page.
- What does not: (1) Idea, S4: the page lists rooms, spa and dining in three equal cards and the headline "Experience timeless royal luxury" could sit on any palace hotel in Rajasthan; there is no idea yet. (2) S3: the 14MB autoplay hero video puts LCP at about 4 seconds on a mid-range Android; the target is 2.5. (3) S2: eleven sections fade up on scroll. (4) S3: the hotel's Devanagari name is set in a default system font at half the Latin size. (5) S3: image captions in #B5B5B5 on white measure 2.05:1 against the 4.5:1 minimum. (6) S2: the booking widget uses 4px corners inside 24px cards.
- The fix: Idea first: "the house wakes slowly"; the scroll follows one day from first light to lamps, using the existing photographs in time order. Headline: the one fact only this house has (the year the courtyard was built, the family that still lives on the top floor). Hero: the first-light still as an AVIF poster under 200KB, video loaded after interaction. One motion: the light shifting across the courtyard, scroll-linked; everything else still. Devanagari name drawn with the Latin in one lockup (03, section 15). Captions to #767676 or darker (4.54:1). Widget radius 16px, concentric with the card. References: Aman's property pages for margin and pace; The Postcard for the host's voice; RAAS Jodhpur for heritage without gold.
- Scores: idea 2, specificity 2, hierarchy 3, craft 3, restraint 2, coherence 3, register 3, currency 3, function and access 1, ending 3; weighted 2.25.

Critique 2: jewellery reel, 30 seconds, a Mumbai fine jewellery brand's bridal edit.
- Verdict: not presentable, with an open S4 (no idea); rubric 1.75 on the film profile, cheap.
- What works: the macro of the clasp mechanism at 0:18; the only shot that shows how the piece is made and handled.
- What does not: (1) Idea, S4: necklaces rotate on black velvet with a sparkle overlay and the super "Timeless elegance for your special day"; it is a catalogue with music. (2) S3: the logo fills frame one, so the reel announces itself as an ad. (3) S2: the score starts on frame one and cuts land 2 to 4 frames off the beat. (4) S3: the one hand shot has orange skin; blacks are crushed on the velvet so the setting detail is lost. (5) S3: captions are editor-default white with a heavy stroke, in the bottom 15 percent of the 9:16 frame, under the platform interface.
- The fix: Make the clasp shot the idea: "fastened by family", the moment a mother fastens the necklace on the bride. Worn on skin in low window light, with a hard point source for fire in the stones and soft fill for the gold. Open on room tone and a 1.5-second hold on hands; music enters on the first cut; cut on or a frame before the beat. Grade skin onto the skin tone line before any look (16, section 8.5); lift blacks to hold the setting. Captions in the brand face, above the bottom safe zone (26, section 2). Logo on the end frame only, on the master artwork. Cutdowns at 15 and 6 seconds built from the same idea. References: Cartier's long-form films for pace, Sophie Bille Brahe for jewellery worn on skin, Zoya for Indian restraint (design-taste, references/worlds.md).
- Scores: idea 1, specificity 1, hierarchy 2, craft 2, restraint 2, coherence 3, register 2, currency 2, function and access 2, ending 2; weighted 1.75.

Critique 3: agency deck, Mask360 credentials for a Dubai developer's launch pitch.
- Verdict: competent, at the floor: rubric 3.05 on the default profile, no open S4.
- What works: the Mumbai launch case study, with the enquiry numbers and the timeline on one slide; proof stated plainly.
- What does not: (1) Idea, S3: the hero of the deck is Mask360; the client appears on slide 19. House rule: the client is the hero. (2) S2: 41 slides; slide 2 is a logo wall. (3) S2: slides run about 60 percent text against the house 70 visual to 30 text. (4) S2: three headline sizes (28, 32, 36px) that read as one. (5) S3: the client's real colour is absent; the deck uses the Mask360 white ground. (6) S3: the strategy slide has three focal points. (7) S2: it ends on "Thank you".
- The fix: 16 slides. Open on the client's buyer and the insight, then the idea in one sentence, then the work, then the proof (case studies after the idea), then the decision asked for. Client deck rules from design-taste, section 7: #0A0A0A ground, the client's real hex from their guidelines as the only colour, Space Grotesk at 700 for headlines at about 96px against 300-weight body at 20px. One idea per slide; the logo wall goes to an appendix. Last slide: the decision and the date. References: Apple keynote pacing; Pentagram's case study pages for showing work large and unlabelled.

## 13. The critique rubric

### 13.1 Dimensions, weights and anchors

Scored 1 to 5 per dimension; anchors at 1, 3 and 5, with 2 and 4 between them. Weights favour the idea, the house's first question, in the way Awwwards weights design and D&AD leads with ideas. Each weight profile (a set of weights for one deliverable type) sums to 100. The default serves decks and anything unlisted; site or app gives function and access 20, because a page that fails LCP and contrast cannot be expensive (Awwwards gives usability 30 percent, 12.2); identity drops ending, which has no meaning for a mark, and judges currency at ten years (9.3); a campaign idea scores the craft promise (5.9, questions 11 to 13) in the craft row and has no hierarchy to judge.

| Dimension | Default | Identity | Site or app | Film | Campaign idea | 1: cheap | 3: competent | 5: expensive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idea | 20 | 20 | 15 | 20 | 30 | No idea, or a category cliché; cannot be said in a sentence | A clear idea that answers the brief; seen before | One sentence, specific, surprising, inevitable once seen |
| Specificity | 10 | 15 | 10 | 10 | 15 | Could be any brand in the category | Belongs to the category and the client's tier | Only this brand; passes cover the logo |
| Hierarchy | 10 | 5 | 10 | 5 | 0 | Three or more focal points; the eye wanders | Clear order with one competing element | One entry point, an intended path, and a resolution |
| Craft | 15 | 20 | 15 | 20 | 5 | S3 and S4 defects (section 3); defaults everywhere | A few S2 defects; type, colour and alignment sound | No defect visible at 400 percent; detail rewards inspection |
| Restraint | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 5 | Every device in the kit; effects stacked | Some excess; one or two removable elements | Remove-until-it-breaks applied; every element earns its place |
| Coherence | 10 | 15 | 5 | 5 | 15 | Each touchpoint looks like a different brand | Consistent with occasional drift | One system across every format and script |
| Register | 10 | 5 | 5 | 10 | 15 | Wrong voice and cultural tone for the audience | Appropriate but generic | Pitched exactly for this audience, market and occasion |
| Currency | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | Dead or peaking looks used straight | Current without a point of view | Rising or timeless, used with a reason; passes the three-year test |
| Function and access | 5 | 5 | 20 | 5 | 5 | Fails contrast, performance or platform specs | Passes the thresholds | Passes with margin; reduced motion and bilingual handled |
| Ending | 5 | 0 | 5 | 10 | 5 | Stops; "Thank you"; a logo with a whoosh | A clean close | The last moment carries the idea |

### 13.2 Scoring rules

- Weighted score: sum of (score times weight) divided by 100.
- Idea gate: if Idea scores below 3, the weighted score is capped at 2.5, because craft cannot rescue a missing idea (4.6).
- S4 gate: any open S4 (4.5) puts "not presentable" at the head of the verdict line; the score is still logged for the maker's trend line and stays inside the studio.
- Function cap: function and access at 1 holds the verdict at competent or below, whatever the weighted score.
- Verdict: 4.2 and above expensive; 3.0 up to 4.2 competent; below 3.0 cheap. The verdict line in the house format uses these words.
- Calibration: two scorers, independently. A difference of more than 1 point on any dimension is discussed with the piece on screen and the reference beside it; the agreed score is logged. Anchor exemplars (three scored reference pieces per dimension, one each at 1, 3 and 5) are re-scored by the CD and the ACD each quarter; the target is 80 percent of dimension scores within 1 point between scorers.
- The log: date, piece, maker, scores, verdict, the top three notes. Over a quarter, a maker's trend line is the growth record (00, section 9, holds the self-assessment rubrics).

### 13.3 The verdict vocabulary

| Word | Meaning in the house | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Expensive | Reads as four hundred hours by someone at the top of the field (design-taste, section 1) | Rubric 4.2 and above |
| Competent | Correct, finished, unremarkable | Rubric 3.0 up to 4.2 |
| Cheap | Defaults, clichés and defects visible in two seconds | Rubric below 3.0 |
| Finished | Nothing left to remove or fix at this stage | The CD signs off; said aloud |
| Generic | Could belong to a competitor | Fails cover the logo |
| Derivative | The surface of a reference taken whole | Pastiche (1.7) |
| Overworked | Polish and devices added past the point of clarity | Restraint scores 1 or 2 |
| Underworked | The idea is there and the craft has not arrived | Craft scores 1 or 2 with Idea 4 or 5 |

## Terms introduced

- 180-degree line: the imaginary line between two subjects that the camera stays on one side of, keeping screen directions consistent.
- 30-degree rule: change camera angle by at least about 30 degrees, or shot size clearly, between consecutive shots of one subject, to avoid a jump cut.
- Articulation: the capacity to name the property, value and reference behind a judgement.
- Articulation ladder: the four rungs of a note: feeling, location, mechanism, fix with reference.
- Banding: visible steps in a smooth gradient from limited bit depth or compression.
- Braintrust: Pixar's group of experienced filmmakers who give candid notes on work in progress and hold no authority over it.
- Coherence: consistency of one system across touchpoints, formats and scripts.
- Content Credentials: provenance metadata attached to media under the C2PA standard.
- Copywork: rebuilding a master's work to measurement to learn its decisions.
- Core Web Vitals: Google's loading, responsiveness and stability measures: LCP (good at 2.5 seconds or less), INP (200ms or less; replaced First Input Delay in 2024) and CLS (0.1 or less), at the 75th percentile.
- Craft tell: a specific execution defect that a trained eye reads as unattended work.
- Critical Response Process: Liz Lerman's four-step feedback method (1990): statements of meaning, artist as questioner, neutral questions, permissioned opinions.
- Crushed blacks: shadow detail clipped to zero on the waveform.
- Currency: how current a piece reads against the wave of rising, peaking, dead and timeless looks.
- Discrimination: telling better from worse in a pair, in about two seconds.
- Emptiness: in Kenya Hara's sense, a receptive state the viewer fills, distinct from simplicity.
- Exposure: the stock of filed references the eye compares against.
- Eyeline: the direction of a character's gaze, which must match across cuts.
- Faux style: bold, italic or small caps synthesised by software from another style.
- Feedback sandwich: praise, criticism, praise; replaced in the house by the design-taste critique format.
- Font flash: fallback text visibly replaced by the web font as it loads.
- Grid drift: elements slipping a few pixels off their columns through nudging.
- Hierarchy: the order in which the eye takes in elements.
- Homage and pastiche: homage borrows one principle of a reference and applies it to new content; pastiche takes the surface without the principle.
- I like, I wish, what if: a three-prompt group feedback format (keep, missing, possibility) used in design-thinking teaching.
- Idea bank: the archive of killed routes, filed with brief, idea, reason and tags.
- Idea gate: the rubric rule capping the score at 2.5 when Idea scores below 3.
- Inevitability: the sense that no other solution was possible.
- Ira Glass gap: the period in which a maker's taste exceeds their ability to execute.
- Kerning pair: a spacing adjustment between two specific letters.
- Misregistration: misalignment between printing plates, seen as coloured fringes.
- Muddy mids: midtones gone grey-brown through poor interpolation or black overlays.
- Negative space: the unoccupied area that shapes and isolates elements.
- Nerve: the willingness to say the verdict aloud and remove what fails.
- Neutral question: a question with no opinion inside it.
- OKLCH: a perceptually uniform colour space described by lightness, chroma and hue, used for building neutrals and interpolating gradients.
- One accessory rule: after the edit, remove one more decorative element.
- Optical alignment: aligning by perceived edge, so round and pointed forms overshoot the measured line.
- Optical centre: the point slightly above geometric centre at which content looks centred.
- Pace: the speed of information or cuts over time.
- Pencil: D&AD's award levels, from Shortlist through Wood, Graphite and Yellow to Black.
- Perceptual tests: the two-second, squint, cover the logo, flip, grey, print it out, phone at arm's length, thumbnail, mute, overnight and other-room checks (4.8).
- Permissioned opinion: an opinion offered only after the maker agrees to hear it.
- Rag: the profile of the ragged edge of unjustified text.
- Register: the formality and cultural tone the work speaks in.
- Remove until it breaks: hiding elements one at a time until the idea or function fails, then restoring the last.
- RERA: India's Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016, whose section 11(2) requires the project registration number in advertising.
- Resolution: the sense that a piece arrives and ends.
- Restraint: choosing fewer elements, effects and words than the maker could.
- Rhythm: the pattern of repetition and interval across a page, sequence or edit.
- Rich black: black built from K plus other inks for density.
- River: a vertical white channel through justified text.
- Runt: a single word alone on the last line of a paragraph or headline.
- Saturation creep: saturation rising by small increments across rounds.
- Severity rating: the S0 to S4 urgency scale, adapted from Nielsen's 0 to 4 usability scale.
- Site of the Day: Awwwards' daily award to the highest jury-scored site.
- Skin tone line: the vectorscope reference along which healthy skin of every complexion falls.
- Specificity: the degree to which a choice belongs only to this brand.
- Studio crit: the six-stage critique (present, clarify, observe, interpret, evaluate, suggest) after a silent look.
- Sunk-cost fallacy: letting unrecoverable past investment drive a future decision.
- Tabular figures: numerals of equal width, for aligned columns.
- Tension: a deliberate imbalance that holds attention.
- Three-year test: asking of each element whether it will embarrass the client in three years.
- Trakheesi: the Dubai property advertising permit whose number must appear on listings.
- Transcreation: rewriting copy in another language for meaning and register.
- Unintended alignment: edges nearly aligned or tangent, read as error.
- Warp wobble: rubbery distortion from over-applied stabilisation.
- Weight: the visual mass of an element.
