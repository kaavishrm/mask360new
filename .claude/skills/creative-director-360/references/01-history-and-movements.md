## 1. How to use a lineage

### 1.1 Why a creative director needs the dead

Every look a client asks for in 2026 ("make it feel Deco", "something Swiss", "that Bauhaus thing", "brutalist but luxury") is a compressed memory of a movement that had dates, a technology, an enemy and a politics. A creative director (CD) who knows the lineage can name the exact thing worth borrowing and the thing that will read as costume. One who does not will paste the surface and the surface is the cheapest part. The design-taste skill (`references/canon.md`) lists who sets taste now; this file lists where their moves came from, so a reference board can cite the origin and skip the imitators.

Three questions settle every movement in this file, and they are the three questions to ask of any reference on a board:

1. What technology made it possible (chromolithography, phototypesetting, the Macintosh, the GPU)? A look borrowed without its technology turns into a filter.
2. What was it against? Arts and Crafts was against the steam press; Swiss style was against the subjective; punk was against polish; the 2020s tactile turn is against machine-generated smoothness. The opposition is the meaning.
3. What did it become once it was absorbed by commerce? Bauhaus became IKEA, Constructivism became the Nike poster, Memphis became the 2016 start-up illustration. Knowing the absorbed form tells you how worn the reference already is.

### 1.2 The format of each entry

Each movement below carries, in order: dates and place with key figures; visual DNA (the recognisable formal habits); the enabling technology and the ideology; representative works; and a borrow-now and leave verdict written for Mask360's categories (luxury hospitality, fashion, beauty, jewellery, real estate, F&B, events, agency showcase) across Mumbai, Jaipur, Delhi, Goa, Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Riyadh. Dates are given as spans with "to". Where a date or a credit is contested, the entry says so.

### 1.3 Reading order

A CD building a reference board reads section 14 first (how to cite without pastiche), then the movement the brief gestures at, then the timeline in section 13 to see what typeface and brand sat beside it. Type history in detail lives in 03-typography.md; brand identity method in 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md; campaign craft in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md; motion production in 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md. This file stops where those start.

## 2. Movements 1850 to 1920: craft, line and the poster

### 2.1 Arts and Crafts (Britain, about 1860 to 1910)

- Dates, place, figures: William Morris (1834 to 1896) founded Morris, Marshall, Faulkner and Co in 1861 and the Kelmscott Press in 1891. John Ruskin's chapter "The Nature of Gothic" in "The Stones of Venice" (1853) supplied the argument. The Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society (1887) gave the name. Walter Crane, C. R. Ashbee (Guild of Handicraft, 1888), Edward Burne-Jones, Emery Walker.
- Visual DNA: dense flat pattern from plant forms; the page as one integrated object (type, border, illustration cut to the same weight); heavy blackletter-leaning roman types; the double-page spread treated as a unit; natural dyes and visible joinery in furniture.
- Technology and ideology: a deliberate refusal of the steam-powered press and the division of labour. Morris cut his own types (Golden, 1890; Troy, 1892; Chaucer, 1892), used hand presses and handmade paper. The ideology was socialist and moral: joy in labour, honesty of material, the medieval workshop as the model of good work. The paradox, which Morris admitted, was that the output was priced for the rich.
- Representative works: the Kelmscott Chaucer (1896, 87 woodcuts by Burne-Jones); Morris's wallpapers "Trellis" (1862) and "Strawberry Thief" (1883); Ashbee's silver.
- Borrow now: material honesty, the spread as one object, the rule that ornament must arise from structure. These feed the 2020s tactile turn (section 7.12) and any heritage jewellery or textile brand in Jaipur that wants its handwork read as a value. Leave: the density and the medievalism. A full Morris page on a 2026 screen reads as a tea tin.

### 2.2 Art Nouveau (Europe, about 1890 to 1910)

- Dates, place, figures: Alphonse Mucha (Paris; the "Gismonda" poster for Sarah Bernhardt, December 1894, launched his career; "Job" cigarette papers, 1896). Aubrey Beardsley (London; "Salome" illustrations, 1894; art editor of "The Yellow Book", 1894 to 1895). Jules Chéret (the first colour lithographic posters from 1866, about a thousand posters). Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec ("Moulin Rouge: La Goulue", 1891). Hector Guimard (Paris Métro entrances, 1900). Regional names: Jugendstil (Germany), Secession (Vienna), Modernisme (Barcelona, Gaudí), Stile Liberty (Italy).
- Visual DNA: the whiplash line (a sinuous, asymmetric curve drawn from plant stems and hair); flat colour areas with a contour; lettering drawn as part of the image; the female figure as the carrier of the product; vertical poster formats.
- Technology and ideology: chromolithography (colour printing from several hand-drawn limestone plates, one per colour, which made large flat colour cheap) and the Paris poster-hoarding economy. Ideologically a search for a style that owed nothing to historical revival, allied to the idea of total design across architecture, furniture and print.
- Representative works: Mucha's "Job"; Beardsley's "The Peacock Skirt"; Chéret's "Folies-Bergère" posters; Gaudí's Casa Batlló (1904 to 1906).
- Borrow now: lettering integrated into the image (a current move in Jaipur jewellery and Goa hospitality marks when drawn by a lettering artist, see 03-typography.md); the single flat colour behind a figure. Leave: the vine, the swooning woman and the Mucha frame; they are the stock visual of every "vintage" cocktail menu in Dubai and read at once as costume.

### 2.3 Vienna Secession and the Wiener Werkstätte (Austria, 1897 to 1932)

- Dates, place, figures: the Secession founded 1897 by Gustav Klimt, Koloman Moser, Josef Hoffmann and Joseph Maria Olbrich (the Secession building, 1898, with the motto "To every age its art, to art its freedom"). The journal "Ver Sacrum" (1898 to 1903) used a square format. The Wiener Werkstätte (Vienna Workshops) founded 1903 by Hoffmann, Moser and the financier Fritz Waerndorfer, running to 1932.
- Visual DNA: the square as module; grids of small squares (the Hoffmann checkerboard); geometric ornament held inside strict borders; gold and black; very tall narrow letterforms; symmetry; white space used as a material.
- Technology and ideology: lithography and fine printing plus a workshop economy that made furniture, metalwork, textiles and postcards under one art direction, which is the first modern example of a brand system across every touchpoint. The ideology was Gesamtkunstwerk (the total work of art, every object of a space designed to one idea), inherited from Wagner and the Arts and Crafts guilds, with aristocratic clients.
- Representative works: Olbrich's Secession building; Hoffmann's Palais Stoclet (Brussels, 1905 to 1911) with Klimt's frieze; Moser's "Ver Sacrum" layouts; Hoffmann's Sitzmaschine chair (1905).
- Borrow now: the square module and the discipline of ornament inside borders, which is the correct historical reference for a luxury hospitality identity that wants pattern without noise (see 04-colour.md for gold restraint). Leave: the literal checkerboard and gold-on-black, which the 2010s hotel bar already used to death.

### 2.4 Plakatstil, the object poster (Germany, 1905 to about 1920)

- Dates, place, figures: Lucian Bernhard's "Priester" matches poster, 1906 (entered in a competition run by the Berlin printer Hollerbaum und Schmidt; two matches, the word Priester, nothing else). Ludwig Hohlwein in Munich (Marco Polo tea, Hermann Scherrer sportswear, 1908 onwards). Hans Rudi Erdt, Julius Gipkens, Julius Klinger.
- Visual DNA: one object, drawn flat and large; one brand name in hand-drawn block letters; two to four flat colours; no slogan, no figure, no setting. Sachplakat ("object poster") is the German term.
- Technology and ideology: lithography again, plus the street as a medium where a poster is read in two seconds from a tram. The ideology was commercial reduction: the shortest route from eye to product name. It is the direct ancestor of the one-object luxury post.
- Representative works: Bernhard's "Priester" (1906), "Stiller" shoes (1908), "Bosch" spark plugs (1914); Hohlwein's "Audi" (1912).
- Borrow now: this is the historical licence for the Jacquemus or Aesop-style single object on a flat ground with the wordmark, which the design-taste skill names as timeless. A fragrance, a bag, a bottle of Goa feni, a Jaipur ring: the Sachplakat is the format. Leave: nothing; the only error is adding a second element.

### 2.5 Futurism (Italy, 1909 to about 1944)

- Dates, place, figures: Filippo Tommaso Marinetti published "The Founding and Manifesto of Futurism" on the front page of "Le Figaro" on 20 February 1909. Fortunato Depero (Campari advertising from 1926; the Campari Soda conical bottle, 1932; the bolted book "Depero Futurista", 1927). Giacomo Balla, Umberto Boccioni, the typographic experiments of "Zang Tumb Tumb" (1914).
- Visual DNA: parole in libertà ("words in freedom": type set at angles, in mixed sizes and faces, as sound and speed on the page); diagonals; repeated outlines to show movement; mechanical, toy-like figures in Depero's commercial work; saturated primaries and black.
- Technology and ideology: the letterpress case broken open (a compositor could mix any faces and rotate them by tilting the forme), plus the motor car, the aeroplane and the telephone as subjects. The ideology glorified speed, youth, violence and war, and the movement allied itself with Italian fascism; this is the part a board must never carry.
- Representative works: Marinetti's "Zang Tumb Tumb"; Depero's Campari posters and the 1932 bottle (still in production); Balla's "Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash" (1912).
- Borrow now: typographic dynamism (size and angle as voice), the origin of today's kinetic type (section 7.12); Depero's toy figure as the ancestor of the character-led F&B identity. Leave: the politics, and the diagonal-everywhere layout, which reads as a 1990s energy drink.

### 2.6 Dada (Zurich and Berlin, 1916 to about 1924)

- Dates, place, figures: Cabaret Voltaire, Zurich, February 1916 (Hugo Ball, Tristan Tzara, Hans Arp). Berlin Dada: John Heartfield (photomontage covers for the magazine "AIZ", 1930 to 1938, including "Adolf the Superman: Swallows Gold and Spouts Junk", 1932), Hannah Höch, Raoul Hausmann, George Grosz. Hanover: Kurt Schwitters, whose "Merz" (a private art-and-design practice built from collage and found print, named from a fragment of the word Kommerz) ran as a magazine from 1923 to 1932.
- Visual DNA: photomontage (cut photographs recombined to make a new argument); collage of tickets, type scraps and newsprint; type set in collision; chance as a method; the anti-aesthetic deliberately ugly page.
- Technology and ideology: cheap mass-printed photographs, scissors and paste, and the illustrated weekly as a distribution channel. The ideology was anti-war, anti-bourgeois and anti-art; Berlin Dada was explicitly political; Schwitters kept Merz largely apolitical.
- Representative works: Heartfield's "AIZ" covers; Höch's "Cut with the Kitchen Knife" (1919); Schwitters's "Merz" issues and the "Ursonate" (1922 to 1932).
- Borrow now: photomontage as argument (one cut, one idea) and the collage texture that the 2020s tactile turn rediscovered; see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md for the hand-cut counter-signal. Leave: chaos as a pose. Dada without a target is a mood board.

## 3. Movements 1909 to 1945: the avant-gardes and the machine

### 3.1 De Stijl (Netherlands, 1917 to 1931)

- Dates, place, figures: the journal "De Stijl" founded in Leiden in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg. Piet Mondrian (Neoplasticism: painting reduced to horizontals, verticals, primaries and non-colours), Gerrit Rietveld (Red Blue chair, designed 1918, painted 1923; Schröder House, Utrecht, 1924), Bart van der Leck, Vilmos Huszár. Van Doesburg's introduction of the diagonal (Elementarism, 1924) caused the break with Mondrian.
- Visual DNA: asymmetric balance on an orthogonal grid; black lines of varying weight; red, yellow, blue, black, white and grey only; rectangles that run off the edge; sans-serif type built on a square grid (van Doesburg's 1919 alphabet).
- Technology and ideology: nothing new in the press; the movement was an idea about universal harmony after the First World War, Calvinist in its austerity, with the belief that a purely abstract order could reorganise life.
- Representative works: Mondrian's "Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow" (1930); the Schröder House; van Doesburg's "Café Aubette" interiors (Strasbourg, 1928).
- Borrow now: the asymmetric grid with one line off the edge, which is still the best way to give a static page tension and balance at once (see 06-ui-design-craft.md on layout). Leave: the primaries as decoration. Yves Saint Laurent's Mondrian dress (1965) was the first luxury borrow and it closed the door; any further literal use is a quotation of the quotation.

### 3.2 Constructivism (Russia, 1919 to about 1934)

- Dates, place, figures: Alexander Rodchenko (the 1924 Lengiz "Books" poster with Lilya Brik shouting; Kino-fot; Mayakovsky's Dobrolet and Mosselprom advertising, 1923 to 1925), El Lissitzky ("Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge", 1919 to 1920; the Proun paintings, "projects for the affirmation of the new", from 1919; "For the Voice", 1923, set with the compositor's case furniture only), Varvara Stepanova (textile and sportswear designs, 1923 to 1924), Lyubov Popova, Gustav Klutsis (photomontage), the Stenberg brothers (film posters, 1923 to 1933).
- Visual DNA: diagonal composition; red and black with white paper; bold grotesque capitals; heavy rules as structural members; photomontage of the worker, the machine and the crowd; circles and wedges as symbols of force.
- Technology and ideology: letterpress rules and bars (Lissitzky built pages from the printer's furniture), the camera and photomontage, cinema and the agitprop train (agitation-propaganda: the state's use of design to carry political instruction). The ideology was that the artist should abandon easel painting and go into production for the revolution.
- Representative works: Rodchenko's "Books"; Lissitzky's "Red Wedge"; Stepanova's sports clothing; the Stenbergs' poster for "Man with a Movie Camera" (1929).
- Borrow now: the photomontage with type, the diagonal used once, red and black as the most legible two-colour system that exists. Nike, Adidas and every sports brand have been drawing on this since the 1980s, so in sports and events it is close to worn. In luxury it is still fresh because almost nobody uses it there. Leave: the raised fist and the revolutionary cliché.

### 3.3 Bauhaus (Germany, 1919 to 1933)

- Dates, place, figures: founded in Weimar in 1919 by Walter Gropius; moved to Dessau in 1925 (the Gropius building, 1926); to Berlin in 1932; closed under Nazi pressure in 1933. Directors: Gropius (1919 to 1928), Hannes Meyer (1928 to 1930), Ludwig Mies van der Rohe (1930 to 1933). Johannes Itten ran the Vorkurs (the preliminary course in materials, colour and form that every student took before specialising) until 1923; László Moholy-Nagy took it over and brought photography, photograms and the New Typography; Herbert Bayer ran the printing workshop and drew the Universal typeface (1925, lowercase only, geometric); Josef Albers taught the Vorkurs from 1928 and later wrote "Interaction of Color" (1963); Joost Schmidt drew the 1923 Bauhaus exhibition poster; Marcel Breuer's tubular steel Wassily chair (1925); Marianne Brandt's metalwork; Gunta Stölzl's weaving.
- Visual DNA: geometric sans-serif type, lowercase; circle, square, triangle in red, blue, yellow; asymmetric layout with heavy rules; photograms and photomontage; products stripped to function; the glass curtain wall.
- Technology and ideology: industrial production as the destination for design (the 1923 slogan "Art and technology: a new unity"); the workshop system; photography as the new drawing. Politics moved from expressionist craft mysticism (Itten) to socialist functionalism (Meyer) to apolitical formalism (Mies).
- Representative works: the Dessau building; Bayer's Universal; the Bauhausbücher series (14 volumes, 1925 to 1930, designed by Moholy-Nagy); Breuer's chairs; Wilhelm Wagenfeld's table lamp (1924).
- Borrow now: the Vorkurs as a method (every designer learns material, colour and form before style; see 00-curriculum-and-method.md), and the principle that the system comes before the artefact. Leave: the Bauhaus costume. The circle-square-triangle in primaries and the Universal lowercase have been a poster shop staple since the 2019 centenary; on a 2026 board they read as a design-school T-shirt.

### 3.4 The New Typography and Tschichold's reversal (Germany and Switzerland, 1925 to 1950)

- Dates, place, figures: Jan Tschichold (1902 to 1974) edited the special issue "elementare typographie" of "Typographische Mitteilungen" in October 1925 and wrote "Die neue Typographie" (1928), the rulebook of asymmetric sans-serif modernism. Arrested in Munich in 1933, he moved to Basel. From about 1946 he argued, in a public exchange with Max Bill, that the New Typography's rigidity was itself authoritarian and he returned to centred classical setting; he then wrote the Penguin Composition Rules (1947 to 1949, for Penguin Books, standardising over 500 titles) and drew Sabon (1967, released jointly by Linotype, Monotype and Stempel so that it set identically on all three systems).
- Visual DNA (New Typography): one sans-serif, asymmetry, flush-left ragged-right, strong rules, photography in the role drawing once had, standardised DIN paper sizes (DIN 476, 1922, the A-series), the page as a machine for reading. Visual DNA (late Tschichold): centred title pages, old-style romans, classical margins (the Van de Graaf canon as he reconstructed it: a geometric construction that places the text block so that it and the margins share the page's proportions), restraint.
- Technology and ideology: the standardisation movement (DIN), the printing trade press, the ideology of objectivity. The reversal matters more than the rulebook: it is the first great designer admitting that a dogma had become a style.
- Representative works: the 1925 issue; the poster "Der Berufsphotograph" (1938); the Penguin Shakespeare titles (1949); Sabon.
- Borrow now: both halves. Asymmetric sans for the digital surface, classical centred setting for the invitation, the menu and the book, with the knowledge that the choice is a tool. Leave: the belief that one of them is morally correct.

### 3.5 Art Deco (France, then global, 1920 to 1940)

- Dates, place, figures: named retrospectively from the 1925 Paris "Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes". A. M. Cassandre (Adolphe Mouron; "Nord Express", 1927; "Dubonnet", 1932; "Normandie", 1935; the typefaces Bifur, 1929, and Peignot, 1937), Paul Colin, Jean Carlu, Charles Loupot, Tamara de Lempicka, Jean Dunand (lacquer), Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann (furniture). Architecture: the Chrysler Building (1930), Miami Beach, and Mumbai, which holds one of the largest Deco ensembles in the world: the Marine Drive and Oval Maidan buildings (1930s), Eros Cinema (1938), Regal Cinema (1933), inscribed with the Victorian Gothic ensemble as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018. Indian Deco adds Hindu and Mughal motifs and is sometimes called Indo-Deco.
- Visual DNA: stepped forms (the ziggurat); sunbursts and fans; streamlines; airbrushed gradients; chrome, lacquer, exotic veneers; geometric sans and stencil letters (Bifur); the monumental machine (ship, train, aeroplane) seen from below.
- Technology and ideology: the airbrush, new materials (Bakelite, chrome plating, plywood), ocean liners and the luxury industries of a Paris that sold modernity to the rich. The ideology was decorative modernism: the machine age as glamour, with none of the Bauhaus social programme.
- Representative works: Cassandre's "Normandie"; Ruhlmann's cabinets; the Chrysler lobby; Eros Cinema's facade.
- Borrow now: Cassandre's reduction of a vessel to three planes and the Mumbai Deco proportions (stepped parapets, porthole windows, vertical fins) as a specific local reference for a South Mumbai hotel, residence or restaurant; see 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md for place-rooted identity. Leave: the Gatsby fan, the gold-foil sunburst and the Broadway typeface. Every wedding invitation in Delhi and every "roaring twenties" event in Dubai has used them; they are the most worn reference in the luxury event category.


### 3.6 Emigration: how modernism left Europe and reached America and India (1933 to 1965)

- What happened: the Nazi closure of the Bauhaus in 1933 scattered its faculty. Josef and Anni Albers went to Black Mountain College, North Carolina, in 1933. Moholy-Nagy founded the New Bauhaus in Chicago in 1937 (later the Institute of Design). Gropius and Breuer went to the Harvard Graduate School of Design in 1937. Mies went to the Armour Institute (later IIT) in Chicago in 1938. Herbert Bayer reached New York in 1938 and moved to Aspen in 1946 for Walter Paepcke's Container Corporation of America, whose "Great Ideas of Western Man" advertisements (from 1950) were the first sustained corporate patronage of modernist artists. Ladislav Sutnar (from Prague, 1939) built information design for Sweet's Catalog Service. Alexey Brodovitch had arrived from Paris in 1930.
- The India channel: the same modernism reached India through architecture and policy. Le Corbusier planned Chandigarh (1951 to 1965) and built four works in Ahmedabad (Mill Owners' Association, 1954; Sanskar Kendra; Villa Sarabhai; Villa Shodhan, both mid 1950s). Balkrishna Doshi worked in Le Corbusier's Paris atelier from 1951 to 1954 and won the Pritzker Prize in 2018. Louis Kahn designed the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad (1962 to 1974). The Eames India Report of 1958 (section 10.2) came through the same Ahmedabad network of the Sarabhai family.
- Why it matters now: a Mumbai, Ahmedabad or Delhi client can cite Indian modernism (Doshi, Kahn, Correa, Le Corbusier's Ahmedabad) at first hand. That reference is specific to place and carries authority that a borrowed Bauhaus costume cannot. See 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md on place-rooted identity.

## 4. Movements 1945 to 1975: order, identity and the idea

### 4.1 Swiss and International Typographic Style (Zurich and Basel, about 1950 to 1975)

- Dates, place, figures: Max Bill (Bauhaus student 1927 to 1929; his 1946 essay "Über Typografie" started the public quarrel with Tschichold; first rector of Ulm). Josef Müller-Brockmann (Zurich; Tonhalle concert posters from the early 1950s, "Beethoven" 1955, "der Film" 1960; the book "Grid Systems in Graphic Design", 1981). Emil Ruder (taught at the Basel Allgemeine Gewerbeschule from 1942; "Typographie", 1967). Armin Hofmann (Basel; "Graphic Design Manual", 1965; Stadttheater Basel posters, "Giselle" 1959). Richard Paul Lohse (painter of serial colour systems). Karl Gerstner ("Designing Programmes", 1964; co-founded the agency GGK). Wim Crouwel (Amsterdam; Stedelijk Museum posters 1964 to 1985; the "New Alphabet" for early cathode-ray displays, 1967; co-founded Total Design, 1963). The journal "Neue Grafik" (1958 to 1965), edited by Lohse, Müller-Brockmann, Hans Neuburg and Carlo Vivarelli, was the movement's organ.
- The typefaces: Akzidenz-Grotesk (Berthold, 1898) was the house face until 1957, when two answers arrived in the same year: Neue Haas Grotesk by Max Miedinger with Eduard Hoffmann (Haas Type Foundry, renamed Helvetica in 1960) and Univers by Adrian Frutiger (Deberny et Peignot, launched as a planned family of 21 numbered weights and widths, the first type family designed as a system).
- Visual DNA: a modular grid (a fixed set of columns, rows and gutters that every element snaps to); flush-left, ragged-right setting (lines aligned on the left edge and left uneven on the right, so word spacing stays even); one grotesk (a sans-serif of the nineteenth-century German lineage); objective photography in place of illustration; asymmetric balance; extreme scale contrast between headline and text; white space treated as an active element.
- Technology and ideology: Swiss trade schools, a precise print industry, and from the 1960s phototypesetting (type set photographically on film, which freed spacing from metal). The ideology was objectivity and universality: the designer as a neutral transmitter of information. It suited multinationals and it suited Switzerland, which has four national languages and needed one system to carry German, French and Italian at once.
- Representative works: Müller-Brockmann's "Beethoven" and "Schützt das Kind!" (1953); Hofmann's "Giselle"; Crouwel's "Vormgevers" poster (1968), which prints its own grid; Ruder's "Typographie" spreads.
- Borrow now: the grid as the backbone of a bilingual system. An English and Arabic identity for Abu Dhabi, or an English and Devanagari one for Delhi, needs a single grid that two reading directions and two script heights share; the Swiss method is the only one built for that problem (see 03-typography.md for multiscript setting). The design-taste skill lists the Swiss grid with a confident grotesk as timeless; this is the reason. Leave: the claim of neutrality. Every grid carries a voice, and Helvetica set "neutrally" in 2026 reads as a corporate default. Leave also the pastiche Swiss poster with a rotated circle and a red rule, which every design student produced in the 2010s.

### 4.2 The Ulm School (Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm, 1953 to 1968)

- Dates, place, figures: founded in Ulm in 1953 by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher and Max Bill, in memory of Hans and Sophie Scholl of the White Rose resistance. Bill was rector until 1956; Tomás Maldonado then moved the school from Bauhaus-style art training towards a science-based method (semiotics, ergonomics, operations research, systems theory). Hans Gugelot ran product design; Horst Rittel taught design methodology. The state withdrew funding and the school closed in 1968.
- Visual DNA: systems before artefacts; modular product families; pictogram sets; one sans-serif; grey, white and one signal colour; the design manual as a deliverable.
- Technology and ideology: industrial mass production and the corporate client. The ideology was rational, socially responsible design and a refusal of "styling" (cosmetic redesign to drive sales). Its legacy as a teaching model is covered in section 12.11.
- Representative works: the Braun SK 4 radio-phonograph (Gugelot and Dieter Rams, 1956, nicknamed "Snow White's coffin" for its clear lid); Lufthansa's identity (Aicher and his Gruppe E5, 1962 to 1963: the crane in a circle, yellow and blue); the Munich 1972 Olympics (Aicher: pictograms on a 45 and 90 degree grid, a palette of light blue, green, silver and orange that excluded red and black, set in Univers); Aicher's Rotis typeface (1988).
- Borrow now: the manual and the pictogram family are the direct ancestors of design tokens and component libraries (see 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md). For a hotel group or an events client with wayfinding, Munich 1972 is still the reference for a pictogram system. Leave: grey modernism without wit; Ulm produced rigour and very little delight.

### 4.3 Mid-century modern (United States and Scandinavia, about 1945 to 1969)

- Dates, place, figures: Charles and Ray Eames (moulded plywood LCW chair, 1946; "House of Cards", 1952; the Eames Lounge Chair, 1956; the film "Powers of Ten", final version 1977). George Nelson (design director of Herman Miller 1945 to 1972; ball clock, 1948; the Marshmallow sofa drawn by Irving Harper, 1956). Alexander Girard (Herman Miller textiles from 1952; Braniff International's "End of the Plain Plane" identity, 1965). Eero Saarinen (Tulip chair, 1956; TWA Flight Center, 1962). Florence Knoll, Isamu Noguchi (coffee table, 1947). Scandinavia: Arne Jacobsen (Egg chair, 1958; the SAS Royal Hotel, Copenhagen, 1960, where he designed the building, furniture, cutlery and door handles), Hans Wegner (Wishbone chair, about 1950), Finn Juhl.
- Visual DNA: organic geometry; warm wood with saturated accents; tapered, splayed legs; open plans; in graphics, playful illustration and loose collage.
- Technology and ideology: wartime materials and processes turned to domestic use (moulded plywood, fibreglass, cast aluminium); the postwar housing boom; design-led manufacturers (Herman Miller, Knoll). The ideology was democratic optimism, summed up in the Eames aim of "the best for the most for the least".
- Borrow now: the SAS Royal Hotel model, one mind across architecture, furniture and tableware, is the strongest historical precedent for selling a luxury hotel identity as a total environment. Leave: walnut, mustard and the sputnik lamp. Mid-century was the default interior of the boutique café from Bandra to Jumeirah between about 2015 and 2022, so it now reads as rental furniture.

### 4.4 The New York School (about 1940 to 1975)

- Paul Rand (1914 to 1996): "Direction" magazine covers (1938 to 1945); "Thoughts on Design" (1947); IBM (1956, striped version 1972), Westinghouse (1960), UPS (1961), ABC (1962), the "Eye-Bee-M" poster (1981), NeXT (1986, a reported 100,000 US dollar fee for a single solution presented in a bound book). His position is in section 12.6.
- Alvin Lustig (1915 to 1955): New Directions book covers from 1941, abstract modernist images on mass paperbacks.
- Alexey Brodovitch (1898 to 1971): art director of Harper's Bazaar 1934 to 1958; his Design Laboratory classes trained Richard Avedon and Irving Penn; "Portfolio" magazine (three issues, 1950 to 1951); the photobook "Ballet" (1945). He borrowed Diaghilev's instruction to his collaborators, "astonish me".
- Herb Lubalin (1918 to 1981): expressive typography, which he called typographics (letterforms drawn and set so tightly that the word becomes an image); "Avant Garde" magazine (1968 to 1971); ITC Avant Garde Gothic with Tom Carnase (1970); co-founded the International Typeface Corporation (ITC) in 1970 with Aaron Burns and Edward Rondthaler; "U&lc" magazine (from 1973); the "Mother & Child" logo (1965). Tight display setting was enabled by Photo-Lettering Inc. (1936) and phototype.
- Milton Glaser (1929 to 2020) and Push Pin: Push Pin Studios founded 1954 by Glaser, Seymour Chwast, Edward Sorel and Reynold Ruffins; the "Push Pin Graphic" (1957 to 1980); Glaser's Bob Dylan poster (1966, folded into the "Greatest Hits" album); co-founded New York magazine with Clay Felker (1968); I Love NY (1977, the heart drawn as a pictogram). Chwast's "End Bad Breath" anti-war poster (1967). Glaser died on 26 June 2020, his 91st birthday.
- George Lois: Esquire covers 1962 to 1972, including Muhammad Ali as Saint Sebastian (April 1968): the cover as a single argument.
- Visual DNA: the big idea (one visual concept that makes the argument, usually a pun or a substitution); wit; type and image fused; American eclecticism (Victorian wood type beside modernist photographs); the designer as author.
- Technology and ideology: offset lithography, phototype and the mass magazine; the ideology reconciled European modernism with American commerce and humour.
- Borrow now: Rand's single visual pun under strict discipline is the most useful lesson here for a jewellery or F&B identity, where one witty mark outlasts a decorative one. Lubalin's tight wordmark setting remains the standard for display logotypes. Leave: the Push Pin psychedelic revival and the 1970s ITC look (swash capitals, crowded ligatures), which now read as a retro filter.

### 4.5 The corporate identity era (about 1955 to 1985)

- Chermayeff and Geismar: founded 1957 as Brownjohn, Chermayeff and Geismar by Ivan Chermayeff, Tom Geismar and Robert Brownjohn (who left in 1960). Chase Manhattan Bank (1960, an abstract octagon, one of the first non-figurative marks for a major American company), Mobil (1964, the red "o"), PBS (1984), NBC (peacock, 1986). The firm has been Chermayeff and Geismar and Haviv since Sagi Haviv became a partner.
- Saul Bass (1920 to 1996) as identity designer: Continental Airlines (1968), Bell System (1969), Quaker Oats (1969), Warner Communications (1972), United Airlines (1974), Girl Scouts (1978), AT&T globe (1983). His title sequences are in section 8.2.
- Unimark International: founded in Chicago in 1965 by Ralph Eckerstrom, Massimo Vignelli, Bob Noorda, James Fogleman, Jay Doblin and Larry Klein, as an identity consultancy built on Swiss method. Vignelli and Noorda wrote the New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual (1970, reissued in facsimile in 2014). Vignelli's diagrammatic subway map (1972) was withdrawn in 1979 for its geographic distortion. His American Airlines identity (1967) ran until 2013.
- Lippincott: founded 1943 by J. Gordon Lippincott with Walter Margulies (long called Lippincott and Margulies). Coca-Cola's "dynamic ribbon" device (1969); the naming research behind Exxon (1972), whose interlocking-X logo came from Raymond Loewy (1966).
- Landor: founded 1941 by Walter Landor in San Francisco; worked from the ferryboat Klamath from 1964; Alitalia (1969); FedEx (1994, Lindon Leader, with the arrow in the negative space between E and x). Landor merged with Fitch in 2020.
- Raymond Loewy: Lucky Strike pack (1940), Air Force One livery (1962), Shell (1971).
- Visual DNA: a symbol plus a logotype plus a standards manual; abstract geometric marks; strict colour specification; one typeface family; applications from stationery to vehicles governed by the manual.
- Technology and ideology: the multinational corporation, television, the jet airline and the motorway. The ideology was corporate modernism: a company should speak with one coherent voice everywhere it appears, and design is how management makes that voice visible.
- Borrow now: the discipline of the standards manual and the idea that a mark gains meaning from use. A Dubai or Mumbai real estate developer needs exactly this rigour across hoardings, sales galleries, brochures, apps and site signage. Leave: the 1970s corporate mark as a style for a fashion or beauty brand. An abstract monogram in a circle reads as a bank.

## 5. National schools: Poland, Japan, Italy, Britain, the Netherlands

### 5.1 The Polish poster school (about 1950 to 1980)

- Dates, place, figures: Henryk Tomaszewski (professor at the Warsaw Academy of Fine Arts from 1952), Jan Lenica ("Wozzeck", 1964), Roman Cieślewicz (the Polish release poster for "Vertigo", 1963), Waldemar Świerzy (Jimi Hendrix, 1974), Franciszek Starowieyski, Wojciech Fangor, Józef Mroszczak. Warsaw hosted the world's first International Poster Biennale in 1966, and the Poster Museum at Wilanów opened in 1968 as the first museum devoted to posters.
- Visual DNA: painterly, hand-made images; lettering drawn as part of the picture; a surreal metaphor that interprets the film or play; dark humour; limited flat colour.
- Technology and ideology: state monopoly publishers (the art publisher WAG and the film distributor CWF) commissioned posters without commercial pressure, so artists had unusual freedom; the poster became one of the few licensed spaces for individual expression under communism.
- Borrow now: the metaphor poster for film, theatre, music and cultural events. For a Dubai Opera season or a Mumbai festival, one surreal image that interprets the programme beats a collage of performer photographs. Leave: the gloom, which reads as Eastern European pastiche when applied to hospitality.

### 5.2 Japanese design (1950 onwards)

- Yusaku Kamekura (1915 to 1997): the Tokyo 1964 Olympic posters, including the red sun over gold rings and the 1962 photographic poster of sprinters leaving the blocks, shot by Osamu Hayasaki at night; founded the Japan Design Center in 1960.
- Tadanori Yokoo (born 1936): pop, psychedelia and ukiyo-e (Edo-period woodblock prints) colliding with rising-sun rays; "Having Reached a Climax at the Age of 29, I Was Dead" (1965); theatre posters for Shuji Terayama; a solo show at MoMA in 1972.
- Kohei Sugiura (born 1932): editorial and book design of dense, layered information; diagrammatic maps; Asian iconography; guest professor at HfG Ulm 1964 to 1967.
- Ikko Tanaka (1930 to 2002): the "Nihon Buyo" poster (1981), a geisha face built from flat geometric planes; founding art director of Muji in 1980.
- Koichi Sato (1944 to 2016): posters of glowing gradients and suspended light, a meditative counterpoint to Yokoo.
- Kenya Hara (born 1958): art director and board member of Muji since 2001; "Designing Design" (English edition 2007) and "White" (English edition 2010); the 2003 Muji "Horizon" campaign photographed on the salt flats of Uyuni, Bolivia. His theory of emptiness is in section 12.9.
- Muji: Mujirushi Ryohin ("no-brand quality goods"), launched in 1980 by the Seiyu supermarket group under Seiji Tsutsumi with 40 products (9 household, 31 food), with Ikko Tanaka, copywriter Kazuko Koike and interior designer Takashi Sugimoto. Naoto Fukasawa's wall-mounted CD player for Muji (1999) is the canonical object of the "without thought" approach (design that fits behaviour so closely it goes unnoticed).
- Nendo: founded 2002 in Tokyo by Oki Sato; the Cabbage Chair (2008, made from waste pleated paper for Issey Miyake); the Tokyo 2020 Olympic cauldron (lit in 2021).
- Also in the lineage: Issey Miyake (Pleats Please, 1993), Comme des Garçons and its magazine "Six" (1988 to 1991), Shiro Kuramata ("Miss Blanche" chair, 1988), and Yohji Yamamoto's catalogues with Peter Saville and Nick Knight (from 1986).
- Visual DNA: ma (the interval: the charged space between things, treated as a component of the design); asymmetric balance; one red circle; extreme refinement of paper and print; at the other pole, Sugiura's and Yokoo's density.
- Technology and ideology: a postwar national project of quality, a print industry of great precision, and aesthetic traditions (ma, wabi-sabi, section 12.10) that value restraint and the incomplete.
- Borrow now: ma and Hara's emptiness for luxury hospitality and wellness; Muji's tone of voice (statements without adjectives) for beauty and home brands; Tanaka's geometry for monograms. Leave: zen kitsch, the bamboo stalk, the pebble stack and the brush-drawn enso circle, which fill UAE spa branding.

### 5.3 Italian design (1945 to 1990)

- Olivetti: under Adriano Olivetti (president 1938 to 1960) design was corporate culture. Giovanni Pintori led publicity from 1936 to 1968; Marcello Nizzoli designed the Lettera 22 portable typewriter (1950, winner of the first Compasso d'Oro in 1954); Ettore Sottsass designed the Elea 9003 computer (1959) and, with Perry King, the red Valentine typewriter (1969); Mario Bellini designed the Divisumma 18 calculator (1973). The Fifth Avenue showroom in New York (1954) was by the architects BBPR. Ivrea, the company town, has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2018.
- Bruno Munari (1907 to 1998): Futurist beginnings, the "useless machines" of the 1930s, children's books ("Nella notte buia", 1956), the 1964 Campari poster for the opening of the Milan metro (the brand name repeated in dozens of typefaces), and "Design as Art" (Penguin, 1971).
- Bob Noorda (1927 to 2010): signage for Milan Metro Line 1 (1964, with the architects Franco Albini and Franca Helg; a red band at eye height carrying the station name, Compasso d'Oro 1964); the New York subway with Vignelli; the Regione Lombardia "Rosa Camuna" mark (1975, with Pino Tovaglia, Roberto Sambonet and Munari).
- Ettore Sottsass: see Memphis, section 6.3.
- Around them: Gio Ponti (founded "Domus" in 1928; Superleggera chair, 1957; Pirelli Tower, 1958), Achille and Pier Giacomo Castiglioni (Arco lamp, 1962), the Compasso d'Oro award (from 1954, created by La Rinascente), Fiorucci (1967), and Tibor Kalman's "Colors" magazine for Benetton (1991).
- Visual DNA: humanist, sensual modernism; colour and wit inside a rigorous structure; the object as sculpture; the poster as abstraction.
- Technology and ideology: a craft-based manufacturing network around Milan and Turin, and Olivetti's community capitalism (design, architecture, libraries and worker welfare as one programme). The corporate patron as cultural institution is the model behind the luxury foundations, Fondazione Prada (Milan, 2015, by OMA) among them.
- Borrow now: the Olivetti model for a Mask360 client that wants to be a cultural patron: publish, build, commission. Munari's play for children's, F&B and event work. Leave: the "dolce vita" pastiche of Riviera stripes, lemons and Vespas, the default summer campaign since the Aperol era.

### 5.4 British graphic design (1960 onwards)

- Alan Fletcher (1931 to 2006): Fletcher/Forbes/Gill (1962, with Colin Forbes and Bob Gill); Pentagram (1972, with Forbes, Theo Crosby, Kenneth Grange and Mervyn Kurlansky), whose partner-owned structure with no chief executive is still its model; Reuters (1965); the V&A logo (1989); "The Art of Looking Sideways" (2001).
- Peter Saville (born 1955): Factory Records from "FAC 1" (1978); Joy Division's "Unknown Pleasures" (1979, the stacked radio pulses of pulsar CP 1919, lifted from the "Cambridge Encyclopaedia of Astronomy"); New Order's "Blue Monday" (1983, die-cut sleeve after a floppy disk); "Power, Corruption and Lies" (1983, a Fantin-Latour still life with a colour-block code); Yohji Yamamoto catalogues with Nick Knight (from 1986); the Calvin Klein logo with Raf Simons (2017); the Burberry wordmark and TB monogram (2018, section 7.7).
- Malcolm Garrett: the Buzzcocks' "Orgasm Addict" sleeve (1977, with Linder Sterling's collage).
- Neville Brody (born 1957): art director of The Face (1981 to 1986) and Arena (1987 to 1990); co-founded FontFont with Erik Spiekermann (1990); the experimental type publication FUSE (1991); dean of communication at the Royal College of Art (2011 to 2018). The Face was founded by Nick Logan in 1980, the same year Terry Jones founded i-D with its winking cover.
- Others in the line: Hipgnosis ("The Dark Side of the Moon", 1973), Barney Bubbles (Stiff Records), Jock Kinneir and Margaret Calvert (British road signs and the Transport typeface, introduced from 1965 after the Worboys report; Rail Alphabet, 1965), Wolff Olins (1965; the London 2012 logo, 2007), Jonathan Barnbrook (David Bowie's "Blackstar", 2016).
- Visual DNA: wit (Fletcher); the record sleeve as art object and the found image recast (Saville); style-press typography (Brody); public information of great clarity (Calvert).
- Technology and ideology: the music industry's budgets, the independent label, the style magazine and the public sector client. The ideology mixed art-school conceptualism with pop.
- Borrow now: Saville's method of appropriation with a code, a found image recast through one systematic intervention, is the model for using archival imagery for a heritage brand (a Jaipur jeweller's family photographs, an old Bombay hotel's ledgers). Calvert's legibility for wayfinding. Leave: the Brody-era 1980s constructivist type revival, unless the brief is the 1980s.

### 5.5 Dutch design (1960 onwards)

- Roots: Crouwel and Total Design (1963; Schiphol airport signage with Benno Wissing, 1967). The 1972 debate at Museum Fodor in Amsterdam between Crouwel (the designer as neutral organiser) and Jan van Toorn (the designer as a visible, subjective commentator) framed Dutch design for the next fifty years. R. D. E. (Ootje) Oxenaar designed Dutch banknotes from 1966 to 1985, an example of the state as an ambitious design client.
- Studio Dumbar: founded by Gert Dumbar in The Hague in 1977; the PTT (Dutch post and telecom) house style (1989); the Dutch police identity with its diagonal livery stripes (1993); staged photography of graphic elements. The studio now trades as Studio Dumbar/DEPT.
- Experimental Jetset: founded 1997 in Amsterdam by Marieke Stolk, Erwin Brinkers and Danny van den Dungen; the "John and Paul and Ringo and George" T-shirt in Helvetica (2001); the Whitney Museum's "Responsive W" identity (2013), a single zigzag line that changes width to fit any format.
- Irma Boom (born 1960): the SHV Think Book (1996, 2,136 pages, five years of work, no page numbers, designed to be read from back to front through time); "Sheila Hicks: Weaving as Metaphor" (2006, with deckled edges); the "No 5 Culture Chanel" book (2013, printed entirely without ink, its content blind-embossed); the Rijksmuseum identity (2013). She was the youngest laureate of the Gutenberg Prize (2001).
- Thonik: founded 1993 by Nikki Gonnissen and Thomas Widdershoven; blunt colour blocks and oversized type for cultural and political clients, including the Dutch Socialist Party.
- KesselsKramer (1996, Erik Kessels and Johan Kramer): the long-running Hans Brinker Budget Hotel campaign, which advertises it as the worst hotel in the world; the "I amsterdam" slogan (2004), whose giant letters were removed from the Museumplein in 2018. Droog (1993, Gijs Bakker and Renny Ramakers), with Tejo Remy's chest of drawers "You Can't Lay Down Your Memory" (1991).
- Visual DNA: conceptual clarity; systems with a personal voice; the book as a physical object; colour used bluntly; humour.
- Technology and ideology: a plain, Protestant culture of directness, generous state cultural subsidy and a government that commissioned design for stamps, money and police.
- Borrow now: Irma Boom's book-as-object for a client's brand book, a hotel's anniversary volume or an agency showcase; KesselsKramer's honesty for an anti-luxury beach property in Goa that sells its own imperfection. Leave: Dutch quirk applied to a brand with no sense of humour.

## 6. Postmodernism, punk, grunge and the end of print

### 6.1 Wolfgang Weingart and Swiss punk (Basel, 1968 to the 1990s)

- Dates, place, figures: Wolfgang Weingart (1941 to 2021) taught typography at the Basel school from 1968 and set out to break Ruder's rules from inside the institution. His covers for "Typografische Monatsblätter" (1972 to 1973) and his layered film-positive posters of the late 1970s defined what Americans would call New Wave. Students and associates: Dan Friedman, April Greiman.
- Visual DNA: letterspaced and stepped type; type set at many weights in one line; layered halftone screens and film overlays; collage made photomechanically.
- Technology and ideology: the darkroom and film positives as a layout tool, just before the computer. Ideologically, Weingart kept the Swiss commitment to structure and attacked its sterility.
- Borrow now: layering with discipline. Leave: the stepped-type cliché.

### 6.2 April Greiman and the California New Wave (1976 to 1990)

- Greiman (born 1948) studied in Basel with Weingart and Hofmann (1970 to 1971), set up in Los Angeles in 1976, and was among the first designers to treat the Macintosh as a medium. "Design Quarterly" 133 (1986), titled "Does It Make Sense?", folded out into a life-size poster of her own body, digitised and composed on a Mac. With "WET" magazine and the Los Angeles scene she made the computer's bitmap and video texture visible as a material.
- Borrow now: the pixel as an honest material (the house pixel face Departure Mono sits in this line; see design-taste, section 7). Leave: the 1980s pastel and triangle layer.

### 6.3 Memphis (Milan, 1981 to 1987)

- Dates, place, figures: founded on 11 December 1980 in Ettore Sottsass's apartment, named after Bob Dylan's "Stuck Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again", which was playing. The first show opened on 18 September 1981 at the Arc '74 gallery in Milan. Members included Michele De Lucchi, Nathalie Du Pasquier (the patterns), George Sowden, Martine Bedin, Matteo Thun, Aldo Cibic, Marco Zanini, with guests Shiro Kuramata, Masanori Umeda, Peter Shire, Michael Graves and Arata Isozaki. Sottsass left in 1985 and the group dissolved in 1987 to 1988.
- Visual DNA: plastic laminate (from Abet Laminati) used as a noble material; clashing printed patterns (Sottsass's "Bacterio", 1978); primaries against pastels; squiggles; terrazzo; furniture as totem (Sottsass's Carlton room divider, 1981).
- Technology and ideology: cheap industrial surfaces and a Milan furniture industry able to make small runs. The ideology was anti-functionalist: design as communication, pleasure and a deliberate offence to good taste. Collectors included Karl Lagerfeld (his Monte Carlo apartment) and David Bowie (his collection sold at Sotheby's in 2016).
- Afterlife: the flat, rubber-limbed illustration style that spread through tech from 2017, often traced to Facebook's "Alegria" system drawn by Buck, was nicknamed corporate Memphis. The design-taste skill lists it as dead; the original Memphis is a different thing and still potent in small doses.
- Borrow now: one Memphis gesture in a disciplined space (a laminate counter, a single pattern) for F&B and retail interiors. Leave: the full squiggle-and-confetti set, which reads as a 2016 start-up.

### 6.4 Cranbrook (Michigan, 1971 to 1995)

- Katherine and Michael McCoy co-chaired the design department at Cranbrook Academy of Art from 1971 to 1995 and brought post-structuralist theory (Derrida, Barthes: meaning is made by the reader, and a text can be read in layers) into layout, so a page could be both read and seen. The "Cranbrook Design: The New Discourse" volume (1990) is the summary document. Students and associates included Edward Fella, Jeffery Keedy, Allen Hori, Scott Makela and Elliott Earls.
- Borrow now: layered reading for a culture or art client whose audience wants to work. Leave: theory as decoration.

### 6.5 Emigre, Licko and VanderLans (Berkeley, 1984 to 2005)

- Rudy VanderLans (born 1955) and Zuzana Licko (born 1961) published "Emigre" magazine for 69 issues between 1984 and 2005. Licko drew bitmap faces for the 72 dpi Macintosh screen and dot-matrix printers (Emperor, Oakland and Universal, 1985), then outline faces once PostScript arrived: Matrix (1986), Base 9 and Base 12 (1995), Mrs Eaves (1996, a Baskerville revival named after Sarah Eaves, Baskerville's housekeeper and later wife) and Filosofia (1996, a Bodoni revival).
- The quarrel: Massimo Vignelli called the work garbage; Steven Heller's essay "Cult of the Ugly" ("Eye", 1993) attacked the cult of the illegible. Licko's reply, "you read best what you read most", is the most useful sentence in the debate: legibility is learnt, so a new form is less legible only until it becomes familiar.
- Borrow now: designing for the medium's real resolution (today: a 1080 pixel phone feed and a variable font). Mrs Eaves still appears on wedding stationery in Delhi; Filosofia is a credible Bodoni for jewellery. Leave: the 1990s Emigre display faces as nostalgia.

### 6.6 David Carson and Ray Gun (1989 to 1995)

- Carson (born 1955), a former professional surfer, art-directed "Beach Culture" (1989 to 1991) and "Ray Gun" (1992 to 1995; the magazine ran to 2000). He set a dull Bryan Ferry interview entirely in Zapf Dingbats, a symbol font, so it could not be read. "The End of Print" (1995, with Lewis Blackwell) became one of the best-selling graphic design books of its decade.
- Borrow now: the page as an emotional response to the content. Leave: illegibility as a style; it is unforgivable in a luxury sales document or an app.

### 6.7 Tomato (London, 1991)

- Founded in 1991 by Steve Baker, Dirk van Dooren, Karl Hyde and Rick Smith (of Underworld), Simon Taylor, John Warwicker, Graham Wood and Jason Kedgley. A collective working across type, film, music and advertising; the book "mmm... skyscraper I love you" (1994). Tomato's typographic film work for Underworld and for brands such as Nike and Levi's in the 1990s is the root of kinetic typography in advertising (section 7.12).
- Borrow now: the collective model of type, sound and image made by the same people. Leave: the 1990s rave typography.

### 6.8 Punk and DIY (1976 to 1984)

- Jamie Reid (1947 to 2023): ran the radical Suburban Press (from 1970); designed the Sex Pistols' "God Save the Queen" (1977, the Queen's portrait defaced, with ransom-note lettering) and "Never Mind the Bollocks" (1977, fluorescent yellow and pink). He drew on the Situationist International's détournement (hijacking an existing image or text so that it turns against its original meaning). Others: Linder Sterling's collage, Barney Bubbles, Raymond Pettibon's four-bar logo for Black Flag, Gee Vaucher for Crass, and the photocopied fanzine (Sniffin' Glue, 1976).
- Visual DNA: letters cut from newspapers; photocopier grain and contrast; Letraset (dry-transfer lettering, 1961) used crookedly; Dymo tape; safety pins; fluorescent spot colours.
- Technology and ideology: the office photocopier (the Xerox 914, 1959, became widely available in the 1970s), scissors and glue. The ideology was that anyone could make it, and that polish belonged to the enemy.
- Borrow now: photocopy texture and cut lettering are the exact counter-signal to AI smoothness that the design-taste currency file lists as rising. Use them in collateral for a Goa beach club or a fashion drop, made by hand and scanned. Leave: the anarchy symbol and the Union Jack. Luxury has already absorbed punk completely (Supreme lifted Barbara Kruger's Futura Bold Oblique on a red box for its 1994 logo), so the literal symbols read as merchandise.

### 6.9 Grunge (1990 to 1998)

- Music and image: Sub Pop (Seattle, 1988) and Charles Peterson's blurred live photographs; Carson's Ray Gun; distressed type such as FF Beowolf (1990, Erik van Blokland and Just van Rossum of LettError, whose outlines randomise at the moment of printing). Fashion: Marc Jacobs's grunge collection for Perry Ellis (spring 1993) cost him his job and became canonical; Corinne Day's photographs of Kate Moss for The Face (July 1990); Mario Sorrenti's Calvin Klein "Obsession" (1993), the start of "heroin chic", a label that drew justified criticism for glamorising emaciation.
- Borrow now: the snapshot, the unretouched face, the flash; these feed directly into Teller and Tillmans (section 11.1). Leave: distressed fonts as decoration, and anything that reads as glamorised illness.

## 7. The digital transition and the interface eras, 1984 to 2026

### 7.1 The Macintosh and desktop publishing (1984 to 1990)

- The machine: Apple launched the Macintosh on 24 January 1984 at 2,495 US dollars, with a 9-inch monochrome screen of 512 by 342 pixels at 72 dots per inch. Susan Kare drew its icons and its first bitmap fonts (Chicago, Geneva, Monaco), establishing the visual grammar of the desktop.
- The chain: the Apple LaserWriter (1985) with Adobe PostScript inside; Aldus PageMaker (1985), whose founder Paul Brainerd coined "desktop publishing" (page layout done by the designer on a personal computer, replacing the typesetting trade); Adobe Illustrator (1987); QuarkXPress (1987), which became the industry's layout standard through the 1990s; Photoshop 1.0 (February 1990, by Thomas and John Knoll).
- Consequence: within about five years typesetting moved from a trade of compositors and Linotype operators to the designer's desk. Control of spacing and kerning (adjusting the space between specific letter pairs) became the designer's responsibility, which is why craft standards dropped first and then rose. Emigre (section 6.5) is the art that came out of the constraint.

### 7.2 PostScript, Fontographer and the font as software

- Adobe was founded in December 1982 by John Warnock and Charles Geschke; PostScript (1984) is a page description language that describes type as scalable outlines, so one font file prints at any size on any device. Adobe Type Manager (1989) brought smooth outlines to the screen.
- Fontographer (Altsys, 1986) let anyone draw and generate a PostScript font on a Mac, which created independent digital foundries (Emigre, FontShop, Font Bureau). Apple's TrueType (1991, licensed to Microsoft) answered Adobe's Type 1 format; Microsoft and Adobe announced OpenType in 1996, a single cross-platform format with room for thousands of glyphs and rules for complex scripts.
- For India and the Gulf the decisive step was script shaping: Unicode 1.0 (1991) encoded Devanagari and Arabic, and OpenType layout features made it possible to render Devanagari conjuncts (combined consonant clusters) and Arabic contextual forms correctly. OpenType 1.8 (September 2016) added variable fonts (one file holding a continuous range of weights, widths or other axes), the basis of kinetic type (section 7.12). Adobe ended support for Type 1 fonts in its applications in January 2023. Detail is in 03-typography.md.

### 7.3 The web, 1995 to 2005

- Browsers: Mosaic (1993) and Netscape Navigator (1994). Layout was built with HTML (HyperText Markup Language) tables and transparent spacer GIFs until CSS (Cascading Style Sheets, the web's styling language) level 1 became a Recommendation of the W3C (World Wide Web Consortium) in December 1996. Designers worked inside a 216-colour "web-safe" palette and the Microsoft "Core fonts for the Web" (1996), which included Georgia and Verdana, both drawn by Matthew Carter for the screen.
- Flash: FutureSplash Animator (1996) was bought by Macromedia in December 1996 and became Flash, the medium of the experimental web: Joshua Davis's praystation, Yugo Nakamura's yugop.com, 2Advanced Studios, Hi-ReS!. The Designers Republic's graphics for the PlayStation game "Wipeout" (1995) set the techno look of the period. The iPhone (2007) shipped without Flash; Steve Jobs's open letter "Thoughts on Flash" (April 2010) ended its future, and Adobe ended Flash Player on 31 December 2020.
- Standards: Jeffrey Zeldman's "A List Apart" and the Web Standards Project (both 1998) argued for semantic HTML and CSS; Dave Shea's CSS Zen Garden (2003) showed one HTML document restyled by hundreds of designers, which converted the profession.
- Borrow now: the Flash era's conviction that a website is an experience is back with WebGL (browser access to the graphics processor), GSAP (the GreenSock Animation Platform, a JavaScript animation library) and scroll-driven animation (see 21-web-design-and-digital-craft.md). Leave: the splash page and the "skip intro" button.

### 7.4 Web 2.0 gloss (about 2004 to 2010)

- Name and date: Tim O'Reilly's Web 2.0 Conference (October 2004) popularised a term Darcy DiNucci had used in 1999. The visual roots were Apple's Aqua interface, shown in January 2000 for Mac OS X, which Steve Jobs said was designed so that "you wanted to lick it", and Windows Vista's Aero (2007).
- Visual DNA: glossy buttons with a highlight band; "wet floor" reflections under logos; rounded corners; "beta" badges and starbursts; pastel gradients; large friendly rounded type. The 2017 nostalgia label "Frutiger Aero" now names the look.
- Borrow now: almost nothing; the design-taste skill lists Y2K Frutiger Aero as dead. Its one lasting lesson is that gloss signalled novelty for about five years and then signalled age.

### 7.5 Skeuomorphism: iOS 1 to 6 (2007 to 2013)

- Definition: a skeuomorph is a design that keeps cues from the material or tool it replaces, used here as ornament (the stitched leather of a paper diary on a calendar app).
- Dates: the iPhone was announced on 9 January 2007 and shipped on 29 June 2007. The style peaked with iOS 5 and 6 and OS X Lion (2011): stitched leather in Calendar and Find My Friends, green felt in Game Center, a wooden shelf in Newsstand, a yellow legal pad in Notes. Scott Forstall, who championed it, left Apple after an announcement on 29 October 2012; Jony Ive took over human interface design.
- Reason it existed: touchscreens were new, and analogy taught novice users what to touch. Reason it ended: once the gesture vocabulary was learnt, the ornament became noise, and retina screens made fine flat typography legible.
- Borrow now: the material reference used once, as concept, which is the 2020s tactile turn (section 7.12). Leave: fake leather.

### 7.6 Flat design: Metro, iOS 7 and Material (2010 to about 2020)

- Microsoft Metro: developed through the Zune player (2006) and Windows Media Center, launched on Windows Phone 7 (October 2010) and Windows 8 (2012); typographic, built on the Segoe family, with live tiles, explicitly citing transit signage and the Swiss style.
- iOS 7: announced at WWDC (Apple's Worldwide Developers Conference) on 10 June 2013, released 18 September 2013 under Jony Ive; very thin weights of Helvetica Neue, translucency and layered planes in place of textures.
- Material Design: announced by Google at I/O on 25 June 2014, led by Matías Duarte; a "paper and ink" metaphor in which flat surfaces sit at defined elevations and cast real shadows. Material You (Material 3) arrived with Android 12 in 2021, bringing dynamic colour drawn from the user's wallpaper; Material 3 Expressive followed in May 2025.
- Enabling technology: retina displays (iPhone 4, 2010, 326 pixels per inch) made hairlines legible; responsive web design (Ethan Marcotte, "A List Apart", May 2010) needed assets that scale; flat assets were cheaper to build and maintain.
- Borrow now: the systematic thinking of Material, the documented elevation and motion specifications (see 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md). Leave: flat colour with no materiality, which made every app look the same.

### 7.7 The blanding era: sans-serif luxury logos (2012 to 2020)

- Definition: blanding (brand plus bland) is replacing a distinctive mark with a near-identical sans-serif wordmark, so that houses become typographically interchangeable.
- The cases: Hedi Slimane renamed Yves Saint Laurent's ready-to-wear Saint Laurent Paris in 2012 with a Helvetica wordmark, keeping Cassandre's YSL monogram (1961) for accessories. Balenciaga (2017, under Demna) and Rimowa (2017, after the LVMH acquisition) followed. Calvin Klein (2017, Saville with Raf Simons) went to wide capitals. In 2018 Celine dropped the accent on its name under Slimane, Balmain changed its mark under Olivier Rousteing, and Burberry replaced its serif and Equestrian Knight with a bold sans wordmark and TB monogram by Peter Saville under Riccardo Tisci. Zara's 2019 logo by Baron and Baron went the other way, a tightly kerned high-contrast serif, and drew as much ridicule for its crowded ligatures.
- The reasons given: legibility at small sizes (app icons, a 110 pixel Instagram avatar), a new creative director signalling a reset, alignment with streetwear and a younger customer, and simpler marks that reproduce cleanly.
- The cost: a sans wordmark carries almost no information, so the "cover the logo" test in the design-taste skill fails across the whole category, and the brand's equity has to live entirely in product and image.

### 7.8 The reversal: Burberry 2023 and the return of the crest and the serif

- Burberry: Daniel Lee, chief creative officer from October 2022, unveiled a new identity in February 2023: the Equestrian Knight Design (EKD, a knight on horseback carrying a pennant marked "Prorsum", Latin for "forwards", first drawn for the house in 1901) restored in Knight Blue, with a custom serif wordmark. A heritage asset that the 2018 reset had dropped came back within five years.
- The wider swing: expressive, high-contrast serifs and heraldic devices are rising (the design-taste currency file has the current reading). Two cautionary episodes bracket the moment. Jaguar's November 2024 rebrand ("Copy Nothing", a mixed-case geometric wordmark and no car in the launch film) was widely read as blanding at its most extreme. Cracker Barrel's August 2025 removal of its "Old Timer" figure was reversed within about a week after public backlash.
- Lesson for a CD: a heritage mark is an asset with a measurable recognition value, and removing it costs more than any new mark can earn back in the short term. Audit equity before simplifying (see 18-brand-strategy-and-identity-design.md).

### 7.9 Neumorphism (2019 to 2020)

- Definition: neumorphism ("new skeuomorphism", also called soft UI) renders controls as soft extrusions or depressions of the background, lit by one light and one dark shadow on a surface of the same colour.
- Origin: Alexander Plyuto's Dribbble shot "Skeuomorph Mobile Banking" (December 2019); Michal Malewicz's article "Neumorphism in user interfaces" (UX Collective, December 2019) named and spread it.
- Why it died: the controls have almost no contrast with the background, so they fail success criterion 1.4.11 of WCAG 2.x (the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines), which requires non-text contrast of at least 3:1 for interface components. It was a Dribbble style that could not ship. CRED's NeoPOP system (section 10.7) is the Indian descendant that solved the contrast problem with hard edges and saturated colour.

### 7.10 Glassmorphism (2020 to 2023)

- Definition: glassmorphism is the use of frosted, translucent panels that blur what lies behind them, built on the web with the CSS backdrop-filter property.
- Lineage: Windows Vista Aero Glass (2007), iOS 7 (2013), Microsoft's Fluent Design acrylic (2017), and macOS Big Sur (November 2020); Malewicz popularised the name in late 2020. Browser support arrived in Safari 9 (2015, prefixed), Chrome 76 (2019) and Firefox 103 (2022).
- Status: the design-taste skill lists glass cards as dead and glass and blur as peaking. The reason: glass reads as a default UI kit unless the material itself is the concept.

### 7.11 Brutalism and anti-design on the web (2014 onwards)

- Name: Pascal Deville's brutalistwebsites.com (2014) borrowed the name from architectural Brutalism (from béton brut, raw concrete; Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, 1952; Reyner Banham's essay "The New Brutalism", 1955).
- Visual DNA: default system fonts and blue underlined links; visible structure; monospace; hard edges and no shadows; raw grids; deliberate awkwardness. Print precedent: "Bloomberg Businessweek" under Richard Turley (2010 to 2014). Accidental precedents: Craigslist and the Drudge Report.
- Ideology: a reaction against the kit-built, flat, frictionless web of the 2010s and against conversion-optimised sameness.
- Status: the hairline brutalist editorial grid is peaking (design-taste currency). For a luxury client it works only when the content is strong enough to carry no ornament: an art gallery, a fashion archive, a typographic agency showcase.

### 7.12 The 2020s tactile and kinetic turn (2020 to 2026)

- Tactile: scanner artefacts, film grain, risograph print (a stencil duplicator, introduced by Riso Kagaku in 1986, prized for its misregistration and soy inks), letterpress, hand-cut collage, uncoated paper. The trigger is generative AI: DALL-E 2 (April 2022), Midjourney's open beta (July 2022), Stable Diffusion (August 2022) and ChatGPT (30 November 2022) made a smooth, perfect image free, so evidence of a hand became the scarce signal. This is the Arts and Crafts argument (section 2.1) restated for a new machine.
- Kinetic: variable fonts (2016) let weight and width animate; identities now move as a first state, with motion designed together with the mark from the first sketch; the root is Futurism (section 2.5) and Tomato (section 6.7). Real-time tools carry it: GSAP (acquired by Webflow in 2024 and made free, including its plugins, in 2025), Rive, Lottie (section 8.8), Unreal Engine 5.
- Faux out-of-home (faux OOH: a CGI, or computer-generated imagery, film that shows an impossible object in a real street, presented as if it were a real installation) became a luxury format in 2023: Jacquemus bags driving through Paris as buses, Maybelline's mascara brush on a London Underground train.
- Borrow now: the hand, made for real and scanned, and one kinetic idea per identity. Leave: fake grain overlays and fake paper textures applied as filters; the audience reads them as AI decoration. Generative production craft is in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md.

### 7.13 Liquid Glass (Apple, 2025)

- Announcement: Apple introduced Liquid Glass on 9 June 2025 at WWDC25 as a translucent system material across iOS 26, iPadOS 26, macOS Tahoe 26, watchOS 26 and tvOS 26, the first time Apple numbered all its platforms by year. Apple's newsroom said the design draws on the depth and dimensionality of visionOS; Alan Dye, then vice president of Human Interface Design, presented it.
- Behaviour: the material refracts and reflects the content beneath it (lensing), shows specular highlights that respond to motion, and floats controls and navigation as a separate functional layer above content. Early betas drew criticism for legibility over busy backgrounds; Apple added a user setting to make the material more opaque in a later 26-series update (verify the current controls in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md).
- People: Alan Dye left Apple in December 2025 to become chief design officer of Meta's Reality Labs; Stephen Lemay succeeded him as head of human interface design.
- What it means for a CD: Apple's glass has a functional job (it separates the control layer from the content layer and lets content run edge to edge), which decorative glassmorphism never had. On Apple platforms, use the system material through native components and test contrast. In brand and web work, treat glass as peaking and do not imitate the system material on a hero or a card; it reads as a borrowed operating system.

## 8. Motion graphics history

### 8.1 Precursors: abstract film (1920s to 1960s)

- Oskar Fischinger ("An Optical Poem", MGM, 1938), Len Lye (painting directly on film, "A Colour Box", 1935, for the GPO Film Unit), Norman McLaren (National Film Board of Canada from 1941) and John Whitney (mechanical analogue computer animation; "Catalog", 1961; the spirals for Bass's "Vertigo" titles, 1958) established that rhythm, colour and abstract form could carry feeling on screen without a story. Every motion identity descends from them.

### 8.2 Saul Bass and the title as prologue (1954 to 1995)

- Bass turned the opening credits into a compressed statement of the film's idea. "The Man with the Golden Arm" (1955, Otto Preminger): a jagged cut-paper arm, the poster and titles built from one symbol. "Vertigo" (1958) with Whitney's spirals; "North by Northwest" (1959), type sliding along the grid of a glass facade; "Anatomy of a Murder" (1959); "Psycho" (1960), with Bass's later claim to have shaped the shower scene disputed by Hitchcock's camp. With Elaine Bass in a second career: "Goodfellas" (1990), "Cape Fear" (1991), "The Age of Innocence" (1993), "Casino" (1995).
- Lesson: one graphic metaphor, set up in the first seconds, that the film then pays off. The first five seconds of a hotel launch film or a jewellery brand film are a Bass problem.

### 8.3 Maurice Binder and the Bond sequences (1962 to 1989)

- Binder created the gun-barrel opening for "Dr. No" (1962) and designed the titles of 14 Bond films, from "Dr. No" and "Thunderball" (1965) to "Licence to Kill" (1989): silhouetted bodies, coloured light, water and weapons. Robert Brownjohn made the two he did not ("From Russia with Love", 1963, credits projected onto a dancer's body; "Goldfinger", 1964). Daniel Kleinman took over from "GoldenEye" (1995).
- Lesson: a repeated sequence format becomes a brand asset across decades. The gun barrel is a fixed opening ritual; a hospitality group's film series can own one in the same way.

### 8.4 Pablo Ferro (1935 to 2018)

- Hand-drawn, elongated lettering over B-52 refuelling footage for "Dr. Strangelove" (1964); the multi-panel split screens of "The Thomas Crown Affair" (1968); the quick-cut trailer for "A Clockwork Orange" (1971); later "Men in Black" and "Good Will Hunting" (both 1997).
- Lesson: hand lettering in motion feels authored in a way set type never does; a wedding, fashion or F&B film can carry a lettering artist's hand.

### 8.5 Broadcast design and MTV (1981 to 1995)

- MTV launched on 1 August 1981. Its logo by Manhattan Design (Frank Olinsky, Pat Gorman, Patty Rogoff) kept a fixed block "M" whose fill changed endlessly, the first mainstream dynamic identity (a logo whose structure stays fixed while its surface or content changes). Station idents were commissioned from dozens of artists and studios, which made the channel a gallery.
- UK parallels: Martin Lambie-Nairn's Channel 4 ident (1982, flying coloured blocks assembling into the 4, an early use of computer animation, produced with Bo Gehring Associates in Los Angeles) and his BBC Two "2" idents (1991). Quantel's Paintbox (1981) put digital paint in broadcast graphics departments.
- Lesson: a dynamic identity lets a brand be different every week and still recognisable; Amul's topical hoardings (section 9.12) are the print version.

### 8.6 Kyle Cooper and Imaginary Forces (1995 onwards)

- Kyle Cooper's titles for David Fincher's "Se7en" (1995), made at R/Greenberg Associates, used hand-scratched type on film, jittering frames and a Nine Inch Nails remix to put the killer's obsession on screen before the story started. Cooper, Peter Frankfurt and Chip Houghton founded Imaginary Forces in 1996; Cooper left in 2003 to found Prologue. Imaginary Forces later made the titles for "Mad Men" (2007) and "Stranger Things" (2016).
- Lesson: texture and process (scratching, photocopying, printing) can carry character; the 2020s tactile turn has the same root.

### 8.7 The After Effects studio era: MK12, Psyop, Buck (1995 to 2015)

- Adobe After Effects (1993, by the Company of Science and Art, acquired by Aldus and then Adobe by 1994) and Maxon's Cinema 4D made the small motion studio viable. The vocabulary of "mograph" (motion graphics as a discipline joining design, animation and compositing) formed around studios and the forum culture of the 2000s.
- MK12 (Kansas City, late 1990s): the interface graphics of "Stranger than Fiction" (2006) and the titles of "Quantum of Solace" (2008). Psyop (New York, 2000): Coca-Cola's "Happiness Factory" (2006, with Wieden+Kennedy Amsterdam). Buck (Los Angeles, 2004): broadcast, product and brand animation, including Facebook's "Alegria" illustration system (2017). Universal Everything (Matt Pyke, Sheffield, 2004) and ManvsMachine (London, 2007) carried design-led motion into product launches for brands such as Nike and Apple. The archive of record for title design is Art of the Title (from 2007).
- Lesson: motion studios are design studios first. Brief them with the identity's logic (grid, colour, rhythm), and the motion system will outlive the launch film. Production method is in 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md.

### 8.8 Lottie and the real-time era (2015 to 2026)

- Lottie: Hernan Torrisi released Bodymovin in 2015, an After Effects plugin that exports animation as JSON (a structured text data format) for rendering at runtime. Airbnb's engineers, with lead animator Salih Abdul-Karim, built the first Lottie renderers for iOS, Android and the web in 2017. dotLottie (proposed 2020) packs a Lottie file and its assets into one compressed container with the .lottie extension. The Lottie Animation Community was established in 2024 to standardise the format.
- Interactive and real-time: Rive (state-machine-driven interactive animation, where an animation responds to inputs and conditions), Unreal Engine 5 (released 2022) and LED-volume virtual production (Industrial Light and Magic's StageCraft on "The Mandalorian", 2019), TouchDesigner and Notch for live installations and concert visuals. Generative video (OpenAI's Sora shown in February 2024, Kuaishou's Kling from June 2024, Runway Gen-4 in 2025, Google's Veo 3 with synchronised sound in May 2025) is covered in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md.
- Lesson: motion is now a deliverable that runs in code, so the CD specifies it like a typeface: timing, easing, states, file budget. See 12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md.

## 9. Advertising history that shapes creative direction

### 9.1 Bernbach and DDB: the creative revolution (1949 to the 1970s)

- Doyle Dane Bernbach was founded on 1 June 1949 by Ned Doyle, Maxwell Dane and Bill Bernbach. Its structural invention was the creative team (a copywriter and an art director working as a pair from the first idea), replacing the old sequence in which copy was written and then handed to a layout department.
- Volkswagen "Think Small" (1959; art director Helmut Krone, copywriter Julian Koenig): a tiny car in an expanse of white space, a short headline, three columns of honest, self-deprecating copy. "Lemon" (1960) followed. Ad Age ranked "Think Small" first among the advertising campaigns of the twentieth century (1999). Avis "We try harder" (1962, copywriter Paula Green) turned second place into a promise.
- Lesson: honesty and white space sell a premium product to a sceptical audience. This is the advertising root of the design-taste rule that copy should not sell.

### 9.2 David Ogilvy (1948 onwards)

- Hewitt, Ogilvy, Benson and Mather opened in New York in 1948. The Hathaway shirt man with an eyepatch (1951) gave a product a story; Commander Whitehead for Schweppes (1953) made the client the face; the Rolls-Royce advertisement (1958) led with the line that at 60 miles an hour the loudest noise came from the electric clock. Books: "Confessions of an Advertising Man" (1963), "Ogilvy on Advertising" (1983). His most quoted rule, "the consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife", argued for respect and long, factual copy. The agency joined WPP in 1989.
- Lesson: research, a specific fact and a long headline can be luxurious. For real estate and hospitality copy, Ogilvy's specificity beats adjectives.

### 9.3 Leo Burnett (Chicago, 1935 onwards)

- Burnett founded his agency in Chicago in 1935 and preached the "inherent drama" of the product: find the truth inside the thing and dramatise it. He built America's mascots: Tony the Tiger (1952), the Marlboro Man (1955, which turned a filter cigarette marketed to women into a symbol of the frontier man), the Pillsbury Doughboy (1965).
- Lesson and warning: a character built with discipline can last seventy years; the Marlboro Man also shows how well iconography can sell harm. The CD owns the ethics of the image (section 12.14).

### 9.4 The British creative revolution (1960 to 1990)

- Collett Dickenson Pearce (CDP, 1960): Hamlet cigars ("Happiness is a cigar called Hamlet", 1960s); Heineken's "Refreshes the parts other beers cannot reach" (1974, copywriter Terry Lovelock); Hovis "Boy on the Bike" (1973, directed by Ridley Scott); the surreal Benson and Hedges gold-pack posters (from 1977, a response to rules that forbade showing smoking as attractive); Fiat Strada "Handbuilt by robots" (1979, directed by Hugh Hudson). CDP was a school for film: Alan Parker, Ridley Scott, Hugh Hudson and Adrian Lyne all made commercials there.
- Saatchi and Saatchi (1970): "Labour Isn't Working" for the Conservative Party (1978).
- Abbott Mead Vickers (1977; David Abbott, Peter Mead, Adrian Vickers): The Economist's white-on-red typographic posters (from 1988, "I never read The Economist. Management trainee. Aged 42."), a body of work that proves a brand can be built from typography and wit alone.
- BBH (Bartle Bogle Hegarty, 1982; John Bartle, Nigel Bogle, John Hegarty): Levi's 501 "Launderette" (1985, Nick Kamen, Marvin Gaye's "I Heard It Through the Grapevine"); the black sheep and "When the world zigs, zag" (from a 1982 Levi's black denim poster). Hegarty had earlier written Audi's "Vorsprung durch Technik" campaign at TBWA London (1982). Publicis took full ownership of BBH in 2012.
- Lesson: the British school proved that wit, craft and cinema could coexist with sales, and that a campaign line can carry a brand for decades.

### 9.5 Chiat/Day and Apple "1984"

- Chiat/Day was founded in Los Angeles in 1968 by Jay Chiat and Guy Day. "1984", directed by Ridley Scott (creative director Lee Clow, copywriter Steve Hayden, art director Brent Thomas), aired nationally once, during Super Bowl XVIII on 22 January 1984, two days before the Macintosh went on sale; Apple's board had nearly cancelled it. Lee Clow's team at TBWA\Chiat\Day made "Think Different" (1997) for Jobs's return.
- Lesson: a launch can be a cultural event bought once, in the one slot where everyone is watching. The Gulf equivalent is the Ramadan television slot (section 9.13).

### 9.6 Wieden+Kennedy (Portland, 1982 onwards)

- Dan Wieden and David Kennedy founded the agency in 1982 around Nike. "Revolution" (1987, with the Beatles' song) and "Just Do It" (1988; Wieden said the line was adapted from the last words of the murderer Gary Gilmore, "Let's do it"). Wieden+Kennedy London made Honda's "Cog" (2003, directed by Antoine Bardou-Jacquet, a real chain reaction of car parts filmed in long takes). "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" for Old Spice (2010) and Nike's "Dream Crazy" with Colin Kaepernick (2018) followed. The agency has stayed independent; Dan Wieden died on 30 September 2022.
- Lesson: a long relationship with one brand and a voice that belongs to the athlete or the user.

### 9.7 Fallon (Minneapolis, 1981 onwards)

- Pat Fallon and Tom McElligott founded the agency in 1981. BMW Films' "The Hire" (2001 to 2002): eight short films starring Clive Owen, directed by John Frankenheimer, Ang Lee, Wong Kar-wai, Guy Ritchie, Alejandro González Iñárritu, Joe Carnahan, John Woo and Tony Scott, distributed online, which founded branded entertainment (content commissioned by a brand to be watched for its own sake). Fallon London made Sony Bravia "Balls" (2005, Nicolai Fuglsig, about 250,000 bouncing balls on a San Francisco hill), Bravia "Paint" (2006, Jonathan Glazer) and Cadbury "Gorilla" (2007, Juan Cabral).
- Lesson: for a premium product, a film people choose to watch is worth more than impressions bought.

### 9.8 Crispin Porter + Bogusky (Miami)

- Founded by Sam Crispin in 1965; Alex Bogusky made it famous. The Mini USA launch (2002), the anti-smoking "truth" campaign (from 2000, with Arnold), Burger King's "Subservient Chicken" website (2004), "Whopper Freakout" (2007) and "Whopper Sacrifice" on Facebook (2009). IKEA's "Lamp" (2002, directed by Spike Jonze) won the Cannes Film Grand Prix.
- Lesson: the media placement and the stunt can be the idea; the work is an action in the world.

### 9.9 Droga5 (New York, 2006)

- Founded by David Droga in 2006. UNICEF "Tap Project" (2007), "The Great Schlep" for the Obama campaign (2008), and "Decode", which hid the pages of Jay-Z's memoir in physical locations searchable through Bing (2010). Accenture Interactive acquired Droga5 in 2019, a marker of consultancies buying creative agencies.
- Lesson: an idea that uses the platform's mechanics (search, maps, the book's pages) as the medium.

### 9.10 Mother (London, 1996)

- Founded in 1996 by Robert Saville, Mark Waites and partners around a kitchen table, with no account handlers in the conventional sense. KFC's "FCK" apology (February 2018, when UK restaurants ran out of chicken) is the canonical crisis response: the logo rearranged on a bucket, one line of copy, full ownership of the error.
- Lesson: humility and wit in a crisis; a logo used as a sentence.

### 9.11 Uncommon (London, 2017)

- Founded in 2017 by Nils Leonard, Lucy Jameson and Natalie Graeme as a creative studio that designs brands, products and campaigns together. British Airways' "A British Original" (2022) and its data-driven outdoor work for BA restored a flag carrier's confidence with portraiture and typography.
- Lesson: a campaign built as a typographic system can run for years across hundreds of formats; this is the model for an airline, hotel group or developer brand in the Gulf.

### 9.12 The Indian creative tradition

- Lintas: Lever International Advertising Services opened in India in 1939 as the agency of Hindustan Lever and became the training ground of Indian advertising.
- Alyque Padamsee (1928 to 2018): led Lintas India for about fourteen years; Liril's waterfall girl (1975), Surf's "Lalitaji" (the shrewd housewife, 1980s), Cherry Blossom's "Cherry Charlie", MRF's Muscleman. He played Jinnah in Richard Attenborough's "Gandhi" (1982) and was a central figure of English-language theatre in Mumbai. He died on 17 November 2018.
- Piyush Pandey (5 September 1955 to 23 October 2025) and Ogilvy India: born in Jaipur, joined Ogilvy in 1982 in client servicing, moved to creative and became the agency's national creative director, executive chairman for India and, from 2019, worldwide chief creative officer; he became chief advisor from 1 January 2024. His work made Hindi vernacular humour the mainstream language of Indian advertising: Fevicol (Pidilite) and Fevikwik, Cadbury Dairy Milk "Kuch Khaas Hai" (the cricket-field dance, 1993 to 1994), Asian Paints "Har Ghar Kuch Kehta Hai", the Hutch pug (2003), Pond's "Googly Woogly Woosh" (2010), the lyrics of "Mile Sur Mera Tumhara" (1988) and the BJP's "Abki Baar Modi Sarkar" (2014). Padma Shri in 2016; the Cannes Lion of St Mark in 2018, shared with his brother, the film director Prasoon Pandey. He died in Mumbai aged 70.
- Cadbury: from "Kuch Khaas Hai" to the Diwali 2021 campaign by Ogilvy Mumbai in which a machine-learning model generated personalised Shah Rukh Khan advertisements for small neighbourhood shops, one of the first large-scale creative uses of generative video and widely awarded internationally.
- Amul topicals: the Amul girl was conceived in 1966 to 1967, when Sylvester daCunha's agency ASP (Advertising and Sales Promotion) took the account; Verghese Kurien, chairman of the Gujarat Co-operative Milk Marketing Federation, asked for a mischievous little girl, and art director Eustace Fernandes drew her. Topical hoardings (posters commenting on the week's news with a pun) have run continuously for decades, including during the 1975 to 1977 Emergency, and continue under daCunha Communications. It is the longest-running proof that a fixed character plus a fixed format plus a news hook can be a brand.
- Air India's Maharaja: created in 1946 by Bobby Kooka, the airline's commercial director, and Umesh Rao of J. Walter Thompson; a moustached host who carried decades of witty posters and the airline's warmth. Salvador Dalí designed a porcelain ashtray for Air India in 1967 and asked to be paid with a baby elephant. After Tata took the airline back in 2022, a new identity, "The Vista" (inspired by the jharokha, the projecting enclosed balcony window of Rajasthani and Mughal architecture, in red, aubergine and gold), was unveiled on 10 August 2023, and the Maharaja was confined to selected premium touchpoints such as lounges and crockery.
- Titan and Tanishq: Titan (a 1984 joint venture of the Tata Group and the Tamil Nadu Industrial Development Corporation, watches launched in 1987 under founding managing director Xerxes Desai) became India's first design-led lifestyle brand and adopted the opening of Mozart's Symphony No. 25 as its sonic logo (a short, repeated sound signature that identifies a brand). Tanishq (launched 1994 to 1995) made the jewellery advertisement a cultural argument: "Remarriage" (2013, Lowe Lintas) showed a dusky bride with a daughter from an earlier marriage; "Ekatvam" (October 2020), an interfaith baby shower, was withdrawn after a backlash, a reminder that Indian jewellery advertising operates in a contested public space.
- CRED's "Indiranagar ka gunda" with Rahul Dravid (2021, during the Indian Premier League cricket season) shows the D2C generation using the same vernacular wit with celebrity casting against type.

### 9.13 The Gulf

- Networks: Impact BBDO (founded in Beirut in 1971, now with its regional base in Dubai), Leo Burnett's Middle East network (Leo Burnett MEA), FP7 (later FP7 McCann), Memac Ogilvy and TBWA\RAAD built the regional industry from Beirut, Dubai and Riyadh.
- Showcase: Dubai Lynx, the region's main creative awards festival, has run since about 2006 in association with the Cannes Lions organisation (verify the current ownership before citing).
- Formats and moments: the Ramadan television and digital slot is the region's Super Bowl, when telecoms, banks and food brands release their long-form emotional films; Emirates' "Hello Tomorrow" positioning (2012, StrawberryFrog) reframed an airline as a global cosmopolitan idea. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 (announced April 2016) and Riyadh Season (from 2019) created a new demand for event, entertainment and cultural branding. Regulatory context (UAE Media Council rules, influencer licensing) is in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md and 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md.
- Lesson: bilingual craft (Arabic and English designed together from the first sketch), family and national pride as emotional registers, and the seasonal calendar (Ramadan, Eid, National Days, Diwali for the Indian diaspora) as the structure of the year.

### 9.14 What the advertising canon teaches a CD

| Source | Year | Principle | Application for Mask360 |
|---|---|---|---|
| DDB, "Think Small" | 1959 | Honesty plus white space | Luxury copy that admits a truth |

## 10. Indian design history

### 10.1 Before independence: print, the bazaar and the calendar (1800 to 1947)

- Type and press: the Serampore Mission Press (from 1800, under William Carey, with the punchcutter Panchanan Karmakar) cut and cast early Indian-script types; the Nirnaya Sagar Press (Bombay, 1864, founded by Javaji Dadaji) set the Devanagari book face that defined Indian printing for about a century.
- Image: the Calcutta Art Studio (1878) and Raja Ravi Varma's Fine Art Lithographic Press (Bombay, 1894, moved to Malavli near Lonavala in 1899) mass-produced chromolithographs of Hindu gods and heroines. This calendar art (devotional and popular prints sold in bazaars and given away as calendars, later printed in volume at Sivakasi) became the visual source of Indian packaging, cinema posters and religious imagery. Christopher Pinney's "Photos of the Gods" (2004) is the standard study.
- Schools: the Sir J. J. School of Art (Bombay, 1857), where John Lockwood Kipling taught from 1865, later added a commercial art department that fed the city's advertising and film industries.
- Architecture as graphic memory: Bombay's Victorian Gothic (Victoria Terminus, now Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, completed 1888) and its Art Deco (section 3.5), together a UNESCO site since 2018.

### 10.2 NID and the Eames India Report (1958 onwards)

- The report: at the invitation of the Government of India, through Pupul Jayakar and with Ford Foundation support, Charles and Ray Eames travelled India and in April 1958 delivered "The India Report", which recommended a design institute rooted in Indian values and problems. Its emblem is the lota, the brass water vessel, offered as an object perfected by generations of use: form, material, balance and ritual in one.
- The institute: the National Institute of Design opened in Ahmedabad in 1961, set up by the central government with Gautam and Gira Sarabhai central to its founding; the designer and sculptor Dashrath Patel was founder-secretary until 1981; H. Kumar Vyas was the first full-time professor. Adrian Frutiger designed NID's logo in the 1960s as a visiting consultant, a direct line from Swiss typography (section 4.1) into Indian institutions.
- Teachers: Vikas Satwalekar (an NID graphic designer who later directed the institute) and Sudarshan Dhir (among its first graphic design teachers) shaped the curriculum that joined Swiss and Ulm method to Indian subjects. Their individual identity credits are poorly documented online; confirm attributions with NID's archive before citing them on a board.
- Wider network: the Industrial Design Centre at IIT Bombay (1969), where R. K. Joshi (1936 to 2008) researched Devanagari calligraphy and type; Amar Chitra Katha (1967, Anant Pai), which defined the drawn image of Indian mythology for generations of children.
- Identities of the era that a CD should know: the State Bank of India mark (1971, attributed to Shekhar Kamat of NID and said to be inspired by Kankaria Lake in Ahmedabad); the Doordarshan "eye" logo (attributed to the NID student Devashis Bhattacharya, about 1970); the rupee sign, chosen on 15 July 2010 from a public competition and designed by D. Udaya Kumar, a postgraduate of IIT Bombay, combining the Devanagari "ra" with the Latin "R".

### 10.3 Satyajit Ray as designer (1943 onwards)

- In 1943 Ray joined the British advertising agency D. J. Keymer in Calcutta as a junior visualiser under art director Annada Munshi, and in the same year began designing book covers for Signet Press, whose founder D. K. Gupta gave him full artistic freedom. Covers include Jibanananda Das's "Banalata Sen" and "Rupasi Bangla", Jawaharlal Nehru's "The Discovery of India" and Jim Corbett's "Man-Eaters of Kumaon". He illustrated "Aam Antir Bhepu", the children's edition of "Pather Panchali", which became his first film (1955).
- He revived the children's magazine "Sandesh" in 1961, drew its illustrations and Bengali display lettering, and wrote the Feluda stories for it. He designed four Latin typefaces (Ray Roman, Ray Bizarre, Daphnis and Holiday Script); Ray Roman and Ray Bizarre won an international competition in 1971. He designed his own film posters and titles and scored his own films from "Teen Kanya" (1961).
- Lesson: the director-designer, one mind across poster, title, set and music, is the Indian precedent for the 360 creative director this corpus describes.

### 10.4 Bollywood poster painting (1930s to the 1990s)

- Studios of painters around Dadar and Lamington Road in Bombay produced hand-painted posters, hoardings and lobby cards. Diwakar Karkare is credited with the poster for "Deewaar" (1975); many studio painters went uncredited. The craft collapsed in the late 1990s when digital printing on vinyl flex made a photographic billboard cheaper than a painted one.
- Visual DNA: saturated, painted portraits with rim light; faces stacked by star billing; action vignettes; drama lettering in Hindi, Urdu and English on one sheet.
- Borrow now: the trilingual hierarchy and the painted portrait, commissioned from a living painter, for event key art or an Indian restaurant in Dubai that wants Bombay specificity. Leave: the kitsch pastiche printed from clip art, which is already the default of themed restaurants and invitations to the sangeet (the pre-wedding music night).

### 10.5 Indian type design

- Lineage: missionary and colonial presses (section 10.1), then the Nirnaya Sagar types, then hot-metal Devanagari from Monotype and Linotype, then the typewriter and phototypesetting, each change imposing technical compromises on the script (fewer conjuncts, simplified matras, the vowel signs that attach to consonants). Digital Unicode and OpenType (section 7.2) finally allowed full conjunct sets.
- Foundries: the Indian Type Foundry (ITF), founded in 2009 by Satya Rajpurohit and Peter Biľak in Ahmedabad, whose Kohinoor Devanagari ships as an Apple system font and whose free platform Fontshare distributes display and text faces; Ek Type (Mumbai), whose open-source multiscript families Mukta and Baloo on Google Fonts made good Indian-script type free for every web project; Universal Thirst (Kimya Gandhi and Rob Keller), focused on editorial-grade Indian scripts paired with Latin.
- Lesson: in a bilingual Indian identity the Devanagari is designed with the same care and the same proportions as the Latin from day one; see 03-typography.md for pairing and metrics.

### 10.6 Raw Mango and Sabyasachi as visual cultures

- Sabyasachi Mukherjee founded his label in 1999 after graduating from NIFT Kolkata. The visual culture is maximal and archival: antique textiles, Calcutta nostalgia, Victorian studio portraiture, candlelit sepia photography, rooms dense with objects, and a Royal Bengal tiger crest. Aditya Birla Fashion and Retail bought a 51 percent stake in 2021; the H&M collaboration "Wanderlust" (2021) took the look to mass retail.
- Raw Mango, founded by Sanjay Garg in 2008 from Chanderi handloom, is the opposite temperature: restraint, saturated single colours on handwoven cloth, poetic Hindi collection titles, quiet typography and stores designed as galleries.
- Lesson: two Indian luxury grammars, baroque and austere, both built on craft as the source of value and both owned so completely that they cannot be borrowed whole. A Mask360 client in bridal or textiles must choose a third position.

### 10.7 The Indian D2C design wave (2015 to 2026)

- The pattern: venture-funded direct-to-consumer (D2C: brands that sell through their own website and app, skipping retail) brands imported the Western D2C kit (sans wordmark, pastel palette, illustrated packaging, Instagram grid) and the better ones localised it with Indian visual memory.
- Cases: CRED (2018, Kunal Shah), whose open-sourced NeoPOP design system answered neumorphism with hard edges, depth and saturated colour, and whose advertising cast Indian heroes against type; Blue Tokai (2013, Matt Chitharanjan and Namrata Asthana), specialty coffee with a cartographic, origin-led identity; Mokobara (2020), luggage with a Scandinavian-minimal palette; Subko (2020, Mumbai), specialty coffee and craft bakehouse whose spaces and packaging use materials and Indian craft references with unusual seriousness; Paper Boat (2013), nostalgic drinks with illustrated childhood memory; Bombay Shaving Company (2016); Nicobar (2016); Bombay Sweet Shop (2019), which redesigned mithai (traditional Indian sweets) with wit.
- Lesson: the Western D2C kit is now generic; the brands that last are those whose image could only come from India. This is the "cover the logo" test from design-taste applied to a market.

## 12. Design theory and philosophy

### 12.1 Form follows function (Louis Sullivan, 1896)

- Source: Sullivan's essay "The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered" (Lippincott's Magazine, March 1896): "form ever follows function". Sullivan was an ornamentalist (the Wainwright Building, St Louis, 1891, is covered in terracotta foliage); the slogan was later narrowed by functionalists into a ban on ornament.
- House reading: function includes meaning, status and pleasure. A luxury object's function is partly to be desired, so ornament that carries meaning is functional.

### 12.2 Ornament and crime (Adolf Loos, 1910)

- Source: Loos's lecture "Ornament und Verbrechen", first delivered in Vienna in 1910 and published in French in 1913. It argues that ornament wastes labour and materials and marks a culture as backward, and it uses racist examples (Papuan tattooing) to do so.
- House reading: a European modernist polemic, influential and partly bigoted. In India and the Gulf, ornament (jaali, the pierced stone screen; mashrabiya, the carved wooden lattice window; block print; zardozi, metal-thread embroidery; geometric Islamic pattern) is a living cultural language. Mask360 uses Loos's discipline (no ornament without reason) and rejects his hierarchy of cultures.

### 12.3 Less is more (Mies van der Rohe) and its answer

- Mies adopted the phrase, which he credited to Peter Behrens and which appears in Robert Browning's poem "Andrea del Sarto" (1855). Robert Venturi replied "Less is a bore" in "Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture" (1966), and with Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour wrote "Learning from Las Vegas" (1972), which distinguished the "duck" (a building whose form is its sign) from the "decorated shed" (a plain building with a sign applied).
- House reading: both are tools. A Dubai sales gallery is often a decorated shed by necessity; make the sign excellent.

### 12.4 Less but better (Dieter Rams)

- Rams (born 1932) worked at Braun from 1955 to 1997, as head of design from 1961; his 606 shelving for Vitsœ (1960) is still made. His ten principles of good design, formulated around 1980, say good design is innovative, makes a product useful, is aesthetic, makes a product understandable, is unobtrusive, is honest, is long-lasting, is thorough down to the last detail, is environmentally friendly, and is as little design as possible. "Weniger, aber besser" (less but better) is the title of his 1995 book. The Braun T3 radio (1958) is the visible ancestor of Apple's iPod (2001).
- House reading: the tenth principle is the design-taste skill's edit step in philosophical form; this corpus cites Rams and leaves the taste rules to that skill.

### 12.5 The Vignelli canon

- Massimo Vignelli's "The Vignelli Canon" (2010, Lars Müller, first released as a free PDF) divides design into intangibles (semantics, syntactics, pragmatics, discipline, appropriateness, ambiguity, design is one, visual power, intellectual elegance, timelessness, responsibility, equity) and tangibles (paper sizes, grids, margins, typefaces, colour, layout). Vignelli argued that a handful of classic typefaces (Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded, Futura, Times, Helvetica) are enough for almost any job.
- House reading: the canon is the best short statement of the modernist position and a good critique checklist. Its typeface austerity predates the independent foundry boom and should be read as discipline, applied today with a wider library (see 03-typography.md).

### 12.6 The Rand position

- Paul Rand's books, "Thoughts on Design" (1947), "Paul Rand: A Designer's Art" (1985), "Design, Form, and Chaos" (1993) and "From Lascaux to Brooklyn" (1996), argue that design is the synthesis of form and content, that a logo gains its meaning from the quality of the organisation it stands for, and that play and timelessness belong together. His 1992 essay "Confusion and Chaos: The Seduction of Contemporary Graphic Design" attacked Cranbrook and Emigre as fashion dressed as theory.
- House reading: Rand's view of the logo is the right brief for clients who want a mark to "say everything". The mark is a vessel; the brand fills it over years.

### 12.7 The Papanek critique

- Victor Papanek's "Design for the Real World: Human Ecology and Social Change" (Swedish 1970, English 1971) opens by saying that there are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few. He attacked styling, waste and design for the rich, and proposed design for the developing world (his tin-can radio). Ken Garland's "First Things First" manifesto (1964, 22 signatories) asked designers to put their skills to worthwhile use; it was republished in 2000 with 33 signatories.
- House reading: Mask360 works for luxury clients, so the critique applies directly. The house answer is craft that lasts, fair pay to makers, truthful claims and no dark patterns (section 12.14); the agency does not pretend the critique away.

### 12.8 Edward Tufte

- "The Visual Display of Quantitative Information" (1983, self-published through Graphics Press), then "Envisioning Information" (1990), "Visual Explanations" (1997) and "Beautiful Evidence" (2006). Concepts: the data-ink ratio (the share of a graphic's ink that shows data, to be maximised); chartjunk (decoration that carries no information); small multiples (a series of small charts on the same scale for comparison); the lie factor (the size of an effect shown divided by the size of the effect in the data). He praised Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's Russian campaign as perhaps the best statistical graphic ever drawn. "The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint" (2003) tied slide culture to engineering failure in the Columbia shuttle investigation.
- House reading: Tufte governs investor decks, deal sheets and dashboards; see the dataviz skill and 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md.

### 12.9 Kenya Hara on emptiness

- In "Designing Design" and "White", Hara separates Western simplicity (reduction for efficiency) from Japanese emptiness (ku: an empty vessel that invites the viewer to bring meaning). Muji's goal, he writes, is "kore de ii" ("this will do", a rational, sufficient satisfaction) in place of "kore ga ii" ("this is what I want", a craving).
- House reading: emptiness as receptivity explains why a luxury page that is mostly blank feels generous when the remaining elements are exact; design-taste states the rule, Hara supplies the reason.

### 12.10 Wabi-sabi

- A Japanese aesthetic of impermanence, imperfection and incompleteness, rooted in the tea ceremony of Sen no Rikyū (1522 to 1591). Leonard Koren's "Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets and Philosophers" (1994) brought it to Western design; Jun'ichirō Tanizaki's "In Praise of Shadows" (1933) is its companion on darkness and patina. Kintsugi, the repair of broken ceramics with lacquer and gold, is its most borrowed image.
- House reading: borrow patina, the unfinished edge and honest ageing of materials (brass that darkens, limewash, handmade tiles in a Goa villa). Leave "Japandi" interiors (a Japanese and Scandinavian hybrid style) and kintsugi used as a metaphor in brand copy, both exhausted.

### 12.11 The Ulm model

- Ulm (section 4.2) taught design as a methodical, interdisciplinary practice grounded in science: analysis, research, systematic variants, testing. Maldonado and the philosopher Max Bense brought semiotics; Horst Rittel brought planning theory. Critics argued that Ulm suppressed the symbolic and emotional side of objects; Klaus Krippendorff (an Ulm graduate) and Reinhart Butter answered with "product semantics" (1984), the study of how a product's form communicates its use and meaning.
- House reading: the Ulm model is the operating system of modern design process and design systems; product semantics is the part luxury needs.

### 12.12 Design thinking and its critics

- Lineage: Herbert Simon's "The Sciences of the Artificial" (1969) defined design as devising courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones; Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber named "wicked problems" (problems with no definitive formulation or final solution, 1973); Peter Rowe's "Design Thinking" (1987); IDEO (1991, David Kelley); the Stanford d.school (2005); Tim Brown's "Design Thinking" (Harvard Business Review, June 2008) and "Change by Design" (2009).
- Critics: Don Norman, "Design Thinking: A Useful Myth" (Core77, 2010); Lucy Kimbell, "Rethinking Design Thinking" (Design and Culture, 2011 and 2012); Natasha Jen of Pentagram, "Design Thinking Is Bullshit" (99U Conference, 2017), who attacked the sticky-note ritual for skipping criticism, evidence and craft.
- House position: design thinking is a useful facilitation format for aligning a client team. The work is the making, judged by critique. Mask360 runs workshops for alignment and never presents a wall of sticky notes as a deliverable.

### 12.13 Speculative and critical design

- Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby: Dunne's "Hertzian Tales" (1999), "Design Noir" (2001), and "Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming" (MIT Press, 2013); they led the Design Interactions programme at the Royal College of Art (2005 to 2015). Critical design uses speculative objects to question assumptions; speculative design imagines alternative futures to debate which is preferable. Their futures cone (probable, plausible, possible and preferable futures) adapts Joseph Voros's 2003 model.
- House reading: for a real estate or hospitality client, a speculative "life in 2040" narrative can make a masterplan legible and memorable; keep it clearly labelled as fiction to avoid misleading buyers.

### 12.14 The ethics turn

- Positions: Papanek and "First Things First" (section 12.7); Mike Monteiro's "Ruined by Design" (2019), which holds designers responsible for the harms their products enable; Harry Brignull's naming of dark patterns (2010: interface designs that trick users into choices they did not intend).
- Law has caught up. The EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Article 25) bans deceptive interface design on online platforms, and its obligations applied to all platforms from 17 February 2024. India's Central Consumer Protection Authority notified the "Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023" on 30 November 2023, listing 13 specified dark patterns: false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing and rogue malware. Accessibility is the other legal and ethical floor: WCAG 2.2 (W3C Recommendation, October 2023).
- House position: no dark patterns in any Mask360 build, even when a client asks; false urgency ("only two villas left") is a named violation in India. Ethics of imagery (casting, skin retouching, cultural motifs, AI likeness) is a CD responsibility, covered further in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md and section 14.6.

## 14. Citing a movement without pastiche

### 14.1 Revival, reference, copy and pastiche

Four different acts hide behind "inspired by". Name the one being performed before anything is drawn.

| Act | Definition | Example | When it is legitimate | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revival | Adopting a period style as a whole because its values are wanted again | Gothic Revival; Tschichold's return to classical setting; the 2020s "future medieval" blackletter | When the values are argued and the technology is current | Costume if the argument is missing |
| Reference | Taking one identified element from a source and placing it in a new context where it means something new | Saville's Fantin-Latour roses on a New Order sleeve; Saint Laurent's Mondrian dress (1965) | Almost always, when credited on the board | Becomes copy if the element is the whole work |
| Copy | Reproducing the recognisable whole of a specific work | Lifting a Cassandre poster layout and swapping the product | Only under licence or for private study | Copyright infringement under India's Copyright Act, 1957, and the UAE's Federal Decree-Law No. 38 of 2021 on copyrights; trademark claims where a mark is involved |
| Pastiche | Imitating the surface of a style without its reason; Fredric Jameson's "blank parody" (1984) | "Bauhaus" posters of primaries and circles; "Deco" sunbursts on wedding invitations | As a deliberate joke for a deliberate audience | Reads as a filter, signals no ideas |

### 14.2 The three percent rule

- The original: Virgil Abloh (1980 to 2021; Off-White, founded 2013; artistic director of Louis Vuitton menswear from March 2018) said that a new design can be created by changing an original by only three percent. He applied it to icons he was licensed to alter: in Nike's "The Ten" (2017) he kept the silhouettes, soles and Swooshes of ten classic shoes and added visible stitching, exposed foam, a zip tie and printed text. Critics tied the method to accusations of plagiarism; its defence is that the original's owner was party to the change.
- The house adaptation, for citing a movement (where no owner consents and the audience fills in the rest): borrow about three percent of the visible decisions, meaning one element, and make the other ninety-seven percent the client's own. One Deco proportion, one Constructivist diagonal, one Swiss grid, one Memphis surface. The reason: an audience recognises a movement from two or three of its traits, so a second borrowed trait turns a reference into a costume.
- Test: count the decisions on the board (type, colour, grid, image treatment, ornament, motion, material, copy tone). If more than one comes from the cited movement, cut until one remains.

### 14.3 The quotation idea

- Abloh put quotation marks on objects ("SHOES", "AIR", "FOR WALKING") to make the borrowing explicit, a stance he called ironic detachment, and he cited Marcel Duchamp's readymades (the urinal signed "R. Mutt", "Fountain", 1917) as the precedent: choosing and recontextualising is itself authorship.
- Use for a CD: when the work quotes, let the quotation show. Credit the source and year in the reference board captions, in a campaign's credits, and sometimes on the work itself (a museum-style caption on a hotel's art programme, a line in a brand book naming the Bombay Deco building whose proportions the identity uses). Visible citation turns imitation into commentary and protects the client from the charge of copying.
- Limit: irony works for streetwear and culture brands. For heritage luxury, citation belongs in the brief and the brand book, while the finished work stays quiet.

### 14.4 The board protocol

1. Name the source precisely: movement, designer, work, year. "Swiss" is a mood; Müller-Brockmann's "der Film" (1960) is a reference.
2. Name the one element borrowed: light, grid, colour relation, scale contrast, letterform, pace, material, crop.
3. Name the technology then and now. The Plakatstil poster was read from a passing tram; the equivalent today is a story frame seen during a thumb's pass. Transfer the constraint and redraw everything else.
4. Name the opposition. What was the movement against, and what is the client against? If the two oppositions do not rhyme, choose another source.
5. Check absorption. Has a category neighbour already used this element? Check the design-taste currency file and the category's last two years of campaigns; an element absorbed by mass retail is no longer a reference for luxury.
6. Translate into the client's material: Bombay Deco fins as a brass-foil line pattern; a Swiss grid carrying Arabic and English; Polish metaphor drawn by a Mumbai illustrator.
7. Run the cover-the-movement test: show the work to someone outside the project. If they name the movement before the brand, the borrow is too large.
8. Commission or license. Never ship lifted artwork, photography or type; buy the font licence, commission the lettering, license the archive image.

### 14.6 Failure modes

- Costume: every element from one movement. Fix with section 14.2.
- Anachronism: the look of a technology without the technology (a CSS "letterpress" deboss, a fake risograph overlay). Either use the real process and scan it, or leave it out.
- The double quotation: citing the absorbed form while believing it is the source, for example a Nike poster taken for Constructivism, or corporate Memphis taken for Memphis. Always go back to the original work and date.
- Political blindness: Futurism's alliance with fascism, Constructivism's state propaganda, Leni Riefenstahl's "Olympia" (1938). Susan Sontag's essay "Fascinating Fascism" (1975) explains how such aesthetics seduce. The look carries its politics whether the client knows it or not.
- Sacred and community motifs: Chanel withdrew dresses carrying Quranic verses from its spring 2013 collection after objections; Gucci's runway turban, later sold at Nordstrom for 790 US dollars (2018 to 2019), offended Sikhs; Nike's Air Max 270 (2019) faced a petition claiming its heel logo resembled the word Allah in Arabic script. In India and the Gulf, check every script, deity, religious pattern and calligraphic form with a qualified reader before it enters a design.
- Nostalgia without a point: a revival chosen because a decade is fashionable on social media. Ask what the decade's values offer this client now; if the answer is only "it looks cool", the board is a mood board.

## Terms introduced

- agitprop: agitation-propaganda, the state's use of design and performance to carry political instruction.
- backdrop-filter: the CSS property that blurs or tints whatever lies behind an element, the technical basis of glassmorphism.
- big idea: one visual or verbal concept that makes the whole argument of a piece, usually through a pun, substitution or contradiction.
- blanding: replacing a distinctive brand mark with a near-identical geometric sans-serif wordmark, so brands become interchangeable.
- Bodymovin: an After Effects plugin (Hernan Torrisi, 2015) that exports animation as JSON for runtime playback.
- bounce lighting: light reflected off a white surface onto the subject to imitate soft daylight, introduced in Indian cinema by Subrata Mitra.
- branded entertainment: content commissioned by a brand to be watched for its own sake, such as BMW's "The Hire".
- calendar art: mass-printed devotional and popular images sold in bazaars and given away as calendars, descended from Ravi Varma's chromolithographs.
- CGI: computer-generated imagery.
- chartjunk: Tufte's term for decoration in a chart that carries no information.
- chromolithography: colour printing from several hand-drawn limestone plates, one per colour, which made large areas of flat colour cheap.
- colour script: a plan assigning a colour or palette to each emotional state or section of a film.
- conjunct: a combined form of two or more Devanagari consonants written without the inherent vowel between them.
- copy (act): reproducing the recognisable whole of a specific work.
- corporate Memphis: the flat illustration style of rubber-limbed figures that spread through technology brands from about 2017, unrelated in intent to the Memphis Group.
- costume: a design in which every element comes from one cited movement, so the source is seen before the brand.
- cover-the-movement test: showing the work to an outsider; if they name the movement before the brand, the borrow is too large.
- creative director (CD): the person accountable for the idea and the craft quality of all of a brand's or campaign's output.
- creative team: a copywriter and an art director working as a pair from the first idea, the structure DDB introduced in 1949.
- critical design: design that uses speculative objects to question assumptions about technology and society.
- CSS: Cascading Style Sheets, the web's styling language.
- D2C (direct to consumer): brands that sell through their own website and app, bypassing retailers.
- dark pattern: an interface design that tricks users into choices they did not intend; regulated in the EU and India.
- data-ink ratio: the share of a graphic's ink devoted to showing data, which Tufte says should be maximised.
- design thinking: a facilitated, user-centred problem-solving process popularised by IDEO and the Stanford d.school.
- desktop publishing: page layout and typesetting done by the designer on a personal computer, a term coined by Paul Brainerd in 1985.
- DIN 476: the 1922 German paper-size standard that defined the A series.
- dotLottie: a compressed container (.lottie) holding a Lottie animation and its assets.
- double quotation: citing the absorbed commercial form of a movement in the belief that it is the original.
- détournement: the Situationist tactic of hijacking an existing image or text so it turns against its original meaning.
- duck and decorated shed: Venturi, Scott Brown and Izenour's distinction between a building whose form is its sign and a plain building with a sign applied.
- dynamic identity: a logo whose structure stays fixed while its fill, content or form changes, as with MTV (1981).
- Elementarism: van Doesburg's 1924 revision of De Stijl that admitted the diagonal.
- Equestrian Knight Design (EKD): Burberry's mounted-knight device, first drawn in 1901 and restored in 2023.
- faux out-of-home (faux OOH): a CGI film showing an impossible object in a real street, presented as if it were a real installation.
- flat design: interface style of flat colour and type without simulated texture or depth, dominant from about 2010 to 2020.
- flush-left, ragged-right: lines aligned on the left edge and left uneven on the right, so word spacing stays constant.
- Frutiger Aero: a 2017 nostalgia label for the glossy, aqua, nature-themed interface look of about 2004 to 2013.
- futures cone: a model of probable, plausible, possible and preferable futures used in speculative design.
- Gesamtkunstwerk: the total work of art, every object of a space designed to one idea.
- glassmorphism: frosted, translucent interface panels that blur what lies behind them.
- grotesk: a sans-serif typeface of the nineteenth-century German lineage, such as Akzidenz-Grotesk.
- GSAP: the GreenSock Animation Platform, a JavaScript animation library for the web.
- Hinglish: spoken and written Hindi mixed with English.
- Indo-Deco: Indian Art Deco that adds Hindu and Mughal motifs to Deco geometry.
- jaali: a pierced stone or wooden screen in Indian architecture.
- Japandi: a hybrid Japanese and Scandinavian interior style.
- jharokha: a projecting enclosed balcony window of Rajasthani and Mughal architecture.
- JSON: a structured text data format used to store animation and other data for software.
- karigar: an Indian artisan or craftsperson.
- kerning: adjusting the space between specific pairs of letters.
- kintsugi: repairing broken ceramics with lacquer and gold, making the break visible.
- kore de ii: "this will do", Hara's term for the rational sufficiency Muji aims for, set against kore ga ii, "this is what I want".
- ku: emptiness as a receptive vessel that invites the viewer's meaning, in Kenya Hara's theory.
- lensing: the refraction of underlying content through a translucent layer, the defining behaviour of Apple's Liquid Glass.
- lie factor: Tufte's ratio of the size of an effect shown in a graphic to the size of the effect in the data.
- Liquid Glass: Apple's translucent system material introduced on 9 June 2025 across its 26-series operating systems.
- lota: the Indian brass water vessel that the Eames India Report held up as an object perfected by use.
- Lottie: a JSON-based animation format and family of runtime renderers, open-sourced by Airbnb in 2017.
- ma: the charged interval between things, treated in Japanese design as a component of the composition.
- mashrabiya: a carved wooden lattice window of Arab architecture.
- Material Design: Google's design system announced in 2014, built on a paper-and-ink metaphor with defined elevations.
- matra: a Devanagari vowel sign that attaches to a consonant.
- Merz: Kurt Schwitters's private art-and-design practice built from collage and found print, also the name of his magazine.
- mithai: traditional Indian sweets.
- modular grid: a fixed set of columns, rows and gutters to which every element on a page snaps.
- mograph: motion graphics as a discipline joining design, animation and compositing.
- Neoplasticism: Mondrian's reduction of painting to horizontals, verticals, primaries and non-colours.
- neumorphism: soft UI; controls rendered as soft extrusions of a same-coloured background with paired light and dark shadows.
- page description language: a language, such as PostScript, that describes a page's text and graphics as scalable outlines independent of the output device.
- parole in libertà: "words in freedom", the Futurist practice of setting type at angles and mixed sizes as sound and speed.
- pastiche: imitation of a style's surface without its reason; Jameson's "blank parody".
- photomontage: cut photographs recombined to make a new image and argument.
- phototypesetting: setting type photographically on film, which freed letterspacing and size from metal.
- Plakatstil: the German object poster of one product, one brand name and flat colour; also called Sachplakat.
- product semantics: the study of how a product's form communicates its use and meaning (Krippendorff and Butter, 1984).
- Proun: El Lissitzky's term for his abstract spatial compositions, "projects for the affirmation of the new".
- quotation idea: Abloh's practice of making a borrowing explicit with visible quotation marks or credit.
- reference (act): taking one identified element from a source and placing it in a new context where it carries a new meaning.
- revival: adopting a period style as a whole because its values are wanted again.
- risograph: a stencil duplicator introduced by Riso Kagaku in 1986, valued for misregistration and soy-ink texture.
- sangeet: the pre-wedding music night of a north Indian wedding.
- silver retention: leaving silver in a film print to deepen blacks and mute colour.
- skeuomorph: a design that keeps cues from the material or tool it replaces as ornament.
- small multiples: a series of small charts on the same scale for comparison.
- sonic logo: a short, repeated sound signature that identifies a brand.
- speculative design: design that imagines alternative futures in order to debate which is preferable.
- state machine: in interactive animation, a set of states and transitions that respond to inputs, as used by Rive.
- styling: cosmetic redesign of a product to drive sales without changing its function.
- Swadeshi: the early twentieth-century movement to boycott British goods in favour of Indian-made ones.
- three percent rule: Abloh's claim that changing an original by three percent makes a new design; in this house, the limit of one borrowed element when citing a movement.
- topical: an advertisement commenting on the week's news with a pun, as in Amul's hoardings.
- typographics: Lubalin's expressive typography, letterforms drawn and set so tightly that the word becomes an image.
- ukiyo-e: Edo-period Japanese woodblock prints.
- Van de Graaf canon: a geometric construction that places a text block so that it and the margins share the page's proportions.
- variable font: one font file holding a continuous range of weights, widths or other axes, added in OpenType 1.8 (2016).
- Vorkurs: the Bauhaus preliminary course in materials, colour and form taken before specialisation.
- wabi-sabi: the Japanese aesthetic of impermanence, imperfection and incompleteness.
- WCAG: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines published by the W3C.
- Web 2.0: the 2004 name for the participatory web, and by extension its glossy visual style.
- web brutalism: websites that expose default HTML styling, raw grids and system fonts as a deliberate stance.
- WebGL: a browser interface that gives web pages access to the graphics processor.
- whiplash line: the sinuous, asymmetric curve of Art Nouveau, drawn from plant stems and hair.
- white paper sweep: a continuous studio backdrop curving from wall to floor so no horizon shows.
- wicked problem: a problem with no definitive formulation or final solution (Rittel and Webber, 1973).
- zardozi: metal-thread embroidery of Persian and Mughal origin.
