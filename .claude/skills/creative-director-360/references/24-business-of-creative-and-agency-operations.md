## 1. Agency models and the market

### 1.1 The models and what each one sells

| Model | What the client buys | Typical size | Revenue shape | Margin pressure comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full service and 360 | One accountable partner across strategy, creative, media, social, PR, production | 40 to 2,000 people | Retainers 60 to 80 percent of fees | Overhead of breadth; every discipline carries idle capacity |
| Creative boutique | Ideas and campaigns, led by two or three named creatives | 8 to 40 | Projects 60 to 80 percent | Feast and famine; founder dependence |
| Design studio | Identity, systems, editorial, packaging, environments | 3 to 30 | Projects, some brand guardianship retainers | Long sales cycles; under-scoped rounds |
| Digital and web studio | Sites, products, commerce, experiential web | 5 to 60 | Projects plus maintenance retainers | Engineering estimates that slip; browser and platform churn |
| Production house | Film, stills, post, CGI, sound | 5 to 100 | Per job, cost-plus with a mark-up | Crew and kit utilisation; weather; client approvals on set |
| In-house agency | A client's own creative team | 3 to 300 | Salary cost, no margin | Scope inflation; talent retention; taste drift |
| Hybrid and collective | A small core plus a named freelance bench, assembled per job | 2 to 10 core, 20 to 60 bench | Projects, a few retainers | Bench availability; quality consistency |

The reason the model matters commercially: the model fixes the overhead ratio (overhead is every cost that is not a billable person: rent, software, admin, new business), and overhead ratio fixes the minimum price. A 40-person full service shop in Mumbai carrying 30 to 35 percent overhead cannot profitably quote the price a 6-person studio with 15 percent overhead can. Mask360 runs as a full service house with a boutique-sized senior creative core and a production bench, which is why its pricing must read as premium everywhere: the breadth is paid for by the taste, and the taste is the product (see design-taste, SKILL.md, section 1).

### 1.2 In-housing and the in-housing trend

In-housing is a client building its own creative team to replace or reduce agency spend. The ANA (Association of National Advertisers, the US client body) has tracked the share of its members with an in-house agency rising from 42 percent in 2008 to a large majority by its 2023 report (exact 2023 figure: verify against the ANA report "The Continued Rise of the In-House Agency"). Indian conglomerates and D2C (direct to consumer) brands followed the same path from 2020: Tata, Reliance Retail, Nykaa, Myntra and the large real estate developers all run sizeable internal studios as of 2026. What in-house teams reliably do well: volume, speed, platform knowledge, product truth. What they reliably cannot do: taste at the level of a dedicated house, a view from outside the building, production at scale, and a senior creative who will tell the CMO no. The commercial consequence: sell what the in-house team cannot make (the idea, the identity, the film, the launch) and offer to run their studio's standards (guidelines, calibration, craft reviews, see section 10) as a retainer instead of competing with them for the daily social post.

### 1.3 The India market structure

Networks hold the FMCG, auto, telecom and bank retainers that are tendered through procurement, and they train most of the senior talent that later goes independent.
- Independents. Design: Elephant (Pune, founded 1989), Lopez Design (Delhi), Codesign (Delhi), Studio Kohl (Mumbai), Landor and Interbrand's Indian offices.
- Where premium sits. Luxury and premium clients in India (hospitality groups, jewellers, fashion houses, developers with branded residences) rarely tender; they buy from a founder or a CMO on referral, chemistry and portfolio. This is the pool Mask360 fishes in. The house account map of Indian agencies and their clients (see the new-business/india-agency-account-map folder in this repository) is the live record of who holds which account and is private house research (section 14.9).
- Scale of the market. Indian advertising expenditure crossed about Rs 1 lakh crore (about USD 12 billion) in the mid 2020s by the Pitch Madison Advertising Report and WPP Media's This Year Next Year estimates, with digital the largest medium since 2023 (exact 2025 total and 2026 forecast: verify against the Pitch Madison Advertising Report 2026). Creative fees are a small slice of that; the bulk is media.

### 1.4 The UAE and Saudi market

- Dubai is the regional hub: Dubai Media City and Dubai Internet City host the MENA headquarters of the networks (Impact BBDO, FP7 McCann, Leo Burnett MEA, Memac Ogilvy, TBWA\RAAD, Horizon FCB, Serviceplan Middle East, Publicis Middle East, VML MENA) and the independents (Science and Sunshine, Socialize, Create). Abu Dhabi's media zone is twofour54, run under the Creative Media Authority.
- Saudi Arabia is the growth engine. Vision 2030 giga-projects (NEOM, Diriyah, the Red Sea, Qiddiya), the Public Investment Fund's consumer brands, and Riyadh Season drive the largest briefs in the region. Since 1 January 2024 the Saudi Regional Headquarters programme requires foreign companies seeking government contracts to base their regional HQ in the Kingdom, which has pulled network leadership and talent to Riyadh. For a Dubai-based house the practical rule: Saudi work needs a Saudi partner or entity for government and giga-project clients; private luxury and hospitality clients can still be served from Dubai.
- How the Gulf buys. Procurement-led tenders for government and semi-government; founder-led buying for family groups, hospitality and real estate. Arabic is mandatory alongside English on most public-facing work (see section 14.6). Payment discipline is better than India for networks and government entities; family groups vary.
- What premium means there. The Gulf client base includes Chanel, Dior and LVMH's regional arms, Jumeirah, Emaar, Aldar, Alserkal, Chalhoub Group and the hospitality operators (Four Seasons, Aman, One and Only, Atlantis). These buy global-standard craft with regional fluency: bilingual typography, Ramadan and National Day calendars, modesty and representation norms. A house that can do both at taste level is rare in Dubai, which is the opening.

### 1.5 What clients pay for taste

Taste is the ability to predict what a sophisticated audience will find desirable, current and inevitable (design-taste, SKILL.md). Commercially, taste converts into price through four mechanisms:

1. Risk reduction. A premium client's downside from a cheap-looking launch (a Rs 40 crore residential tower that reads as mid-market, a jeweller whose campaign looks like a bank's) is far larger than any agency fee. The fee is insurance.
2. Scarcity. Few teams can produce it, and the client knows it from the portfolio in thirty seconds.
3. Speed of agreement. A house with taste wins approvals in fewer rounds because the work arrives already edited; fewer rounds is lower cost for both sides.
4. Signalling. The client's choice of agency is itself read by their peers.

Price taste as a premium on the hour, on the project and on the retainer: Mask360's floor sits in the top quartile of the Indian independents and the mid-to-upper band in Dubai (see the agency-negotiator skill for the live floors). The reason for refusing entry-level pricing is arithmetic. Senior craft hours cost more, and a discounted senior hour trains the client to expect discounted senior hours forever.

### 1.6 The 2026 landscape

- AI pricing pressure. Generative tools (image, video, copy, code) have cut the hours in layout, retouching, variant production and first-draft copy by half or more since 2023. Clients know this and procurement teams now ask for the hours saved to be passed through. Two positions exist. The time-and-materials position (hold rate cards, let AI shrink the hours, accept a lower invoice) is defended by procurement and by the large networks' client-services arms. The value position (price the outcome, keep the saving, invest it in craft) is argued by Blair Enns (Pricing Creativity, 2018) and David C. Baker (The Business of Expertise, 2017). The house follows the value position for ideas, identities, films and campaigns, and uses transparent reduced hours for variant production and adaptations where the client can see the machine doing the work. The reason: pricing by hours on AI-assisted work makes the agency's income fall exactly as its leverage rises. See 22 for the AI tool layer and the disclosure rules.
- Production decoupling. Decoupling is the client separating production (adaptations, retouching, versioning, print management, social volume) from the creative agency and awarding it to a production specialist (Hogarth under WPP, Tag, Oliver's in-house model, or an India-based studio) at lower rates. It has moved from FMCG to every category with volume. Response: keep the origination and the quality system, and either run the decoupled production at a transparent lower rate card or set the standards the production partner must meet (section 10).
- Design engineers and small teams. The design engineer (a person who designs in Figma and ships in code, usually React, Next.js, Framer or Webflow with motion in GSAP or Rive) has replaced the designer-plus-developer pair for premium sites and products. Four people with taste, a design engineer, a motion designer and a producer now deliver what took fifteen in 2019. Pricing follows outcomes because headcount is no longer the proxy for value.
- Retainers versus projects. Retainers (a fixed monthly fee for an agreed scope and capacity) give the agency cash stability and the client a standing team; projects give the agency margin on ideas and the client a defined spend. The 2026 pattern for premium clients: a modest retainer for brand guardianship and social, with projects for launches, films and identities. Cash flow in India makes the retainer worth more than its margin suggests (section 13.6).
- Holding company consolidation. Omnicom's absorption of Interpublic, WPP's reorganisation under its 2025 leadership (per WPP's newsroom, read October 2026: Tyler Turnbull appointed CEO of WPP Creative on 8 October 2026, Adam Gerhart Global Chief Client Officer on 14 October 2026, Eric Campbell succeeding Jon Cook as VML CEO from 2 October 2026, and a flagship production hub opened in East London on 23 September 2026), and Dentsu's review of its international business all point the same way: fewer, larger network brands, more internal production platforms, more AI "operating systems" sold to clients. Independent houses win on taste, speed and senior attention, which the consolidated networks structurally cannot sell.

## 2. Roles and organisation

### 2.1 The roles and what each one owns

| Role | Owns | Reports to | Reads the work for |
|---|---|---|---|

| ECD (executive creative director) | Creative output across a group of accounts or an office; pitches; senior client relationships | CCO | The idea and whether it is big enough |
| CD (creative director) | An account group's work end to end; the brief, the idea, the craft sign-off | ECD | Idea, craft, consistency with the brand |
| ACD (associate creative director) | Day-to-day creative leadership of a team of two to six; first sign-off | CD | Craft and completeness before the CD sees it |
| AD (art director) | The visual idea: composition, image, type, layout; directs photographers and designers | ACD or CD | Whether the picture carries the idea |
| Copywriter | The verbal idea: headlines, scripts, body, names, tone | ACD or CD | Whether the words carry the idea |
| Brand designer | Identity systems, guidelines, print, packaging | CD or design director | System integrity |
| Digital designer | Social, web, display, email, decks | AD or design director | Platform fitness, consistency |

| 3D artist | CGI product, environments, type in space | AD | Material and light credibility |
| Photographer and DP (director of photography) | Stills and moving image on set; freelance by default | AD and producer | Light, frame, performance |
| Producer | Budgets, schedules, crews, vendors, insurance, delivery for film and stills | Head of production | Feasibility and money |
| Line producer | The on-the-ground execution of a shoot: crew calls, permits, locations, catering, transport | Producer | Schedule and cash on the day |
| Strategist | Brief, positioning, audience, proposition, measurement | Head of strategy | Whether the work answers the brief |
| Account and client services | Relationship, scope, expectations, money conversations, approvals | Client services director | Whether it will sell and whether it is in scope |
| Project manager and traffic | Scheduling, resourcing, status, risk; traffic routes work through the studio | Head of operations | Whether it will ship on time |

The reason to write ownership down is that creative teams fail at hand-offs more than at craft. Every brief should name the one person who owns the idea, the one who owns the craft, the one who owns the money and the one who owns the date.

### 2.2 Team shapes

Five people (the founder-led studio): founder as CD and new business; one senior designer who art directs; one designer or motion designer; one producer or project manager who also handles vendors; one account or ops person who also runs finance admin. Everything else freelance. Billable capacity: about 3.5 people. Revenue ceiling at premium Indian rates: about Rs 2.5 to 4 crore a year before the founder becomes the bottleneck.

Fifteen people: founder as CCO; one CD; two ADs; one copywriter; three designers (brand, digital, product); one motion designer; one editor; one producer; one strategist; two account managers; one project manager and traffic; one ops and finance. Billable capacity: about 10.5. Revenue band: about Rs 8 to 15 crore in India, AED 8 to 14 million in Dubai at the rates in section 15.

Forty people: two account groups each led by a CD with an ACD, two ADs, a copywriter, four designers and a motion designer; a shared production unit (head of production, two producers, two editors, one 3D); a strategy pair; a client services director with four account people; a three-person ops team (design ops, traffic, finance); a new business lead. Billable capacity: about 28. The reason the overhead grows faster than the billable count is that coordination becomes a job at this size; a traffic function is the first sign of a real agency.

### 2.3 The freelance network

Premium work in India and the Gulf runs on freelancers: photographers, DPs, editors, colourists, 3D, composers, voice, stylists, hair and make-up, set builders, retouchers. Manage the bench like staff:

- A vendor database (section 7.8) with rate, last job, quality score, reliability notes, kit, city and usage terms.
- Preferred-partner terms: a committed number of days a year in exchange for a 10 to 20 percent reduction from rate card and first call on dates.
- Briefing discipline identical to staff: the same brief format, reference boards and sign-off gates.
- Payment on time. A freelancer paid within 15 days in a market where 60 days is normal will hold dates for the house ahead of better-paying clients. This is the cheapest loyalty money buys.
- Exclusivity only when paid for: a day rate buys the day, and nothing else.

### 2.4 Talent markets and rates, October 2026

India. Senior creative talent clusters in Mumbai (advertising, film, fashion), Bengaluru (product, tech, the newer independents), Delhi and Gurugram (corporate, real estate, luxury retail), with Goa as a remote base for senior freelancers. Agency salaries for creatives are published annually by AmbitionBox and Glassdoor from self-reported data and by Michael Page India's salary guide; the house bands in section 15.1 sit at the upper end of those because luxury work needs senior hands. Day rates for freelancers in Mumbai are the highest in the country; Jaipur and Goa crews run 20 to 40 percent lower for the same skill.

UAE. Dubai salaries are quoted monthly in AED, tax-free, with a housing component sometimes separated. The Michael Page UAE Salary Guide 2027 and the Hays GCC Salary Guide 2026 are the published references (both exist as of October 2026; their creative role rows are behind a download, so verify the exact bands against the guides before quoting a candidate). Freelancing in Dubai requires a permit: the GoFreelance package offered jointly by the Dubai Media City, Dubai Internet City, Dubai Design District and Dubai Knowledge Park communities costs AED 7,500 a year for media, design, education and tech activities, with a one-year employment visa at AED 4,600 (two-year AED 5,042) and an establishment card at AED 2,000 on top, and the page notes that visa and card fees change (gofreelance.ae, read October 2026). This is why Dubai freelance day rates run two to three times the Mumbai equivalent: the freelancer carries permit, visa, insurance and Dubai rent.

Saudi. Riyadh salaries run 10 to 25 percent above Dubai for the same role because of the housing and hardship premium and the RHQ demand, and senior Arabic-first creatives are the scarcest talent in the region.

## 3. Scoping and pricing

### 3.1 Pricing models

| Model | Mechanism | Use it for | Risk sits with | House view |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Rate per hour per role, billed on timesheets | Open-ended consulting, overflow work | Client | Only for retainers' overflow and for change orders |
| Day rate | Rate per 8-hour day per role | Shoots, edits, on-site work, freelance | Client | Standard for production |
| Project (fixed fee) | One price for a defined scope and rounds | Identity, site, film, campaign | Agency | Default for creative work |
| Retainer | Monthly fee for defined scope or capacity | Social, brand guardianship, standing design support | Shared | Default for ongoing work; capacity defined in days, not posts |
| Value based | Price set from the value to the client, agreed before scope is detailed | Launches, identities for funded brands, naming | Agency | Use when the client's upside is quantifiable and the house's taste is the deciding factor |
| Licensing and usage | Fee for the right to use an asset for a term, territory and medium | Photography, film, talent, music, illustration, fonts | Shared | Always separate from the creative fee |

The reason the house prices projects as fixed fees: the client buys certainty and the agency keeps the gain from working fast and well. The reason hourly billing is kept for overflow only: it caps income at hours worked and penalises skill (Jonathan Stark, Hourly Billing Is Nuts, 2016; Blair Enns, Pricing Creativity, 2018).

Three-option pricing. Present every significant project with three options (Enns' anchoring method): the lean option that answers the brief, the recommended option, and a premium option the client did not ask for but would want. The middle option sells about 60 percent of the time, and the top option reframes what "expensive" means. The lean option should still be work the house is proud of; a deliberately poor option is dishonest and the client sees it.

### 3.4 Rounds, change orders and scope creep

- A round is one consolidated set of client feedback on one presented stage, answered with one revised presentation. Two rounds per stage is the premium norm; three is generous; unlimited is a cost the client is not paying for. The reason to count rounds: the number of rounds is the single best predictor of margin erosion on a fixed-fee job.
- A change order is a written, priced addition or alteration to scope, signed before the work starts. Price it from the rate card at hourly or day rate, with a minimum of half a day. The habit that protects the house: say "yes, and here is the change order" in the same breath, never "yes" alone.
- Scope creep is the accumulation of small unpriced additions. Clauses that prevent it: a defined deliverables list with quantities; a defined rounds count; a definition of "round" and of "feedback consolidated through one point of contact" (section 8.4); a change-order process; a clause that work not listed is excluded; an assumptions list (client supplies copy, images, access, approvals within 5 working days).
- Kill fee. A kill fee is the amount payable if the client cancels after work begins: all work completed to date at rate card, plus a percentage of the remaining fee (25 to 50 percent, higher after creative has been presented), plus all third-party costs committed. The reason for the percentage: the house turned away other work for the dates.

### 3.5 Payment terms

- Projects: 50 percent advance (40 percent minimum for large clients), 25 percent on concept approval, 25 percent on delivery. Files, source and rights transfer on final payment. Never release finals before the final payment clears; the lever disappears the moment the files leave.
- Retainers: billed monthly in advance, due within 15 days; two months of non-payment pauses the work automatically (write that into the contract).
- Production: third-party costs (crew, talent, locations, kit, post) paid 100 percent in advance by the client, or paid directly by the client to the vendor on the house's purchase order with the house's mark-up invoiced separately. The reason: a Rs 40 lakh shoot financed on the house's balance sheet at 90 days is a loan the house did not agree to make.
- The Indian reality. Large Indian clients pay in 60 to 90 days whatever the invoice says, and some in 120. Protections, in order of power: advance; milestone billing tied to approvals rather than dates; purchase orders issued before work starts (no PO, no start); a named accounts-payable contact and a vendor registration completed in week one; invoices submitted on the client's portal the day the milestone lands; a late-payment interest clause (even if rarely enforced, it changes the conversation); and, where the house qualifies as a micro or small enterprise under Udyam registration, the MSMED Act 2006 payment protections (section 13.6).
- The UAE reality. Networks and government pay on terms, often 60 days; family groups negotiate; Saudi government entities can run 90 to 180 days. Post-dated cheques are still used as security in the UAE and bouncing one carries legal consequences for the issuer, which makes them a meaningful instrument.

### 3.6 Currency and tax

India.
- GST (goods and services tax) on advertising, design, production and most professional services is 18 percent under SAC heading 9983 (verify the current rate notification; the September 2025 GST rate restructuring moved goods between slabs and left standard-rated services at 18 percent). Sale of advertising space in print media is taxed at 5 percent, which matters when the house buys print media for a client.
- Export of services (client outside India, payment in convertible foreign exchange or in INR where RBI permits, service consumed outside India) is zero-rated: invoice without GST under a LUT (letter of undertaking filed with GST authorities each financial year) and keep the FIRC or FIRA (foreign inward remittance certificate or advice, the bank's proof of foreign receipt) for every invoice.
- TDS (tax deducted at source) is withheld by the client from the invoice and deposited against the house's PAN; the house claims credit via Form 26AS. Advertising contracts attract TDS under section 194C (1 percent for individuals and HUFs, 2 percent for companies) per CBDT Circular 715 of 1995, while fees for professional services under section 194J attract 10 percent (technical services 2 percent). The 194J threshold is Rs 50,000 a year per payee and per payment type, raised from Rs 30,000 by the Finance Act 2025 with effect from 1 April 2025. Payments by the house to freelance artists, photographers and models fall under 194J at 10 percent. Build TDS into cash planning: a Rs 10 lakh invoice under 194J arrives as Rs 9 lakh plus GST.
- Payments to foreign vendors (a Dubai colourist, a London composer) need Form 15CA and often 15CB from a chartered accountant, and withholding under section 195 unless the India-UAE DTAA (double taxation avoidance agreement) and a tax residency certificate remove it.

UAE.
- VAT is 5 percent (Federal Decree-Law No. 8 of 2017); mandatory registration above AED 375,000 taxable turnover in 12 months, voluntary above AED 187,500. Services supplied to a customer resident outside the UAE and consumed outside the UAE are zero-rated under the export of services rules, subject to the customer having no UAE presence connected to the supply (verify the conditions against the FTA's Executive Regulation, Article 31).
- Corporate tax has applied since financial years starting on or after 1 June 2023: 0 percent on taxable income up to AED 375,000 and 9 percent above (Federal Decree-Law No. 47 of 2022). Small Business Relief lets a resident business with revenue of AED 3,000,000 or less in the current and all previous tax periods elect to be treated as having no taxable income, for tax periods ending on or before 31 December 2026 (Ministerial Decision No. 73 of 2023, Article 2, as explained in the FTA guide CTGSBR1 of August 2023); a Qualifying Free Zone Person cannot elect it, so a free zone entity chooses between the two reliefs. A Qualifying Free Zone Person pays 0 percent on qualifying income, which is why a Dubai Media City or DMCC entity matters for a house serving non-UAE clients.
- E-invoicing. The UAE eInvoicing Programme (mof.gov.ae/einvoicing, read October 2026) uses a decentralised continuous transaction control and exchange model: structured invoices in the PINT AE format pass through UAE Accredited Service Providers on both sides and tax data reports to the FTA; PDFs and emails stop counting as invoices once a business is in scope. The ministry's 2025 timeline set a pilot from 1 July 2026, go-live on 1 January 2027 for businesses with revenue of AED 50 million or more and 1 July 2027 for the rest, and the 2026 ministerial decisions (Nos. 168 and 197 of 2026) amended the framework, so confirm the current phase dates on the ministry page before choosing an accounting system. India already mandates e-invoicing with an IRN (invoice reference number, generated on the GST portal) for businesses with aggregate turnover above Rs 5 crore, in force since 1 August 2023.

Cross-border invoicing between the two entities. Invoice from the entity that signed the contract; keep intercompany work on a written services agreement at arm's length pricing (India's transfer pricing rules under section 92 apply to associated enterprises); quote the client in their currency and hold a 3 to 5 percent currency buffer on fixed fees longer than three months.

### 3.7 Usage rights and buyouts

A usage right is permission to use a created asset (image, film, voice, music, likeness) for a defined term, territory and set of media. A buyout is a lump-sum purchase of broad or unlimited usage. Every usage right is priced on five factors: term (1 year, 2 years, 5 years, perpetuity), territory (India, GCC, worldwide), media (digital and social; print and OOH; TV and cinema; all media), exclusivity (whether the talent or photographer can work for a competitor), and prominence (hero image versus one of fifty).

| Asset | Who holds the right by default | Typical premium pricing, India (about) | Typical, UAE (about) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Photography | Photographer, unless assigned; commissioned photographs sit under section 17(b) of the Copyright Act, so write it down either way | 1 year, India, digital plus print: 50 to 100 percent of the day fee; worldwide perpetuity: 200 to 400 percent | 1 year GCC all media: 50 to 100 percent of day fee; worldwide perpetuity 200 to 300 percent |
| Film (director, DP) | Production company via contracts | Included in production fee for the agreed term; extensions 10 to 25 percent of production fee per year | Same structure |
| Talent (models, actors) | The person | Shoot fee plus usage: 1 year India digital 50 to 100 percent of shoot fee; TV adds 100 percent; each extra territory 50 to 100 percent; celebrity usage negotiated per campaign in lakhs to crores | 1 year GCC all media 100 percent of shoot fee; worldwide adds 100 to 200 percent |
| Music (library) | Platform licence | Subscription covers client work on business tiers; paid-media coverage must be confirmed per plan | Same |
| Music (bespoke composition) | Composer unless assigned | Rs 1 to 10 lakh for a 60 second brand track with assignment or long licence | AED 15,000 to 80,000 |
| Music (commercial sync) | Label (master) and publisher (sync) separately | Film song sync from about Rs 5 lakh for digital-only short terms to crores for TV and long terms | Regional and international catalogue via the label's MENA office |
| Voice | The artist | Per spot plus per year of usage; Hindi and English artists Rs 10,000 to 1,00,000 per spot; celebrity voices separate | AED 1,500 to 8,000 per spot, Arabic and English; Gulf Arabic dialect talent at a premium |
| Illustration and type | The illustrator or foundry | Licence by use; fonts per the foundry EULA (see 03) | Same |

The reason usage is priced separately from creation: the client who needs a two-year India digital licence should not pay for worldwide perpetuity, and the client who wants perpetuity should pay for it knowingly. Unlimited buyouts are the expensive option because they remove every future negotiation for the artist.

Rules of the house: no shoot without written releases (section 14.2); every usage term ends on a date written in the delivery note; a diary reminder 60 days before expiry so the client can renew or retire the asset; AI training uses excluded unless explicitly licensed.

## 4. Proposals and contracts

### 4.1 Proposal structure

A proposal is the document that turns a conversation into a priced offer. Ten sections, in this order, because the order is the order in which a buyer decides:

| Section | What it must contain | Length | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding | The client's situation in the house's words: the business, the audience, the problem, the ambition | 1 page | The buyer decides here whether the house listened |
| Approach | How the house will work: method, phases, the taste position (reference-led, single idea, edit; see design-taste, SKILL.md, section 4) | 1 to 2 pages | Sells the method before the price |
| Scope | What is in, with quantities (pages, screens, films, posts, routes, rounds) | 1 page | Quantities are the only defence against creep |
| Deliverables | Each file the client receives, with format and quantity | Table | Removes the "I thought that was included" conversation |
| Timeline | Phases with dates, and the client dependency dates (assets, approvals) in a separate colour | 1 page | Puts half the schedule where it belongs: with the client |
| Team | Named people with roles and one line each; the CD's name is the sale | Half page | Premium clients buy people |
| Fees | Three options (section 3.1), phased payment schedule, currency, tax line | 1 page | Anchoring; the schedule sets the cash |
| Assumptions | Client supplies copy, images, access; feedback consolidated within 5 working days; two rounds per stage | Bullets | Each assumption is a future change order |
| Exclusions | Production costs, media, licences (fonts, stock, music), printing, talent usage, translation, legal clearance, hosting | Bullets | What is excluded is read more carefully than what is included |
| Terms | Validity 30 days; payment; IP position; termination; governing law; reference to the MSA | Half page | Short because the MSA carries the weight |

Rules. Write it in the house grammar (lean, few words, client as hero, dark ground for client decks with the client's hex: design-taste, SKILL.md, section 7). The fee page never surprises; the number was spoken on the call before the document exists. A proposal longer than 12 pages signals that the scope is not understood. Validity 30 days, because rates, availability and exchange rates move.

### 4.2 SOW anatomy

A SOW (statement of work) is the contract-grade description of one engagement under a master agreement. Its parts: parties and MSA reference; objectives; scope and deliverables with acceptance criteria per deliverable ("site design accepted when every page in Appendix A is approved in Figma by the named approver and the design QA checklist is signed"); out of scope; schedule and milestones with dependencies; roles and responsibilities (a RACI: responsible, accountable, consulted, informed, per deliverable); rounds and the feedback protocol (section 8.3); change control (section 3.4); fees and a payment schedule tied to milestones; third-party costs and the handling mark-up (15 to 20 percent when the house carries the cost and risk, 10 percent when the client pays the vendor directly, both disclosed); acceptance and deemed acceptance (silence for 10 working days after delivery counts as acceptance, because an unanswered delivery otherwise never closes); assumptions; term and termination; IP schedule; signatures. The reason to write acceptance criteria per deliverable: "done" is the most expensive undefined word in the business.

### 4.3 MSA basics

An MSA (master services agreement) is the umbrella contract that sets the legal terms once so each SOW can be short. Clauses the house's MSA carries: term (2 to 3 years, auto-renewing); services ordered by SOW; fees, invoicing and late payment (in India, the MSMED Act rate for a registered micro or small enterprise, otherwise a contractual 1.5 percent a month; in the UAE a contractual rate, since the Civil Code lets courts moderate it); taxes; IP (section 4.4); confidentiality (4.5); data protection (14.8); warranties (originality and non-infringement for the house's own work; no warranty over client-supplied material or wholly AI-generated elements); mutual indemnities (the house for IP infringement in its work, the client for materials, claims and products it supplied); limitation of liability (4.8); insurance (14.10); non-solicitation of staff for 12 months with a fee of 25 to 50 percent of annual salary on breach (a client hiring the designer is a real risk in a small market); publicity and portfolio (4.6); termination (4.7); dispute resolution and governing law (4.11); notices; entire agreement. Client paper: networks, listed companies and Gulf government entities send their own MSA, usually 30 to 60 pages with uncapped indemnities and full IP assignment on creation; those are negotiated with a lawyer (4.12), never signed as received.

### 4.4 IP: assignment versus licence, and moral rights

An assignment transfers ownership of the copyright; a licence grants permission to use while the creator keeps ownership. Both are priced, and the price difference is the usage economics of section 3.7.

India, under the Copyright Act 1957 as amended in 2012:

- First ownership (section 17). The author is the first owner, with exceptions. Under proviso (b), a photograph, painting, portrait, engraving or cinematograph film made for valuable consideration at the instance of a person belongs to that person in the absence of an agreement to the contrary. Under proviso (c), a work made in the course of employment under a contract of service belongs to the employer in the absence of an agreement to the contrary. Consequence: staff work belongs to the house; freelance work (a contract for service) belongs to the freelancer unless assigned in writing; a commissioned photograph belongs to the commissioner by default but the agreement should say so, because "at the instance of" is argued over.
- Assignment (sections 18 and 19). Must be in writing, signed by the assignor, identifying the work, the rights, the term and the territory. If the term is not stated it is deemed five years; if the territory is not stated it is deemed India (section 19(5) and 19(6)). An assignment without a stated term is therefore a five-year assignment, which is the most common drafting trap in Indian creative contracts. Under section 19(4), rights not exercised within one year of assignment lapse unless the agreement says otherwise.
- Moral rights (section 57). The author's special rights: paternity (to claim authorship) and integrity (to restrain or claim damages for distortion, mutilation or modification prejudicial to honour or reputation). They exist independently of the copyright and survive assignment. Whether they can be waived by contract is contested: the statute is silent, practitioners draft "agrees not to assert" clauses, and Amar Nath Sehgal v Union of India (Delhi High Court, 2005), in which the government's destruction of a commissioned mural led to damages and return of the remains, is the leading integrity case. House position: the freelancer agreement carries an assignment, a moral rights non-assertion clause, and a credit clause, because credit is what the creator actually wants; and the house does not alter a photographer's work beyond agreed retouching without telling them.
- Authorship of machine output. Section 2(d)(vi) names "the person who causes the work to be created" as author of a computer-generated work, but the Copyright Office's practice and the ANI v OpenAI litigation in the Delhi High Court (filed 2024, pending as of October 2026) leave AI authorship unsettled; treat wholly AI-generated material as unprotectable unless substantial human authorship can be shown (see 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 3.2).
- Term and formality. Copyright arises on creation; registration with the Copyright Office is optional evidence. Term is the author's life plus 60 years for literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works including photographs since the 2012 amendment; 60 years from publication for films and sound recordings.

UAE, under Federal Decree-Law No. 38 of 2021 on Copyrights and Neighbouring Rights (replacing Federal Law No. 7 of 2002): the author owns the work; economic rights in commissioned and employee work pass by written contract; moral rights (attribution, integrity, the right to decide first publication) are perpetual and cannot be waived or assigned, which is why a UAE contract uses a non-assertion undertaking; the general term is the author's life plus 50 years, with 50 years from publication for works of applied art and collective works; registration with the Ministry of Economy is optional. Verify article numbers against the decree-law before citing them in a contract.

House default position, written into every SOW: the final approved deliverables are assigned to the client on receipt of full payment; unused and rejected routes stay with the house (a client paying for one identity should not own three); the house's pre-existing materials, methods, code libraries and system components stay with the house under a perpetual, royalty-free licence to the client for use within the deliverable; third-party materials (fonts, stock, music, talent) pass under their own licences, listed in the delivery note; the house keeps portfolio rights (4.6).

### 4.5 Confidentiality

A mutual NDA (non-disclosure agreement) with a term of 2 to 3 years from disclosure, trade secrets indefinite, the usual carve-outs (public domain, independently developed, legally compelled), a return-or-destroy clause and no non-compete (a non-compete is unenforceable against the house in India under section 27 of the Indian Contract Act 1872 beyond the term of the contract, and it would also block the house from its own market). The house's research (section 14.9) is confidential information of the house. In a pitch, the house asks for an NDA before presenting ideas, or includes the line "concepts presented remain the property of Mask360 until commissioned" in the submission; both are weak protection, and the real protection is section 5.5.

### 4.6 Portfolio and credit rights

The house may show the work in its portfolio, case studies, awards entries, social channels and press after public release, or after a 90-day embargo for unlaunched work, unless the client requested confidentiality in writing before signature. Credit reads "Agency: Mask360" with named team members. The client's veto is limited to unreleased work and confidential figures. The reason the house will not trade this away for a fee: the portfolio is the only durable asset an agency owns.

### 4.7 Termination

For convenience by either side on 30 days' notice for projects and 60 to 90 days for retainers, paying for work done to the notice date at rate card plus the kill fee of section 3.4. For cause on 15 days' notice with a cure period. Effect: deliverables paid for transfer; unpaid ones do not; work in progress passes only on payment. A retainer contract carries an automatic pause after two months of non-payment (section 3.5).

### 4.8 Liability caps

Cap the house's total liability under a SOW at the fees paid under that SOW in the 12 months before the claim; exclude indirect and consequential loss (lost profit, lost revenue, reputational loss); carve out IP indemnity, confidentiality breach and wilful misconduct from the cap because clients will insist and courts expect it. The reason: a Rs 30 lakh identity must not expose the house to a Rs 300 crore brand's claimed lost sales. Indian law helps: under sections 73 and 74 of the Indian Contract Act 1872 courts award reasonable compensation up to, never beyond, a stipulated sum (Fateh Chand v Balkishan Dass, Supreme Court 1963; Kailash Nath Associates v DDA, Supreme Court 2015). The UAE Civil Transactions Law (Federal Law No. 5 of 1985) lets courts adjust agreed compensation to the actual loss, so a UAE cap is a ceiling the court may lower, never a floor.

### 4.9 The AI clause

Every SOW since 2024 carries an AI clause with six parts: the house may use generative tools in process (ideation, comps, retouching, variants, code) under its own disclosure rules; final deliverables containing AI-generated elements are identified in the delivery note; the house does not enter client confidential material into tools whose terms allow training on inputs (enterprise and API tiers generally exclude it, consumer tiers often do not; check the current terms per tool); no synthetic likeness or voice of a real person without that person's written consent; labelling follows the ASCI guidelines on synthetically generated content (section 14.6) and platform rules; the client may prohibit AI in named deliverables, priced accordingly. The IP warranty and indemnity exclude wholly AI-generated elements, because copyright offices (the US Copyright Office's 2023 guidance and its January 2025 report on copyrightability take the same line) treat output without human authorship as unprotectable, and the client should know what it owns. The craft side of this is 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md.

### 4.10 The talent and music clauses

Talent: the release (section 7.5) states term, territory, media, exclusivity and fee; renewals are the client's cost; the house prefers the production company or the client to contract talent directly on the house's form of release, so that liability for using an image past its term sits with the party using it. Music: the licence or assignment is named in the SOW (library plan and tier, bespoke assignment, or commercial sync with both master and publishing cleared); the client warrants it will not use the music beyond the licensed term and media; cue sheets are delivered with the film. The reason for both: usage breaches are the most common claim against agencies in India and the Gulf, and they are claims the house can route to the right party only if the paper says so.

### 4.11 Indian and UAE law basics

India. Contracts are governed by the Indian Contract Act 1872; electronic signatures are valid under the Information Technology Act 2000 (Aadhaar e-sign or a digital signature certificate); stamp duty on service agreements is nominal and state-specific, paid by e-stamp; the limitation period for a contract claim is three years (Limitation Act 1963); courts take years, so the house's contracts seat arbitration in Mumbai under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 with a sole arbitrator, and payment disputes with large buyers go to the MSME Samadhaan route (section 13.5). Commissioned-work copyright is section 4.4.

UAE. Onshore contracts fall under the Civil Transactions Law (Federal Law No. 5 of 1985 as amended) and the Commercial Transactions Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 50 of 2022); onshore courts work in Arabic and require certified translations of every document; the DIFC Courts and ADGM Courts are English-language common-law courts and parties can opt into DIFC Courts jurisdiction by contract even without a DIFC presence; arbitration is seated at DIAC (Dubai International Arbitration Centre), which absorbed the DIFC-LCIA under Dubai Decree No. 34 of 2021. A bounced cheque remains a serious matter for the issuer, which is why post-dated cheques still work as security. Media content is regulated under Federal Decree-Law No. 55 of 2023 on Media Regulation and the UAE Media Council's 2025 regulations (section 14.7).

### 4.12 When to involve a lawyer

Always: any MSA; any client paper; any contract above Rs 25 lakh or AED 150,000; cross-border contracts; celebrity talent; commercial music sync; anything with uncapped liability, exclusivity or a non-compete; a data processing agreement; equity or revenue share instead of fees; a dispute. Budget 1 to 2 percent of contract value for review on large contracts and a fixed annual retainer with a commercial lawyer in each country for the small questions, because the expensive mistakes are the ones nobody asked about.

## 5. Pitching

### 5.1 RFI and RFP responses

An RFI (request for information) asks who the house is: credentials, size, clients, process, finances. An RFP (request for proposal) asks for scope and price, and sometimes for creative. Qualify before answering either. Go only when: the decision maker is reachable before submission; the budget is stated or discoverable; the number of agencies invited is four or fewer; the timeline allows at least three weeks for creative; the IP terms do not claim pitch ideas; price carries less than 40 percent of the scoring weight (above that, the buyer is buying rate, and rate is what the house is worst at). Answer the questions in their order, in their numbering, so the procurement reader can score; put the house's own story on one page at the front, because the decision maker reads only that page.

### 5.2 Chemistry meetings

Sixty to ninety minutes, the founder and the CD in the room, three to five cases chosen for this brief and shown large, and a listening ratio of about 60 to 40 (the house asks more than it tells). The goal is to hear the real problem under the brief and to leave one point of view the client did not have before; the solution waits for the paid phase. Send a one-page recap within 24 hours.

### 5.3 Credentials decks

Twelve to twenty slides in the house grammar: work shown large and unlabelled, the studio voice in one or two lines, typographic identity, a single moving moment, credentials as proof (names, numbers, awards), the team, the point of view (design-taste, references/worlds.md, the agency and studio showcase entry; design-taste, SKILL.md, section 7 for the dark client deck). Keep a master credentials file sectioned by category (hospitality, fashion, beauty, jewellery, real estate, F&B, events) with case pages kept current, so a bespoke deck assembles in two hours. What is left out: service grids, "we are a 360 agency" as a headline, logo walls, team photos with crossed arms.

### 5.4 Pitch theatre and its cost

Pitch theatre is the staged element: a dressed room, printed books, mock-ups, a film, a built prototype. It wins when the idea is strong and loses nothing when the idea is weak. Cost it honestly. A typical creative pitch at the house:

| Role | Hours | India at mid rate (Rs) | Dubai at mid rate (AED) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategist | 40 | 2,40,000 | 24,000 |
| Creative director | 40 | 4,80,000 | 44,000 |
| Art director | 60 | 3,30,000 | 30,000 |
| Designer (senior) | 80 | 4,00,000 | 40,000 |
| Copywriter | 40 | 2,20,000 | 20,000 |
| Motion designer | 30 | 1,65,000 | 15,000 |
| Producer | 20 | 70,000 | 7,000 |
| Account lead | 30 | 1,05,000 | 10,500 |
| Total time | 340 | about 20 lakh | about 190,000 |
| Hard costs (mock-ups, print, travel) | | 50,000 to 3,00,000 | 5,000 to 25,000 |

At a 30 percent win rate, each win costs three to four pitches, or Rs 60 to 80 lakh of unbilled senior time in India. A win must therefore carry at least that much in gross margin over its first two years to break even, which at a 55 percent margin means about Rs 1.2 to 1.5 crore of fees. That is the arithmetic behind the house rule: no full creative pitch for an account below about Rs 1 crore a year in India or AED 1 million in Dubai.

### 5.5 Paid pitches and the ethics of free pitching

Two positions. The first holds that free creative pitching is the cost of entry: the networks and procurement-led clients run it, and in India the "creative shootout" with four to eight agencies is still normal for FMCG, auto and telecom. The second holds that creative work in a pitch is spec work (work done on speculation, without pay) and corrodes the profession: AIGA's position against spec work, the NO!SPEC campaign, D&AD's guidance, and Blair Enns' The Win Without Pitching Manifesto (2010). The IPA and ISBA Pitch Positive Pledge (UK, 2022) sits between the two: it commits clients to pitch only when necessary and to say in writing why, to invite "the right number of agencies, not an excessive list", to respect working hours, weekends and public holidays in the timeline, and to give direct feedback win or lose; agencies commit to manage the workload of the pitch team and to accept the decision. House position: chemistry and credentials are free; strategic thinking is free up to one meeting; creative concepts are produced only against a pitch fee (Rs 2 to 10 lakh in India, AED 15,000 to 75,000 in the Gulf, credited against fees on award) or inside a paid discovery sprint of two to three weeks that replaces the pitch. The reason: a client who will not pay for ideas in the pitch will not pay for them in the contract, and the sprint shows the client the house's way of working, which is the thing actually being bought.

### 5.6 The win rate maths

Expected value of a pitch equals the probability of winning multiplied by the first-year gross margin, minus the pitch cost. Score the probability on a fixed card before committing: existing relationship (0 to 3), portfolio fit (0 to 3), budget confirmed (0 to 2), competitors (2 for one or two agencies, 1 for three or four, 0 for more), incumbent in the room (0 if yes, 1 if no). Go at 7 of 11 or above with a positive expected value. Track four numbers quarterly: pitches entered, win rate, cost per pitch, cost per win. The target is a win rate above 40 percent on qualified pitches, reached by declining the unqualified ones; a house that wins one in six is pitching too much, and the sixth win pays for the five losses only if it is large.

### 5.7 How to decline

Within 48 hours, by phone and then by email. One honest reason (timeline, conflict, fit, terms), an alternative where there is one (a paid sprint, a referral to a house that fits better), and an open door. A good decline is remembered as confidence; a late one is remembered as discourtesy.

### 5.8 Pitch follow-up and reference calls

Follow up within 24 hours with a one-page recap and answers to open questions; ask for the decision date; check in once on that date and once two weeks later; then stop. For reference calls, brief the referee on what was asked and on the result, choose referees who will name a problem the house solved (a reference with no problem sounds coached), and rotate referees so no client is called more than twice a year.

### 5.9 The pitch to win versus the pitch to learn

A pitch to win is fully resourced and theatrical. A pitch to learn is entered to understand a category or client type the house wants (a Saudi giga-project, a global tech brand), run lean under a fixed hour cap with the learning written up afterwards for section 9.9. Both are legitimate. A pitch to fill idle time is neither: it trains the team that pitching is free.

### 5.10 New business engines

- Referrals from clients, architects, hospitality consultants, PR firms, photographers and real estate brokers: the largest source for premium independents, and the cheapest; the QBR (section 8.7) is where to ask.
- Content: case films, the founder's channels (see the kaavish-personal-ig skill), the agency's social (section 12.3 and file 17 on social and short form), writing that shows judgement.
- Awards (section 12.5): a filter for pitch invitations and a recruiting signal.
- Outbound: researched, specific, few; the cold-email and linkedin-dm skills carry the method; the India account map in the new-business/india-agency-account-map folder tells the house who holds what and when a review is likely.
- Events and juries: Design Yatra, Goafest, Dubai Lynx week, Cannes; judging is cheaper than entering and reads better.
- Pipeline discipline: a weekly new business hour; stages (lead, qualified, chemistry, proposal, pitch, won, lost) with conversion rates per stage; qualified pipeline held at three times the revenue target because a third converts.

## 6. Timelines and project management

### 6.1 Typical durations

| Deliverable | Total | Phases (weeks) | What stretches it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity | 8 to 16 weeks | Discovery 2; strategy 2 to 3; concepts 3 to 4; development 2 to 3; guidelines and rollout 2 to 4 | Naming and trademark clearance (add 4 to 6); multi-market stakeholders |
| Website | 10 to 20 weeks | Discovery and IA 2; design 4 to 8; build 4 to 8 (overlapping design by 2); QA and launch 2 | Content written by the client; photography not shot; CMS complexity |
| App design | 12 to 24 weeks | Research 2 to 4; flows and wireframes 3 to 4; UI and system 4 to 8; prototype and test 2 to 4; handoff 2 | Engineering availability; compliance (payments, KYC) |
| Campaign | 6 to 12 weeks | Brief 1; strategy 1 to 2; concepts 2 to 3; production 2 to 4; adaptation 1 to 2 | Talent and location availability; legal clearance |
| Brand film | 4 to 10 weeks | Pre-production 2 to 4; shoot 1 to 3 days; post 2 to 4 | Weather, permits, music clearance, celebrity schedules |
| Social month | Rolling 4-week cycle | Plan week 1; shoot week 2; produce weeks 2 to 3; approve week 3; schedule week 4 for next month | Late approvals; events and festivals |
| Deck | 1 to 3 weeks | Outline 2 days; write 3 to 5 days; design 3 to 5 days; revise 2 days | Missing numbers; rewriting after design |
| Print piece | 4 to 8 weeks plus 2 to 3 print | Copy and layout 2 to 4; photography 1 to 2; artwork 1; proofing and print 2 to 3 | Paper availability; proof rounds; binding |

These durations assume two rounds per stage and feedback within five working days; each extra round adds a week and each late approval adds its delay plus a re-start cost.

### 6.2 Critical path and dependencies

The critical path is the chain of dependent tasks whose total duration sets the end date; any slip on it slips delivery one for one. The recurring dependencies in this business: strategy sign-off before concepts; copy before layout (layout around lorem ipsum is redone); photography before final site design, or agreed crop ratios and placeholders; fonts licensed before guidelines ship; trademark clearance before a name is revealed to anyone outside the room; legal and claims clearance before a film shoots; client assets (product, data, logos, access) before build; Arabic and Hindi translation before bilingual layout, because line counts and reading direction change the page (see 03-typography.md on multiscript setting). Mark the client's dependencies in the plan in their own colour; the schedule is shared, so the delay is too.

### 6.3 Gates and approvals

Gates, in order: brief sign-off; strategy sign-off; concept selection; design development sign-off; pre-production (the PPM, pre-production meeting, where boards, cast, locations, wardrobe and schedule are approved); final artwork sign-off; launch readiness. Each gate has a named approver, a written approval (an email or a tool comment, never a verbal), and a date. Nothing proceeds past a gate without it, and the gate is where any change-order conversation happens, because the cost of change doubles at every gate after it.

### 6.4 Buffers

Add 20 percent to every internal estimate and one week per client approval cycle; hold the buffer, never show it as a line. Indian clients promise five working days and deliver ten to fifteen; Gulf throughput halves in Ramadan (expected to begin about 8 February 2027; confirm the date each year) and around the Eids; India loses the Diwali and Holi weeks and the wedding season slows founder-led clients from November to February. Plan calendars around all of these at proposal time, because a deadline that lands in Ramadan was never real.

### 6.5 The sprint model for design

For product and site work, two-week sprints with design running one sprint ahead of engineering. Ceremonies: planning (what ships this sprint), a daily 15-minute stand-up, a review where the client sees working software or finished screens, and a retrospective. A definition of done per ticket includes design QA against the system (see 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md). Keep critique separate from sprint review: the review is for acceptance, the crit is for craft. Linear is the house tool for product teams (issues, cycles, projects, fast keyboard-driven triage); Figma branches (section 9.2) keep the library stable while a sprint explores.

### 6.7 Status reporting and the weekly client rhythm

Monday, 30 minutes, internal traffic meeting across every live job (traffic is the function that routes work through the studio and spots collisions). Monday, one status email per account: done last week, this week, needed from you and by when, risks, with a RAG flag (red, amber, green) and a one-line reason for anything not green. Mid-week, a work-in-progress review with the client where the stage calls for one. Friday, the internal craft review (section 10.4). The reason for the fixed rhythm: clients who hear from the house every Monday stop calling on Tuesday.

### 6.8 Risk management

A risk register per project: risk, likelihood (1 to 3), impact (1 to 3), owner, mitigation, trigger. The ten that recur: client approval delay; scope creep; the founder client changing the idea after concept selection; key freelancer unavailability (the DP booked elsewhere); weather (Mumbai monsoon June to September, Dubai heat June to September); permits (Dubai public locations need a Dubai Film and TV Commission permit; Mumbai public locations need municipal and police permissions); payment delay; vendor failure; platform spec change mid-production (see 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md); legal or trademark clearance failure. Each has a mitigation in this file; the register's job is to make the owner say the risk out loud on Monday.

## 7. Production management

### 7.1 Sourcing vendors

Categories the house buys: photographers and DPs; directors; editors and colourists; 3D artists; printers and finishers; fabricators (exhibition, retail, signage); composers and sound designers; voice artists; casting; locations; stylists, hair and make-up; retouchers; translators (Hindi, Marathi, Arabic). Where they are found: the house database first (section 7.9), then the portfolios of people whose work the CD has already admired (Instagram and Behance for stills and design, Vimeo for film, ArtStation for 3D), then referrals from crew already trusted. Vetting, in order of weight: portfolio match for this brief's light and feel (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 2); references from the last three jobs; reliability on dates; kit and crew; rate; usage terms; insurance; tax registration (a GST-registered vendor in India gives the house input credit, which is 18 percent of the invoice).

### 7.2 Briefing vendors

The vendor brief carries: objective; the strategy in two lines (vendors do better work when they know why); references with the exact thing borrowed from each (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 4); deliverables with specs (see 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md); schedule; usage terms; budget band; approval path; who from the house is on set and who signs off. Send it in writing and walk through it on a call. A vendor briefed by WhatsApp voice note delivers a WhatsApp voice note's worth of precision.

### 7.3 Quotes

Three quotes, like for like: the same deliverables, days, crew size, kit, usage and post, on one comparison sheet, or the comparison is meaningless. A production estimate lists pre-production, crew, talent, locations and permits, art and props, wardrobe, hair and make-up, equipment, transport and catering, post (edit, grade, sound, VFX), music, insurance, a contingency of 10 percent, and the house mark-up (15 to 20 percent when the house carries cost and risk, 10 percent handling when the client pays the vendor directly, disclosed in both cases). The cheapest quote is checked for what it left out before it is believed.

### 7.4 Production insurance

Types: public liability (injury or damage to third parties on set); equipment cover (hired kit is the hirer's responsibility, and a single cinema lens can cost Rs 10 lakh); production or shoot insurance (negative and media, weather, cast non-appearance) for large shoots; workers' compensation for crew (the Employees' Compensation Act 1923 in India); professional indemnity for the agency itself (section 14.10). Rule: no shoot without public liability and kit cover in place and named on the call sheet; check whether the client's or the production company's policy already covers the day before buying a second one.

### 7.5 Contracts and releases

Crew deal memos (role, days, rate, overtime, credit, IP); talent releases (usage term, territory, media, fee, exclusivity, right to alter, no obligation to use); property and location releases; parental consent for minors; composer and voice agreements with assignment or licence terms; a release written in a language the signatory reads (Hindi, Marathi or Arabic as needed), because consent that was not understood is not consent, and the names of the house's model release forms are kept in the vendor database.

### 7.6 On-set management

A call sheet the evening before (times, locations, contacts, weather, nearest hospital, the shot list). The house's roles on set: the CD or AD approves frames against the boards; the producer guards the schedule and the money; one client representative with one voice, agreed in advance. The shot list is signed off before wrap. Shift rules: Indian film crews work a 12-hour shift with overtime after it; Dubai crews 10 to 12 hours; heat protocols in the Gulf summer; Ramadan hours for fasting crew. A wrap report the same day: what was shot, what was missed, what changed, what it cost.

### 7.7 Post schedules and approvals

Offline cut v1 within five to seven working days of wrap; two rounds of offline; picture lock; then online, grade, sound design, music and voice; then deliverables per spec. Clients approve picture lock before grade begins, because every change after grade is paid for twice. Approvals live in Frame.io with timestamped comments (section 9.5); the editorial workflow itself is in 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 5.

### 7.8 Delivery and archiving

Deliver the package of section 9.4 with a delivery note. Archive: freeze the project folder; keep camera originals and RAW for two years on LTO tape or cold cloud storage (Backblaze B2, AWS Glacier or Google Archive class); keep final masters and source files indefinitely; record usage expiry dates in the delivery note and in the house calendar with a 60-day reminder. The reason for the two-year RAW rule: re-use requests arrive at 18 months, when the client's next campaign is planned.

## 8. Client management

### 8.1 Onboarding and immersion

Kick-off within five working days of signature. Immersion: visit the property, the store, the showroom or the site; read the last three years of the brand's work; interview five to eight stakeholders for 30 minutes each; hear the founder's story from the founder; collect assets and guidelines; audit the brand against the category (design-taste, references/worlds.md). Set up the shared workspace, the review tools and the onboarding document: contacts, approvers, cadence, tools, invoicing details, PO process, vendor registration on the client's system (which in India can take three weeks and gates every payment). The reason: most later friction is set in the first two weeks, when the house either learnt who decides or did not.

### 8.2 Stakeholder mapping, decision makers and approvers

Map every account: the decider (signs off; the founder or CMO), the approvers (sign off on stages), the influencers (the founder's spouse, the architect, the brand consultant, the agency the client used before), the gatekeepers (procurement, legal, the executive assistant), and the users (the marketing manager who lives with the work daily). Write the map, agree in the SOW who approves what, and insist on a decider check-in at concept selection even when the decider skips the other reviews, because a decider who sees the work for the first time at final artwork resets the project to concept.

### 8.3 The single point of feedback

The SOW names one person through whom all feedback is consolidated within five working days of a presentation; conflicting views are resolved on the client side before they reach the house; the house does not act on feedback from anyone else, including the decider's late-night message, until it comes through that person. The reason: unconsolidated feedback doubles rounds and the house is paid for two.

### 8.4 Rounds policy, difficult feedback and the taste education

Rounds are defined in section 3.4. Present every stage with a recommendation and the reasons for it; a presentation of options with no opinion asks the client to be the creative director. Handling feedback: listen to the end, restate it, separate the problem from the proposed fix ("make the logo bigger" is a fix; the problem is "I cannot see the brand", which has better fixes), and answer the problem. Taste education happens over months: references shown at every stage, the physics of expensive explained in plain words (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 2), the cover-the-logo test done together, and the category's tropes named and refused in the brief (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 4.3). Never argue taste in email; call, then confirm in writing. When the feedback is "I don't like it", ask what it would need to feel like and return to the single idea agreed at the brief; if the idea has changed, that is a change order and a conversation with the decider.

### 8.5 Scope and expectation setting, and saying no

Set expectations at the brief: what premium costs in rounds, weeks and money, and what the client must bring. Say no to unpriced scope, timelines that force cheap work, feedback outside the protocol, and work that would hurt the brand, because the house's name goes on it. The form of no: acknowledge the request, state the consequence (cost, time, quality), offer the alternative with a price. A no with a priced alternative is a yes the client can afford.

### 8.6 Client archetypes

- The founder client (most of the house's luxury clients): fast, intuitive, changes mind, buys taste and people, and messages at midnight. Needs the decider check-in, the single idea in one sentence they can repeat, a 20-minute weekly call, and a named senior on the account. Beware the influencer circle (section 8.2) and the WhatsApp approval that the marketing team never saw.
- The marketing manager client: needs to look good to a boss. Wants rationale, documentation, options and safety. Give them the deck that sells the work internally, the numbers, and a rehearsal before they present it upward.
- The procurement conversation: procurement measures rates, discounts and compliance. Move the conversation from rate to scope: three options (section 3.1), scope removed before price is cut, the rate card ranges the networks quote held in reserve, the scoring criteria asked for, and the decider kept informed, because procurement cannot buy taste and knows it.

### 8.7 Relationship rhythm

Weekly status (section 6.7). Monthly review for retainers: output, results, next month's plan. A QBR (quarterly business review) with the decider: results, learning, roadmap, relationship health, and the question "what would make us better for you". Annual planning in the last quarter of the client's fiscal year (April to March in India; calendar year in the UAE) with a one-page "state of the brand" and the next year's scope and fee. Reviews are also where referrals and case study permissions are asked for, at the moment of a success rather than at the end of a contract.

### 8.8 Churn signals

Slower approvals; a new marketing head; a request for "benchmarking" rates or a procurement review; reduced scope at renewal; missed QBRs; another agency's name in conversation; late payment where it was punctual; a request for all source files and passwords. Response: a direct conversation with the decider within a week, a re-pitch plan where the account is worth it, and a clean exit plan where it is not. Waiting is the only wrong move.

### 8.9 Upsell and cross-sell with integrity

Propose only what the brand needs and the house can deliver at the same standard; put the "what next" page in the QBR; never bundle a weak discipline to fill a retainer; where the house subcontracts (a specialist 3D studio, a media buyer), say so and mark it up transparently. The reason: a premium house's cross-sell is believed exactly as long as it has never been padding.

### 8.10 References, case studies and the breakup

Ask for the case study, the reference and the testimonial in launch week, with the case format of section 12.4. The breakup comes when an account stays unprofitable after re-scoping, when conduct is abusive to staff, or when the client's taste and the house's cannot meet. Give notice per the contract, hand over cleanly (files, passwords, vendor contacts, a transition document), say nothing in public, and keep the door open; clients return and decision makers move companies.

## 9. Creative operations

### 9.1 File structure and naming conventions

One structure for every project, so that anyone can find anything in a project they have never opened:

```
CLIENT_Project_2026/
  01_Admin            proposal, SOW, PO, invoices, approvals
  02_Brief_Strategy   brief, strategy, research
  03_References       reference boards, competitor audit
  04_Assets_In        client-supplied, dated subfolders
  05_Design           Figma links, working files by discipline
  06_Copy             copy decks, translations
  07_Production       shoot, post, print, vendor quotes
  08_Review           every export sent to the client, by round
  09_Final            approved masters and the delivery package
  10_Archive          frozen at close
```

Naming: CLIENT_Project_Deliverable_Variant_v03_20261014_KR.ext. Versions run v01 to v99 and the word "final" never appears in a filename; the 09_Final folder is the only "final". Dates in ISO 8601 order (YYYYMMDD) so files sort by time. No spaces or special characters; Devanagari and Arabic in filenames break some render pipelines and CDNs, so transliterate. Review exports carry the round (R1, R2). The reason: naming is retrieval, and a file that cannot be found is a file that gets remade.

### 9.2 Version control for design

Figma branching and merging are available on the Organization and Enterprise plans (and the Education K-12 and Government plans), with no cap on the number of branches; creating a branch needs a Full seat, and merging needs edit access to the main file, with a review step for anyone without it (Figma help centre, read October 2026). Use branches for explorations on a published library and for keeping the client-visible file stable; name a version in version history at every gate ("R2 presented 2026-10-14"). Abstract, the git-style version control built for Sketch, is legacy and the house does not run Sketch. Code lives in Git on GitHub, trunk-based with short-lived branches and pull request review; design tokens live in the repo (see 07-design-systems-and-tokens.md).

### 9.3 Asset management, DAM and brand portals

A DAM (digital asset management system) is the searchable, permissioned library of approved assets with metadata and usage terms. Tools: Bynder and Brandfolder (now part of Smartsheet) for enterprise libraries; Frontify for brand guidelines with an integrated library, the house default for client brand portals because guidelines, assets and downloadable formats sit in one place; Dropbox and Google Drive for working files. Drive structure: a Shared Drive per client (never a person's My Drive, because ownership must survive people leaving), with the project structure of section 9.1 inside. A brand portal is the living guidelines site (Frontify, or a custom Next.js site for identities where the house wants control of the typography); for identities above about Rs 15 lakh or AED 75,000 the house delivers a portal and a PDF, because a PDF is out of date the day a new asset is made.

### 9.6 Systems for recurring work

Build a format once for each recurring thing: social months (a Figma file with artboards per platform format and the brand's components), decks (the house system: dark client deck, white house deck), proposals (the ten sections of 4.1 in Notion), reports, invoices (Zoho Books or Tally in India, Zoho Books or Xero in the UAE, both with e-invoicing support), call sheets, delivery notes, vendor briefs. The reason: a format removes the fifth of every job spent reinventing structure and keeps the craft hours for craft. The risk is the one design-taste names in section 3: the format must never become the design.

### 9.7 QA gates and checklists

The preflight checklists per deliverable are in file 23 (critique, portfolio review and preflight). The operational rule here: no deliverable leaves the building without its checklist signed by someone other than its maker. The items that recur across every checklist: spelling and grammar in every language; legal lines and disclaimers; logo clear space; colour profile; bleed; font embedding; link checks; alt text; platform specs; captions; usage expiry on the assets used; the AI label where section 14.6 requires it.

### 9.8 Post mortems and retros

Within two weeks of delivery, 45 minutes, the team and the account lead: what was planned, what happened, what was learnt, what changes (one to three changes with owners and dates). Blameless by rule. The numbers on the table: estimated versus actual hours, rounds planned versus actual, gross margin, the client's satisfaction score. Every learning either changes a reference file in this corpus (section 9.9) or is discarded; a learning that lives in a meeting note is forgotten in a month.

### 9.9 Knowledge management

This skill corpus is the house's knowledge system: a written, versioned, cross-referenced reference that a new person or Claude reads before acting. Rules: every file has an owner and an as-of date; claims that can change carry their date; post mortems feed it; a quarterly review retires what is stale; the operational layer (clients, vendors, projects, rates) lives in Notion and the craft layer lives here. The reason to write it rather than teach it in the corridor: taste that lives only in the founder's head leaves when he is on a plane.

### 9.10 Time tracking, utilisation and capacity planning

Utilisation is billable hours divided by available hours. Available hours per person per year are about 1,800 to 1,900 after public holidays (10 to 15 in India depending on state; about 14 in the UAE) and leave (15 to 24 days). Targets: 65 to 75 percent for makers, 40 to 50 percent for CDs, 20 to 30 percent for the founder. Track in Harvest or Toggl, review weekly. Below 60 percent means too few projects or too much unbilled work; above 80 percent means no time for crits, references or rest, and the quality of the next job pays. Capacity planning: a rolling 12-week view of booked hours per person against capacity, booked to 80 percent so the unexpected fits; hire (section 13.8) when the view runs above 90 percent for six consecutive weeks with pipeline to support it.

## 10. Quality system

### 10.1 The CD sign-off

Nothing reaches a client without the CD's sign-off, recorded in the review tool ("Approved by CD, date"). The CD reviews against the brief, the single idea, the taste tests (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 4.7) and the preflight. The sign-off means the CD owns the failure if it fails; that is the point of it.

### 10.2 Preflight checklists per deliverable

Held in file 23, one per deliverable type (identity, guidelines, site, app, campaign, film, social, deck, print), each run by a second pair of eyes. The quality system here only enforces the gate: a deliverable without a signed preflight is not finished.

### 10.3 The standards document

Two to four pages of the house's non-negotiables per discipline, drawn from the corpus: type setting (03-typography.md), colour (04-colour.md), motion (12-motion-principles-and-ui-motion.md), copy (the mask360-copywriting skill), the house layer (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 7), file naming (9.1), the AI rules (4.9 and 14.6). Read on day one, signed, revised quarterly. The reason for keeping it short: a 40-page standards document is read once; a 3-page one is used.

### 10.4 Calibration sessions and the craft review cadence

Calibration: once a month, the team scores five pieces of work (three from the house, two from outside) as expensive, competent or cheap, independently and in writing, then compares. The purpose is agreement on the scale, so that "cheap" means the same thing in every mouth, and the disagreements are where the teaching happens. Craft review: every Friday, 60 minutes, work in progress, in the critique format of design-taste, SKILL.md, section 8 (verdict, what works, what does not and why, the fix with the reference). Quarterly: a portfolio review of everything shipped, with the question of which pieces the house would show.

### 10.5 Measuring quality

| Measure | Definition | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Rework rate | Hours spent after client approval divided by total hours | Under 10 percent |
| Rounds per stage | Average rounds actually used | 2 or fewer on 80 percent of stages |
| Out-of-scope revisions | Count of revision requests priced as change orders | Tracked per client; rising count is a churn signal |
| On-time delivery | Milestones met on the agreed date | 90 percent |
| Awards | Entries, shortlists, wins per year | Shortlist rate above 30 percent of entries |
| Referrals | New clients introduced by existing ones | Half of new business |
| Satisfaction | A 1 to 10 score at every QBR | 8 or above |
| Portfolio inclusion | Share of shipped projects the CD would show | Above 50 percent |

The reason for measuring: a quality system without numbers is a mood.

## 11. Talent

### 11.1 Hiring for taste

Portfolio review (method in file 23): five minutes per portfolio, the two-second test, cover the logo, look for the edit (what they left out) and for one project they can explain in depth. ### 11.6 Culture of craft

Burnout signals: missed crits, defensive feedback, late nights as a habit, falling utilisation with rising hours. The response is a conversation and a workload change in the same week. The reason: the taste the house sells lives in rested heads, and a tired senior approves cheap work.

### 11.7 Performance reviews for creatives

Twice a year, portfolio-based (what was made, before and after, what was removed), competency-based (the six of 11.2), and behavioural (crit contribution, reliability, client conduct). The output is a growth plan with named projects for the next six months and a compensation decision once a year (April in India, January in the UAE, to match budget cycles).

### 11.8 Letting go

For performance: a written 60 to 90 day plan with observable outputs, weekly check-ins, then exit with notice per contract (30 to 90 days is typical in India under the state Shops and Establishments Acts and the employment contract; the UAE Labour Law, Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021, sets notice at 30 to 90 days and end-of-service gratuity). For redundancy: say it plainly, on the day, with the most generous terms the house can afford, portfolio help and a reference. Never by message. The reason beyond decency: the market is small, and the person leaving will be on a client's side of the table within two years.

## 12. The agency's own brand

### 12.1 Positioning

One sentence, held for years: the house that luxury hospitality, fashion, beauty, jewellery and real estate in India and the Gulf call when the work must look expensive and current. The proof is the work, the client names and the founder's judgement in public. Everything else (services, process, size) is detail. ## 13. Finance basics for creative leaders

### 13.1 Revenue per head

Revenue per head is annual fee revenue (excluding pass-through production costs) divided by total headcount. It falls out of the rate card and utilisation: a mid designer at Rs 3,000 an hour, 1,850 available hours, 70 percent utilisation yields about Rs 38.9 lakh a year; a senior at Rs 5,000 and 65 percent yields about Rs 60 lakh; a CD at Rs 10,000 and 45 percent yields about Rs 83 lakh; account, ops and finance staff bill nothing directly. Blended across a 15-person house (section 2.2) that gives about Rs 8 to 15 crore, or Rs 55 lakh to 1 crore per head. House targets: Rs 35 to 60 lakh per head in India and AED 350,000 to 600,000 in Dubai; below the floor, either rates or utilisation is wrong.

### 13.2 Gross margin

Gross margin is fee revenue minus the direct cost of delivery (billable staff cost at loaded rates, freelancers, and pass-through costs billed at cost), divided by fee revenue. Targets: 50 to 60 percent on projects; 40 to 50 percent on retainers (lower, because a retainer sells capacity the client does not always use); 15 to 25 percent net after overhead. Worked example: a Rs 20 lakh identity uses 320 staff hours at a loaded cost of Rs 2,000 (Rs 6.4 lakh) and Rs 1.5 lakh of freelance, so gross margin is Rs 12.1 lakh (60 percent); overhead allocated at 35 percent of revenue is Rs 7 lakh; net is Rs 5.1 lakh (25 percent). Add one extra round at 40 hours and the net falls to Rs 4.3 lakh; add three, and the project is at break-even. That is why section 3.4 counts rounds.

### 13.3 Overhead and the loaded cost

Overhead is every cost that is not a billable person: rent, software, admin and finance staff, new business, insurance, the founder's non-billable time. Premium independents run 30 to 40 percent of revenue. Loaded cost per hour equals (annual salary multiplied by about 1.2 for statutory and benefits, plus the person's share of overhead) divided by billable hours. Example: a designer on Rs 12 lakh CTC costs Rs 14.4 lakh loaded, carries Rs 6 lakh of overhead, and bills 1,300 hours a year, so the loaded cost is about Rs 1,570 an hour; at a Rs 3,500 rate the hour earns 55 percent gross margin. Every rate on the card in section 3.2 was built this way, which is why it does not move for a client who asks nicely.

### 13.4 Pricing to margin

Price equals hours multiplied by loaded cost, divided by one minus the target margin, plus third-party costs with mark-up; then adjust upward to value (section 3.1) where the client's upside justifies it. Never price below loaded cost plus 40 percent except for a documented reason (a portfolio piece, a category the house wants to enter), capped at 10 percent of annual revenue, because every discounted job is paid for by the others.

### 13.5 Cash flow and the receivables problem in India

DSO (days sales outstanding) is receivables divided by daily revenue; Indian independents run 60 to 120 days against a contractual 30. Cash rules: three months of overhead in the bank at all times; advances and milestones as in section 3.5; invoice the day the milestone lands, on the client's portal; a weekly receivables call. The statutory lever: under section 15 of the MSMED Act 2006, a buyer must pay a registered micro or small enterprise within the agreed period, which cannot exceed 45 days (15 days where nothing is agreed); under section 16, delayed payment carries compound interest at three times the RBI bank rate; from assessment year 2024 to 2025, section 43B(h) of the Income-tax Act denies the buyer a tax deduction for sums owed to micro and small enterprises beyond that period, which made finance departments notice the rule. Udyam registration is the key to all of it; the revised thresholds announced in the Union Budget 2025 and in force from 1 April 2025 put a micro enterprise at investment up to Rs 2.5 crore and turnover up to Rs 10 crore and a small enterprise at Rs 25 crore and Rs 100 crore, so a 15-person house qualifies. Disputes go to the MSME Samadhaan portal and the state Facilitation Council. Receivables from large buyers can also be discounted on the TReDS platforms (RXIL, M1xchange, Invoicemart). In the UAE the levers are the advance, the LPO (local purchase order, without which nothing is paid), the post-dated cheque, and the DIFC Courts' small claims track for amounts up to AED 500,000 when the contract opts in.

### 13.6 Retainers as stability

Hold 40 to 60 percent of revenue in retainers. Design them as capacity in days per month (never as a count of posts), with unused days expiring monthly or rolling one month, a quarterly re-scope, an annual price review tied to the rate card, and 60 to 90 days' notice. The margin is lower than projects, but a retainer paid monthly in advance is worth more than its margin in India, because it is the only revenue that arrives on a date.

### 13.7 The cost of a pitch

Section 5.4: about Rs 20 lakh of senior time plus hard costs per full creative pitch in India, about AED 190,000 in Dubai; at a 30 percent win rate the cost per win is Rs 60 to 80 lakh. Record it per pitch in the time system so the number is real and the go or no-go card of 5.6 is honest.

### 13.8 When to hire

Hire when booked capacity runs above 90 percent for six consecutive weeks and three months of the role's loaded cost is covered by contracted work; otherwise freelance for three months first. Order of hires for a growing house: a producer or project manager before another designer (the bottleneck is coordination, section 2.2); a design engineer before a developer; a senior before a junior, because juniors need seniors to become anything.

### 13.9 Pricing AI-augmented work

Three cases. Where AI cuts the hours on variants and adaptations, pass part of the saving through: adaptation sets priced per unit at about half of their 2023 price, and the volume won on it. Where AI is the medium (generated film, synthetic imagery, voice), price the idea and the direction as creative fees, pass the tool costs through with mark-up (Higgsfield, Runway, Midjourney, Kling and similar credits), and disclose per sections 4.9 and 14.6. Where AI is in ideation and process, nothing changes in the price; it is the house's method. Value pricing stays for ideas, identities and films (section 1.6), and the client is shown the machine wherever the machine is doing machine work.

## 14. Legal and ethics

### 14.1 Trademark checks

Before any name or mark is presented: a knock-out search on the IP India public search (ipindiaonline.gov.in) under the Trade Marks Act 1999 in the relevant Nice classes (3 cosmetics, 14 jewellery, 25 clothing, 35 advertising and retail, 36 real estate, 41 entertainment, 43 hospitality); the UAE Ministry of Economy trademark register for the Gulf; the WIPO Global Brand Database for international marks; domain and social handle availability. The house's search is indicative and the proposal says so; a lawyer's clearance opinion precedes launch, and the Madrid Protocol (India since 2013, UAE since 2021) is the route to international filings. The reason: a name revealed and then withdrawn costs the client the launch and the house the account.

### 14.2 Model and property releases

As in section 7.5. Personality rights in India are judge-made: Titan Industries v Ramkumar Jewellers (Delhi High Court, 2012) on using a celebrity's image without consent, and Anil Kapoor v Simply Life India (Delhi High Court, 2023) protecting a performer's name, voice and likeness against AI misuse. In the UAE, photographing or publishing a person's image without consent is an offence under the cybercrime law (Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021) and the Penal Code, with fines and imprisonment, so no street photography of identifiable people in Dubai without a release.

### 14.3 Music sync

Both a master licence (the recording, from the label) and a synchronisation licence (the composition, from the publisher) are needed for any commercial recording; library platforms (Artlist, Epidemic Sound, Musicbed) bundle both within their plan terms, and paid-media coverage must be confirmed per plan; Indian film music sits with labels (T-Series, Saregama, Sony Music India, Zee Music) and is priced per section 3.7; events and public playback in India need IPRS (composition) and PPL (recording) licences. Cue sheets go with every film.

### 14.4 Font licensing

Desktop, web, app, broadcast and logo licences are separate products at most foundries, and a wordmark drawn from a licensed font may need a logo licence; the client buys fonts in its own name; the house never ships font files outside the EULA. Detail in 03-typography.md.

### 14.5 Stock and image licensing

Getty, Shutterstock, Adobe Stock, Stocksy and Unsplash+ for commercially released imagery; editorial licences do not cover advertising; sensitive-use clauses exclude certain categories; model and property releases are checked per image. The house avoids stock on principle (design-taste, SKILL.md, section 2) and treats generic AI imagery as stock with extra steps; where AI imagery is used, section 14.6 governs.

### 14.6 AI disclosure

ASCI's Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising were signed by the Board of Governors on 17 September 2026 and take effect three months after publication, so from mid December 2026. Synthetically generated content (SGC) is audio, visual or audio-visual content in an advertisement that is artificially or algorithmically created or materially altered to appear real. Labelling is mandatory where SGC materially influences a consumer decision; the guideline's own examples include synthetic influencers and ambassadors, a real person's replicated likeness or voice even with consent, fabricated events or settings, a product that does not yet exist ("a 3D model of an unbuilt housing complex", which is every real estate launch the house works on), product-relevant sound design, and paid AI recommendations, which must read "Sponsored by [Brand]". No label is needed for minor enhancements (colour correction, blemish removal, copy refinement), decorative backgrounds and ambient sound, obviously fantastical effects, text and administrative uses, and accessibility aids. Suggested labels are "Audio/Video created using AI" and "Audio/Video enhanced using AI". A label never cures prohibited content: fabricated endorsements, exaggerated results, non-existent locations, unauthorised copyrighted work, deepfakes or likeness without consent breach the code regardless. The house's own practice (the delivery note list, the no-synthetic-likeness rule, the craft tells) is in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md. For EU-facing work, the EU AI Act's transparency obligations for synthetic content (Article 50) apply from August 2026; for the Gulf, the UAE Media Council's content rules (14.7) and each platform's own synthetic-media labels apply.

### 14.7 The ASCI code and UAE advertising standards

The ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India) code has four chapters: truthful and honest representation, non-offensive to public decency, no harmful products or situations, and fair competition; Chapter III was last updated on 29 April 2024 and Chapter I carries the chairman's signature of 10 July 2025 (ascionline.in, read October 2026). Its guidelines include influencer advertising in digital media (May 2021: disclosure labels such as #ad or #collab, prominent, in the language of the post, for any material connection), harmful gender stereotypes (June 2022), and the synthetic content guideline above. ASCI is self-regulatory, and the statutory teeth sit in the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and the CCPA Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements 2022 (penalties up to Rs 10 lakh for a first violation and Rs 50 lakh after). Real estate adds RERA: every advertisement for a registered project carries the RERA registration number and unregistered projects may not be advertised (Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016; MahaRERA in Maharashtra), and in Dubai property advertising needs a permit number from the Dubai Land Department's Trakheesi system shown on the ad. In the UAE, Federal Decree-Law No. 55 of 2023 on Media Regulation and the UAE Media Council's 2025 regulations govern content: respect for religion, the state and national identity, public morals and the Arabic language in public advertising; no alcohol, tobacco or gambling advertising; an advertiser permit for anyone paid to advertise on social media (announced free of charge for an initial period in 2025; confirm current terms with the Council); fines running into hundreds of thousands of dirhams for breaches. Ramadan, National Day and modesty norms are craft matters in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md; here the rule is that the house checks every Gulf execution against the Council's standards before the client does.

### 14.8 Data protection

India: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (assented 11 August 2023) with the DPDP Rules 2025 notified in November 2025 and phased in over 18 months, so the substantive obligations bite in 2027 (confirm the exact commencement dates on the MeitY site): consent and notice, data fiduciary obligations, breach notification, children's data, and penalties up to Rs 250 crore for failing security safeguards. Relevance to the house: client CRM data for campaigns, lead forms on sites the house builds, contests, UGC, pixels and analytics. UAE: the Personal Data Protection Law, Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021, in force since 2 January 2022, regulated by the UAE Data Office under Federal Decree-Law No. 44 of 2021 (executive regulations still awaited per the government portal, read October 2026); DIFC entities fall under DIFC Law No. 5 of 2020 and ADGM entities under the ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021. EU clients and EU data subjects bring GDPR: lawful basis, a data processing agreement, standard contractual clauses for transfers. The house acts as a processor and signs a DPA in every MSA; sites the house builds ship with cookie consent and a privacy notice drafted by the client's counsel.

### 14.9 NDAs and competitive intelligence

The house's research is private: the India agency account map, the vendor database, the rate card, the pitch records and this corpus. Staff and freelancers sign confidentiality undertakings; nobody brings a previous employer's confidential material in; competitive intelligence is built from public sources only (press, awards, LinkedIn, the agencies' own sites) and never from a friend inside a rival. The reason beyond law: in a market this small, the house's reputation for discretion is a selling point to the clients who matter most.

### 14.10 Insurance

Professional indemnity (errors and omissions) with cover of Rs 1 to 5 crore in India and AED 1 to 5 million in the UAE, because Gulf networks and government entities ask for it in the MSA; public liability; cyber cover for client data; directors' and officers' cover for the company; workers' compensation; production insurance per shoot (section 7.4); mandatory employee health insurance in Dubai. Indian clients rarely ask; the house carries it anyway, because the claim that arrives is the one nobody asked about.
