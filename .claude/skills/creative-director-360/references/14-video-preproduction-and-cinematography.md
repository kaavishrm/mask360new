## 1. Roles and the set

### 1.1 Who does what

A film set is a chain of command built so that one decision (the director's) reaches forty hands without being retold. Every role exists because a specific failure happened often enough to need a person. Know the role by the question it answers.

| Role | Department | Owns | The question they answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Director | Creative | Performance, shot choice, the film's point of view | What is this moment for? |
| Director of photography (DP, "DOP" in India and the UK) | Camera and lighting | The image: lens, light, exposure, camera movement, the look | How does this moment look and feel? |
| Camera operator | Camera | Framing and moving the camera during the take (on small shoots the DP operates) | Is the frame right through the whole move? |
| First assistant camera (1st AC, the focus puller) | Camera | Focus, lens changes, camera build, marks with the actors | Is it sharp where it must be? |
| Second assistant camera (2nd AC, clapper loader) | Camera | Slate (the clapperboard that marks scene, shot and take for picture and sound sync), media cards, camera reports, marks on the floor | Which take is this, and where is the card? |
| Digital imaging technician (DIT) | Camera | Offload with checksum verification (a hash comparison proving each copy matches the card), on-set look (a LUT, a lookup table converting one set of colour values to another, or a CDL, the ASC (American Society of Cinematographers) Color Decision List of slope, offset, power and saturation values), exposure and focus QC (quality control), dailies (the day's footage, synced and with the look applied, for review) | Is the footage safe, and does it look as intended? |
| Data wrangler | Camera | Offload and backup only, on smaller sets where no DIT is booked | Are there three copies? |
| Gaffer (chief lighting technician) | Electric | Executes the DP's lighting plan, runs the electricians ("lightmen" in Mumbai) | Where does the light come from, and is it safe? |
| Best boy electric | Electric | The gaffer's deputy: crew, power distribution, rental orders, generator | Do we have the power and the people? |
| Key grip | Grip | Rigging, camera support (dollies, cranes, car mounts), flags, frames, negative fill (black surfaces that take light away from one side of the subject) | How is the camera held and the light shaped without touching the lamp? |
| Dolly grip | Grip | Pushes the dolly or crane, hits marks in time with the operator | Does the move land on the same frame every take? |
| Production sound mixer (sound recordist) | Sound | Recording dialogue and room tone, timecode | Is the dialogue clean? |
| Boom operator | Sound | Holds the boom microphone just outside frame | Is the mic close without being seen? |
| Producer | Production | Budget, schedule, contracts, client relationship, legal and insurance | Can we afford it, and are we allowed? |
| Line producer | Production | The budget line by line, vendor deals, daily cost control | What does each line cost today? |
| Production manager | Production | Logistics: transport, catering, accommodation, permit paperwork | Is everyone fed, moved and permitted? |
| First assistant director (1st AD) | Production | The shooting schedule, running the floor, safety, the order of the day | Are we on time, and is it safe? |
| Second assistant director (2nd AD) | Production | Call sheet, talent movement through hair and make-up (HMU) and wardrobe | Who is needed where, and when? |
| Script supervisor | Continuity | Continuity, timings, take notes, the editor's log | Will these shots cut together? |
| Production designer | Art | The look of every space and object | What is the world made of? |
| Art director | Art | Executes the production designer's plan, manages builds and budget | Can it be built by Tuesday? |
| Set dresser | Art | Places furniture and objects, resets after each take | Is the room as it was? |
| Props master | Art | Hand props and their duplicates | Is there a spare? |
| Stylist (fashion) or costume designer (narrative) | Wardrobe | Clothes, fittings, continuity of wear | Who is this person by what they wear? |
| Hair and make-up (HMU) | HMU | Hair, make-up, skin finish, touch-ups between takes | Does the face hold under this light? |
| Casting director | Casting | Finding, auditioning and booking talent, usage terms | Who can carry this? |
| Location manager | Locations | Finding, contracting and permitting locations, on-day liaison with owners | Are we allowed to be here, and when must we leave? |
| Drone operator | Aerial | Pilot (and camera operator on dual-operator rigs), airspace permission | Is the flight legal and safe? |
| Stills photographer | Stills | Unit stills, behind the scenes, or campaign stills on a hybrid set | Do we have the still for the press and the feed? |
| Video assist and playback | Camera | The monitor feed to video village, recording takes for playback | Can the client see it and replay it? |

Mumbai terms a Dubai crew will not know: "spot boys" (crew who provide tea, water, chairs and umbrellas), "unit" (the whole crew), "shift" (the 12 hour working day, with overtime by the hour), "lightmen" (electricians supplied with a lighting vendor's package, whose wages, conveyance and food are separate quote lines; see 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 5.8), and FWICE (Federation of Western India Cine Employees, the umbrella of craft unions whose members staff most Mumbai sets).

### 1.2 How the set talks

- One voice to talent. The director speaks to cast; the agency and client speak to the director, through the creative director at video village (the monitor position where agency and client watch). Two voices give an actor two films to play. See 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md for the creative director's station.
- The order of a setup: block (the director walks the action with cast and DP), light (cast leave, stand-ins, people of similar height and colouring, hold the marks while the DP and gaffer light), rehearse (cast return, camera rehearses the move, the 1st AC takes marks), shoot. Blocking before lighting is the rule because light placed before the action is known gets moved twice.
- The calls, run by the 1st AD: "Roll sound", "Speed" (sound is recording), "Roll camera", "Rolling", "Mark it" (the 2nd AC claps the slate), then the director's "Action" and "Cut". The director owns "Action" and "Cut" and nobody else says them.
- The last shot before a company move (the whole unit relocating) or the second-last of the day is the "Abby Singer" (after the Hollywood assistant director Abby Singer, who called it); the last is the "martini shot". Both tell the crew how to pace the wrap.

## 2. Crew sizes, tiers and day rates

### 2.1 The four tiers

| Tier | Crew | Kit | Right for | Wrong for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One person | Director who shoots, edits and records sound | Sony FX3 or iPhone 17 Pro or 18 Pro, a DJI Osmo Pocket 3 as pocket crash and behind-the-scenes camera, a gimbal (a motorised stabiliser), two wireless lavalier mics, one or two LED lights, ND (neutral density) filters | Hotel Reels, event recaps, founder content, social-native series where speed beats polish | Anything with a face lit for a campaign, any product hero shot, any client with an approval chain on set |
| Three to five | Director, DP who operates, 1st AC who doubles as DIT, gaffer who doubles as grip, sound recordist; HMU when talent is on camera | FX6, Canon C400, or a rented Alexa Mini LF or Alexa 35; a prime set; a small lighting package; a slider or gimbal | Interviews, a short brand film with one or two people, a restaurant film, a real estate walkthrough | Movement-heavy fashion films, multiple locations in one day, any crowd |
| Ten to fifteen | Director, DP, operator or 1st AC, 2nd AC, DIT, gaffer, two electricians, key grip, sound mixer and boom, producer, production manager, 1st AD, stylist, HMU, art director, stills | Cinema camera, a matched prime set, a dolly or slider, a full lighting truck, a generator | The standard Mask360 hotel or jewellery film; a two-day fashion film with three looks | A television commercial with builds, stunts or crowd |
| Thirty plus | All of the above with full art department (production designer, art director, set dressers, props, builders), costume team, casting, location managers, a second unit, catering, security, unit drivers | Two cinema bodies, crane or Technocrane (a telescoping crane), motion control (a robotic arm that repeats a move exactly), vehicle rigs, drone unit | A TVC (television commercial) for a national launch, a palace film with cast and horses, a festival campaign with sets | Anything the idea does not need: a large crew in a small hotel suite slows every setup and damages the room |

The house rule: crew to the idea. A crew of 12 in a 40 square metre suite gets less done than a crew of 6, because every setup waits on bodies moving. Size by the hardest shot of the day, then cut one role.

### 2.3 Hours and turnaround

House rule (from 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md): no shoot day over 12 hours and at least 11 hours between wrap and the next call. The reason is image quality as much as welfare: fatigue shows first in the precision jobs (focus, continuity, the dolly mark) late in a long day, which is when the take that matters is often shot.

### 2.4 Booking, holds and cancellation

Crew and kit move through four states: pencil (an informal interest), first hold, second hold and confirmed. When another production challenges a hold (asks for the same dates), the first holder gets a short window, commonly 24 hours, to confirm or release, so an unconfirmed first hold on a sought-after DP can be lost in a day. Cancellation terms go in the deal memo; a common convention is 50 percent of the fee inside 48 hours and 100 percent inside 24 hours, with weather days paid at an agreed fraction (house planning; confirm per vendor). For monsoon and dust-storm exteriors, set a go or no-go weather call (for example 18:00 the day before) so a cancellation lands inside the cheaper window. Mumbai crew are booked through the production house and its FWICE-affiliated vendors. Dubai freelancers must hold a valid freelance permit or be engaged through the licensed production service company that holds the filming permit (verify). Contract terms are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 7.5.

## 3. Pre-production documents

Every document below exists to move a decision earlier, when it is cheap. A lens chosen at the treatment costs nothing; the same lens chosen on set costs the hour the camera spends being rebuilt. The campaign side of these documents (brief, idea sentence, PPM book: the document for the pre-production meeting, where client, agency and production sign off every decision) is in 19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md, section 14.7; this section is the film-craft side.

### 3.1 The treatment

A treatment is the director's written and visual proposal for the film: what it will feel like, how it will be shot and what happens in it. It is how a director wins the job and how the client approves the film before a frame exists. Length for a 60 to 120 second brand film: 10 to 20 pages, image-led, about 70 percent pictures to 30 percent words (the house ratio in design-taste, section 7).

Structure:

1. The idea in one sentence, as a feeling. "A palace at the hour when the staff are the only guests."
2. Tone: three words and what each means on screen. "Hushed: no dialogue, ambient sound, a cello entering at 40 seconds."
3. Visual approach: light (source, time of day, quality), lens (focal lengths and format), camera (movement or stillness, and why), colour (palette from the material world of the place, see 04-colour.md), texture (grain, diffusion).
4. References: three to five frames or films, each captioned with the exact thing borrowed ("the 85 mm compression and the empty corridors of this Aman film; nothing else"). Use ShotDeck (a searchable library of film stills tagged by lens, light and colour) or film grabs; never use the client's competitor's film as a reference.
5. Scene outline: the beats in order with durations that add up to the target length.
6. Casting, wardrobe and styling direction.
7. Music and sound direction.
8. Deliverables: master length, cutdowns, aspect ratios, stills.
9. What we are not doing: the category clichés refused by name (design-taste, section 4, step 3; and references/worlds.md).

Example scene outline for a 90 second palace hotel film:

| Beat | Duration | What we see | What we hear |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Before light | 0 to 12 s | Blue hour (the twilight when the sky's brightness matches lit interiors) exterior, the palace in silhouette; one window lit | Peacocks, distant temple bell |
| 2. The staff wake the house | 12 to 35 s | Hands opening shutters, brass being polished, a gardener's rake, all on 85 mm, static | Shutters, cloth on brass, footsteps on marble |
| 3. Light enters | 35 to 60 s | Sun across a courtyard, dust in a shaft through a jharokha (an enclosed overhanging balcony window of Rajasthani architecture), a still life of breakfast | Cello enters, room tone under it |
| 4. The guest | 60 to 80 s | One guest, seen from behind, walks through the space the staff prepared | Cello resolves |
| 5. Ending | 80 to 90 s | The courtyard empty again; logo on black | Silence, then one bell |

### 3.2 Script formats

Advertising and brand films use the AV script (audio-visual), a two-column table with picture on the left and sound on the right, timed against the clock; narrative and dialogue-heavy films use screenplay format.

AV script example:

| Time | Video | Audio |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00 to 00:04 | ECU: a hand turns a brass key in a carved door. 100 mm macro, static. | SFX: key, latch. Room tone. |
| 00:04 to 00:09 | WS: the door opens onto a courtyard in morning sun. 35 mm, slow push in. | SFX: birds. Music enters, solo piano. |
| 00:09 to 00:14 | MCU: a woman in white looks up from a book. 85 mm, static. | VO (female, Hindi, subtitled): "Some places keep time differently." |

Rules: write only what the camera can see and the microphone can hear; give each line a time; mark shot sizes and lenses where they carry meaning. Voice-over pace: about 2 to 2.5 words a second for unhurried luxury VO, so a 30 second spot carries 60 to 75 words at most; the house aims well under, because silence is the expensive sound.

Screenplay format: Courier 12 point, scene headings (sluglines) in capitals ("INT. PALACE COURTYARD - DAY"), action in present tense, character names in capitals above dialogue; one page is about one minute of screen time, which is why schedules count length in eighths of a page. Software: Final Draft, WriterDuet, Celtx, and Fade In.

### 3.3 Shot list

The shot list is the DP's and 1st AD's working document: every setup, in shooting order or scene order, with the technical decision already made.

| Scene | Shot | Size | Movement | Lens | Duration | Frame rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2A | ECU hands on brass | Static | 100 mm macro | 4 s | 50 fps | Backlight from window; polish cloth in white |
| 2 | 2B | MS gardener in courtyard | Static, wide of the action | 85 mm | 6 s | 25 fps | Shoot through foreground jasmine |
| 2 | 2C | WS courtyard from upper gallery | Slow tilt down, 4 s | 35 mm | 8 s | 25 fps | Sun must be off the floor; before 08:30 |
| 3 | 3A | Insert: dust in light shaft | Static | 50 mm | 6 s | 100 fps | Haze, sun through jharokha at 09:10 |

Columns that matter and why: size and lens together define the image; movement with its speed prevents the "gimbal float" being invented on the day; duration tells the editor what was intended and the 1st AD how long to roll; frame rate (frames per second, fps) decides whether lights need a flicker test (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 5.7); notes carry the light and the time window. StudioBinder, Shot Designer (Hollywood Camera Work, which also draws overhead blocking diagrams) and a spreadsheet all work; the house keeps a Google Sheet shared with the DP and 1st AD.

### 3.4 Storyboard

A storyboard is the film drawn as frames, one or more per shot, numbered to match the shot list (2A, 2B), with movement shown by drawn arrows inside the frame and notes underneath. Draw inside the delivery aspect ratio (a 2.39 board for a 2.39 film) with the 9:16 extraction marked if verticals are owed. Variants: the photoboard (stills from the recce or stand-ins), the animatic (boards cut to time with temp sound, the way an edit will feel), and AI-generated previs (previsualisation: a rough moving version of the film made before the shoot; 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 6.1), which is fast and dangerous because it shows light and lenses the location may not have. Tools: Storyboarder (free, Wonder Unit), Boords, Procreate, and Cine Tracer or Previs Pro for 3D previs with real lens data.

### 3.5 Shooting schedule and stripboard

The stripboard is the schedule laid out as one strip per scene, colour-coded by the convention: white for interior day, yellow for exterior day, blue for interior night, green for exterior night. Strips are moved until the day order works; Movie Magic Scheduling, StudioBinder and Yamdu do this digitally. Scene length is counted in eighths of a page.

Order of priority when scheduling, with the reason:

1. Location, because a company move (the whole unit relocating) costs at least an hour and, in Mumbai traffic, often two (house planning).
2. Light, because the sun will not wait: shoot east-facing rooms in the morning, west-facing terraces at golden hour (the hour after sunrise and before sunset, when the sun is low and warm), and put the blue hour exterior where nothing else can be scheduled. Plan the sun with Sun Seeker, Sun Surveyor or PhotoPills on the recce.
3. Talent availability and limits, especially children (section 4.1) and celebrities on hourly calls.
4. Complexity: the hardest setup goes early, when the crew is fresh and there is time to fail once.
5. Wardrobe and make-up changes grouped so HMU resets happen once.

#### 3.5.1 How much a day holds

House planning bands for a 12 hour shift, recalibrated after every job against the camera reports:

| Shoot type | Per 12 hour day | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lit performance or dialogue, crew of 10 to 15 | 8 to 12 setups | Each setup takes about 45 to 90 minutes from blocking to the last take |
| Available-light B-roll (supporting footage, section 12.2), crew of 3 to 5 | 20 to 30 setups | No lighting reset; the limit is moving and composing |
| Tabletop with motion control or high-speed pours | 6 to 10 finished shots | Each pour is a reset of 10 to 20 minutes (clean, refill, dry) |
| Fashion film | 2 to 3 looks | HMU and wardrobe changes take 45 to 90 minutes per look |
| Blue hour | 1 setup, or 2 if the second is pre-lit | The window is about 20 to 25 minutes near the tropics (section 14.5) |

The first-shot target: the 1st AD aims to roll within about 60 to 90 minutes of crew call, because a late first shot is where most overtime starts. The rule: divide the shot list by these rates before the estimate names a number of days. A board of 40 lit setups is 3.3 to 5 days at 8 to 12 a day, so it is quoted as four; quoting it as two moves the third and fourth days into contingency.

### 3.6 Call sheet anatomy

The call sheet is the daily schedule sent to every cast and crew member the evening before, usually by the 2nd AD and approved by the 1st AD and producer. Its anatomy, top to bottom: a header (production title, client, director, producer, date, "Day 2 of 3"); the general crew call and shooting call (first shot) with meal times; weather with sunrise, sunset, golden and blue hour times and the rain (monsoon, June to September in Mumbai) or heat forecast; the location (address, map pin, parking, unit base (where the trucks, catering and crew base sit), holding area (where cast wait), nearest hospital and police station with phone numbers); the schedule of setups in order with scene, description, cast and planned time; cast with pick-up, HMU, wardrobe and on-set times; the crew list by department with calls and phones; special instructions (drone windows, haze, children, animals, vehicles, permits in force, dress code for religious or heritage sites); the 1st AD's safety notes and emergency contact; tomorrow's schedule in one paragraph; and the idea sentence printed at the top (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md), so every crew member reads why the film exists.

### 3.7 Location recce report

A recce (reconnaissance visit) with the director, DP, gaffer, production designer and location manager produces one report per location:

- Address, GPS pin, owner and on-day contact, access hours, load-in route and lift sizes.
- Floor plan with dimensions and ceiling heights; 360 degree photos and a walk-through video.
- Sun path for the shoot date: where the sun enters each room at each hour, from the sun app on site.
- Power: socket types (India 16 A and 6 A; UAE BS 1363), circuits available, three-phase, generator parking.
- Sound: traffic, air conditioning that cannot be switched off, prayer calls (five times daily across the Gulf and in many Indian neighbourhoods), aircraft paths, construction.
- Restrictions: no rigging on heritage walls, no haze (fire alarms), no tape on marble, guest areas off-limits during service, staff to be present.
- Permissions needed (section 4), holding areas, toilets, catering space, Wi-Fi, mobile signal (and uplink speed if the client will review remotely, section 13.7).
- Hotel operations (section 14.5): rooms taken out of inventory and their cost, service windows for restaurants, lobbies and pools, and the approval chain for the cut.
- Photos at the lenses planned, shot with Artemis Pro (a director's viewfinder app that simulates a chosen camera and lens).

### 3.8 Casting brief

Character in a paragraph (who they are: behaviour, history, what they want), age range, look, skills (horse riding, Kathak (the north Indian classical dance), Arabic dialect, Hindi diction), wardrobe sizes, availability dates, fittings, usage (media, territory, term, as in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7), exclusivity (no competing jewellery brand for 12 months, for example), nudity or intimacy terms if any, and the AI clause: no synthetic reuse of likeness without separate consent (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 7.4). For the Gulf, state modesty expectations and wardrobe constraints in the brief so talent can decline before the callback. Emirati and Saudi national dress (for men the kandura, a full-length robe, with the ghutra headcloth and the black agal cord; for women the abaya, a full-length robe, and the sheila headscarf) is worn correctly and cast with care; casting non-nationals in national dress in a role that implies national identity needs client and permit-authority review (verify per emirate). Physical contact between unrelated male and female talent is kept minimal and agreed in the brief. In Saudi Arabia female talent may bring a chaperone, who is a budget line (section 5.1). The treatment's casting and wardrobe pages go to the production service company before the permit is filed (section 4.4).

### 3.9 Wardrobe and props lists

Wardrobe list: character, scene, look number, item, brand, size, source (pull, purchase, rental), cost, return date, continuity photo. Props list: scene, item, hero or background, quantity, source, duplicates. Duplicates rule: three of anything that is consumed, broken, poured or stained on camera (a glass of wine, a cake, a silk scarf for a wind shot), because the third take is usually the one.

High-value product (jewellery, watches, unreleased pieces): a custody log records each piece with serial number, value and photograph, signed out to set and back by the brand's representative at every setup, and licensed security stays with the pieces. Production insurance often excludes client goods above a stated value, so confirm the brand's own all-risk or transit cover in writing (section 4.2). Crew sign an NDA (non-disclosure agreement) for unreleased pieces, phones stay away from the product table, and behind-the-scenes content is held until the launch date. Blocking and lighting use duplicate or display samples; the hero piece arrives only for takes, which keeps its handling time and its risk to a few minutes a setup.

## 4. Releases, insurance, permits and drones

Legal paperwork protects the film's usage, which is where its value sits. A hotel film with one unsigned staff member in frame cannot run as paid media.

### 4.1 Releases

- Talent release: grants the right to record and use the person's image, voice and performance; states media, territory, term and fee (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7); waives approval of the final cut; includes a consent clause for personal data; and states that the likeness will not be used to train or drive a synthetic model without a separate written consent and fee (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 7.4).
- Location release: the owner grants access for stated dates and hours, the right to depict the property (and to name it, or to keep it unnamed), restoration obligations and the fee; signed by someone with authority (the general manager of a hotel, a trust secretary at a palace museum).
- Minors: signed by a parent or legal guardian. India: the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986, as amended in 2016, permits a child to work as an artist in audio-visual media; the Amendment Rules of 2017 require that a child work no more than five hours a day and no more than three hours without rest, that the production obtain permission from the District Magistrate, and that at least 20 percent of the child's income be deposited in a fixed deposit in the child's name (verify the current rule text before the shoot). UAE: Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021 sets 15 as the minimum age for employment, so child talent is engaged through guardians and the production service company's permits (verify per emirate).
- Background and crowd: signage at the edges of public locations ("filming in progress; by entering you consent"), plus individual releases for anyone featured recognisably. In hotels, guests are kept out of frame or released individually; never rely on signage for a guest.
- Clearances in frame: the art department keeps a clearance log of every painting, sculpture, photograph, branded product, car badge and screen in frame. Uncleared brands are covered or replaced (greeking); palace collections need the trust's written consent per artwork; iconic private buildings used as hero imagery need the owner's permission in commercial work (Emaar for the Burj Khalifa, verify current terms, and the Nakheel and Meraas developments named in section 4.4). Music played on set needs its licence agreed in advance (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 12.2).
- Data protection: an identifiable face is personal data. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, with its Rules notified in November 2025 and phased in (verify the phase dates), and the UAE's Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (the PDPL) both require a lawful basis and a stated purpose; the release is that basis, so write the purpose broadly enough to cover cutdowns and future edits within the term.

### 4.2 Insurance

Cover types, owners and the house position are in 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 7.4. The film-specific points: rental houses ask for an insurance certificate naming them before the kit leaves the shelf; equipment cover must include the lens package (a single cinema lens can cost Rs 10 lakh); public liability covers the marble floor scratched by a dolly wheel in a palace corridor; weather cover is priced for exteriors in monsoon months; and drone flights need third-party cover in their own right (section 4.5).

### 4.3 Permits in India (verify each before quoting)

- National: foreign productions apply through the Film Facilitation Office of the National Film Development Corporation under the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, whose India Cine Hub portal also lists state incentives. The incentive for foreign productions was raised in November 2024 to up to 40 percent of qualifying spend with a cap of Rs 30 crore (reported; verify). Domestic brand shoots deal with states, cities and owners directly.
- Maharashtra and Mumbai: the Maharashtra Film, Stage and Cultural Development Corporation (which runs Film City, the Dadasaheb Phalke Chitranagari in Goregaon) operates an online single window for shooting permissions in Mumbai, routing municipal (Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation), police and traffic permissions (verify the current portal). Separate owners grant their own spaces: railways (Western and Central Railway, including Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus), the Mumbai Port Authority, housing society committees (a no-objection certificate, NOC, from the society for any residential building), and the forest department for Sanjay Gandhi National Park.
- Heritage sites: centrally protected monuments (the Taj Mahal, Qutub Minar, Humayun's Tomb, Elephanta Caves, Ajanta and Ellora) need written permission from the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), with a per-day fee, a security deposit, fixed hours, and conditions that forbid rigging on the fabric, haze and unapproved lighting; drones are a separate permission and several monuments sit inside no-drone areas. State-protected monuments go through the state department of archaeology: Amer Fort in Jaipur through Rajasthan's Department of Archaeology and Museums and the Amber Development and Management Authority.
- Rajasthan palaces: most are private or trust property, so permission is a commercial contract with the owner: the City Palace, Jaipur (Sawai Man Singh II Museum Trust); the City Palace, Udaipur (the Maharana of Mewar Charitable Foundation for the museum, the HRH Group of Hotels for its hotels); Mehrangarh, Jodhpur (Mehrangarh Museum Trust); Umaid Bhawan, Jodhpur, and Rambagh, Jaipur (palace hotels operated by Taj, part of IHCL, the Indian Hotels Company Limited). Expect location fees by the day, guest-hour restrictions, no fixing to walls (plan stands, weights and floor protection), and approval of the cut where the property is named. Rajasthan's film tourism policy (2022) offers incentives for productions shot in the state (verify the current terms).

### 4.4 Permits in the UAE and Saudi Arabia (verify each before quoting)

- Dubai: the Dubai Film and TV Commission (DFTC) issues filming permits, applied for through a Dubai-licensed production company; allow several working days for exteriors and a location fee for public spaces (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 5.8). Much of Dubai's photogenic land is privately master-planned and needs the developer's permission on top of the DFTC permit: Emaar (Downtown Dubai, Dubai Creek Harbour), Nakheel (Palm Jumeirah), Meraas (City Walk, Bluewaters, La Mer) and the DIFC Authority. Government buildings, police, military sites, palaces and the ruling families' images need specific permission or are off-limits. Content sensitivity: modesty in wardrobe, alcohol on screen, religious sites and dress at them, and the national flag are reviewed; during Ramadan, shoot hours, catering and public conduct change: crews avoid eating and drinking in public view in daylight out of respect, and set catering indoors (Dubai relaxed the rules for restaurants in 2021; verify the position for the shoot date). Influencer and paid-content rules are under the UAE Media Council (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 7.6, and 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md for the Advertiser Permit of July 2025).
- UAE media content: the UAE Media Council enforces content standards under Federal Decree-Law No. 55 of 2023 on media regulation, with penalties of up to about AED 1 million, in force from 2025, for breaches such as disrespecting national symbols, religion or the state (verify the current schedule). The permit application is where these are tested, which is why casting and wardrobe pages go to the production service company first (section 3.8). Pork and alcohol props are cleared with the permit, and prayer-time breaks go on the call sheet.
- Abu Dhabi: the Abu Dhabi Film Commission, part of the Creative Media Authority, issues permits and runs a cash rebate on qualifying production spend, advertised at 35 percent base since its 2023 revision (verify the current rate, cap and minimum spend).
- Saudi Arabia: the Saudi Film Commission issues filming permits and advertises a cash rebate of up to 40 percent of qualifying spend (verify); AlUla runs its own film office (Film AlUla) with location support. Drones fall under the General Authority of Civil Aviation (GACA).

### 4.5 Drones in India (Drone Rules 2021 and amendments; verify on Digital Sky)

The Drone Rules, 2021 (notified 25 August 2021) govern civil drones, administered by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) through the Digital Sky platform.

| Category (all-up weight) | Range | Pilot requirement for a commercial shoot |
|---|---|---|
| Nano | Up to 250 g | No remote pilot certificate required |
| Micro | 250 g to 2 kg | Remote pilot certificate required for commercial use (exempt only for non-commercial use) |
| Small | 2 kg to 25 kg | Remote pilot certificate required |
| Medium | 25 kg to 150 kg | Remote pilot certificate required |
| Large | Above 150 kg | Remote pilot certificate required |

- Airspace map on Digital Sky: green zones (no permission needed) up to 400 ft (120 m) above ground, and up to 200 ft (60 m) in the band 8 to 12 km from an operational airport's perimeter; yellow zones (controlled airspace, permission from the air traffic control authority) include the airspace 5 to 8 km from an airport perimeter and anything above the green ceilings; red zones need central government permission. The 2021 rules cut the yellow zone around airports from 45 km to 12 km. Check the map on the day: temporary red zones appear for VIP movements.
- Registration and pilots: each drone carries a Unique Identification Number (UIN) from Digital Sky; the Drone (Amendment) Rules, 2022 replaced the pilot licence with a remote pilot certificate (RPC) issued through DGCA-authorised training organisations. Ask the operator for the UIN, the RPC and the airspace permission screenshot before booking.
- Import: complete foreign-made drones have been banned from import since 9 February 2022 (DGFT notification), with exemptions for research, defence and security; so for any foreign-made drone, confirm it was imported before the ban or under an exemption and carries a UIN before it is booked.
- Local orders: city police issue their own prohibitions (Mumbai Police have repeatedly issued 30-day orders banning drones and other flying objects over the city); heritage monuments and military areas are no-fly. Penalties under the rules run to Rs 1 lakh.
- Insurance: the rules apply the third-party insurance provisions of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 to drones (verify).
- Status: the Ministry of Civil Aviation circulated a draft Civil Drone (Promotion and Regulation) Bill, 2025 for consultation to replace the Drone Rules, 2021; check whether it has been enacted and whether the Digital Sky categories and penalties have changed before quoting (to verify, October 2026).

### 4.6 Drones in the UAE (verify with GCAA and DCAA)

Federal airspace is regulated by the General Civil Aviation Authority (GCAA); Dubai has its own Dubai Civil Aviation Authority (DCAA). Commercial filming needs a registered, authorised operator, a registered aircraft and a flight permit for the specific location and time, coordinated in Dubai through the DFTC permit and the DCAA; in Abu Dhabi through the Abu Dhabi Film Commission. Recreational flying was suspended nationwide in January 2022 and has since been reopened in stages for registered users (verify the current position). No-fly areas include airports (DXB and DWC in Dubai, AUH in Abu Dhabi), palaces, government and military sites. Book a licensed operator, because the permit belongs to the operator and travels with them. The commercial permit route has been revised in stages, so re-read the GCAA and DCAA pages before each quote (to verify, October 2026).

### 4.7 Safety and risk assessment

- Midday heat bans: the UAE Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation (MoHRE) bans work under direct sun from 12:30 to 15:00 between 15 June and 15 September, with fines of about AED 5,000 per worker up to AED 50,000 where several workers are involved; Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (HRSD) bans outdoor work in direct sun from 12:00 to 15:00 over the same months (annual ministerial decisions; verify the 2026 announcement and its exemptions). Schedule interiors or a shaded unit base across those hours, because rigging a terrace at 13:00 in July is a legal breach as well as a heat-stroke risk.
- Written risk assessment: the 1st AD issues one at the PPM and briefs it at call. It covers heat (shade, water and electrolytes, a rest rotation, a named heat-illness responder); work at height (cranes, scaffolds, rooftops); water (a lifeguard for pool shoots, an RCD on every circuit, the residual current device that cuts power when current leaks to earth, and lamps kept back from the edge); monsoon electrics (RCDs, cable ramps, generators on dry ground); vehicles and road work (section 14.11); drones (sections 4.5 and 4.6); animals (section 13.4); haze and fire alarms in heritage buildings; and crowds.
- Medical cover: a set medic for crews of 30 or more and for any stunt, water or height work, with the nearest hospital already on the call sheet (section 3.6).

## 6. Camera fundamentals

### 6.1 Sensor size and crop factor

Sensor size sets field of view for a given focal length, depth of field at a given framing and stop, and the noise floor. Crop factor is the ratio of the full-frame diagonal (43.3 mm) to the sensor's diagonal; multiply a lens's focal length by it to find the full-frame lens that frames the same. For a 16:9 recording, compare widths instead, because the recorded area is cropped from the sensor.

| Format | Typical active area (mm) | Diagonal (mm) | Crop factor vs full frame | Example cameras |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Super 16 | 12.52 by 7.41 | 14.5 | about 3 | Film; Blackmagic in S16 window modes |
| Micro Four Thirds | 17.3 by 13.0 | 21.6 | 2.0 | Panasonic Lumix GH7 |
| APS-C (Sony) | 23.5 by 15.6 | 28.2 | 1.5 | Sony FX30, Fujifilm X-H2S |
| Super 35, 16:9 recording | about 24.9 wide | n/a | about 1.45 by width | Alexa 35 in 4K 16:9 (4,096 photosites, the sensor's light-sensitive cells, at 6.075 micrometres is 24.9 mm), Sony FX9 in S35 mode |
| Super 35, 6K 17:9 | about 27.03 by 14.26 | 30.6 | about 1.33 by width | RED Komodo-X (RED's published sensor size; verify) |
| Super 35, open gate | 27.99 by 19.22 (Alexa 35, 4.6K 3:2) | 34.0 | 1.27 by diagonal | ARRI Alexa 35 and 35 Xtreme |
| Full frame | 36 by 24 | 43.3 | 1.0 | Sony Venice 2, FX6, FX3, Blackmagic Pyxis, Canon C400 |
| ARRI large format | 36.70 by 25.54 | 44.7 | 0.97 | Alexa Mini LF |
| 65 mm class | 50.8 to 54.1 by about 24 to 26 | 56 to 60 | about 0.72 to 0.77 | Alexa 65 and 265, Blackmagic URSA Cine 17K 65 (verified: 65 mm, 51 by 24 mm) |

Equivalence in practice: a 35 mm lens on Super 35 in 16:9 frames like a 50 mm on full frame. At the same framing and the same stop, the larger format gives about one stop shallower depth of field per 1.4x of crop factor. The "large-format look" that DPs describe (a sense of space around a face at a close framing) is that combination: a longer lens for the same framing, from the same distance, with shallower focus. Whether a format has a look of its own is contested. ARRI markets the Alexa Mini LF on its "distinct and cinematic large-format aesthetic" (arri.com, October 2026), and many DPs describe the effect. Steve Yedlin ASC argues in "Common Misconceptions About Large Format Optics" (yedlin.net, 2019) that matched angle of view, camera position and blur circle reproduce the result on any format, and shows it in side-by-side tests. The house follows the equivalence position, because it turns the look into decisions the DP can plan: lens, distance and stop. Large format is booked when the shot needs shallow focus at a wide framing or a lens set designed for it, and when the focus budget allows; at T1.5 on full frame an 85 mm close-up holds a few centimetres (section 8.10).

### 6.2 Resolution and image quality

Resolution is the number of photosites; image quality is dynamic range, colour, noise, highlight roll-off and motion rendering. The ARRI Alexa 35 records 4.6K and is the benchmark for skin and highlights; a 12K sensor earns its data cost in reframing and VFX. Reasons to want more photosites: oversampling (a 6K sensor downscaled to 4K is cleaner and sharper than native 4K), reframing (punch-ins and 9:16 extraction from a 16:9 master, section 10.7), stabilisation in post, and VFX tracking. Reasons against: data volume, rolling shutter time on larger sensors, and lenses that resolve less than the sensor. Deliverables for Mask360 are 4K UHD (3840 by 2160) at most for screens and HD to 2K for social; see 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 5.1.

### 6.3 Dynamic range

Dynamic range is the span between the darkest tone a sensor records with acceptable noise and the brightest before it clips, measured in stops (each stop a doubling of light). Current cinema sensors claim 13 to 17 stops: Blackmagic Pyxis 6K 13 stops, Pyxis 12K and URSA Cine 16 stops, ARRI Alexa 35 17 stops (all verified at the makers' pages, October 2026). Makers measure differently (the noise threshold chosen at the bottom end varies), so compare cameras by testing the same scene, and read claims as ranking within one brand. What dynamic range buys: a window and a face in the same frame without lights, a white kandura in sun without clipping, a candlelit dinner with detail in the shadows. A Rec.709 display (the HD and SDR standard, section 6.4) shows far fewer stops than the sensor holds, which is why the camera records log (6.4) and the grade decides what to keep.

### 6.4 Log profiles, Rec.709 and the LUT workflow

A log profile encodes scene light on a logarithmic curve so that the sensor's full range fits in a 10 or 12 bit file; the image looks flat and grey until a transform is applied (04-colour.md and 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 7, cover the colour science). Each maker has a curve and a gamut (the range of colours the encoding can describe):

| Log curve | Gamut | Cameras | 18 percent grey on the waveform, about | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ARRI LogC4 | ARRI Wide Gamut 4 (AWG4) | Alexa 35, 35 Xtreme, Alexa 265 | 28 percent (computed from ARRI's published LogC4 formula); LogC3 at exposure index (EI) 800 about 39 | Introduced with the Alexa 35 (2022) to hold its 17 stops; LogC3 remains on older Alexas |
| Sony S-Log3 | S-Gamut3.Cine or S-Gamut3 | Venice 2, Burano, FX6, FX3, FX2, FX30 | 41 percent; 90 percent white about 61 | Sony's published curve |
| Canon Log 2 and Canon Log 3 (C-Log2, C-Log3) | Cinema Gamut | C400, C80, C70, C500 Mark II | Unknown here: read Canon's white paper | C-Log2 keeps more shadow range; C-Log3 is easier to expose and grade |
| Panasonic V-Log | V-Gamut | Lumix GH7, S1 II, S1H, S5 II | 42 percent; 90 percent white about 61 | Panasonic's published curve |
| Blackmagic Film Generation 5 | Blackmagic Wide Gamut | Pyxis, URSA Cine, Cinema Camera 6K | Unknown here: read Blackmagic's documentation | Generation 5 colour science, with a 12 bit gamma curve on Pyxis and URSA Cine (verified) |
| Apple Log and Apple Log 2 | Apple Log 2 uses a wider gamut | Apple Log on iPhone 15 Pro and later; Apple Log 2 on iPhone 17 Pro and 18 Pro | Unknown here: read Apple's Apple Log profile document | Apple Log 2 listed on the iPhone 18 Pro spec page (verified); Final Cut Pro 11.2 (19 September 2025) added ProRes RAW controls for iPhone footage and the Apple Log 2 LUT (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 6.3) |
| RED Log3G10 | REDWideGamutRGB | V-Raptor [X], Komodo-X | 33 percent | Designed so 18 percent grey maps to one third of the code range, with 10 stops above grey |

These are the references for exposing log without a LUT. On mirrorless log, the ETTR (expose to the right) offset of section 9.7 shifts them up by the stops added: an FX3 rated one stop over in S-Log3 puts grey at about 48 percent (from Sony's S-Log3 formula).

The LUT workflow on a Mask360 set:

1. The camera records log (or RAW, which is debayered, converted from single-colour photosites to full RGB pixels, to log in post).
2. The DIT or DP loads a viewing LUT (a lookup table converting log to Rec.709 for display) on the monitors, sometimes a show LUT that also carries the film's look, built in pre-production from the camera test.
3. Exposure is judged on the log signal or on false colour (section 9.5), because a contrasty viewing LUT hides highlight headroom.
4. Dailies and editorial proxies carry the LUT baked in, so the client reviews the intended look.
5. The colourist grades from the log originals through a colour space transform or ACES (the Academy Color Encoding System, the Academy's colour management framework; 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, sections 7.2 and 7.3), with the show LUT as the starting reference.

Rec.709 (ITU-R BT.709) is the standard for HD and SDR (standard dynamic range) delivery: its gamut and its transfer, displayed at gamma 2.4 in a grading suite. Shooting directly in a Rec.709 profile bakes in contrast and clips highlights; it is right only for live events with no grade, and for one-person social content where speed matters more than latitude.

### 6.5 RAW, ProRes and H.265

RAW records the sensor data before debayering, so white balance and ISO remain adjustable metadata in post. Intra-frame codecs (ProRes) compress each frame on its own; long-GOP codecs (H.264, H.265) store a full frame and then only differences, which saves space and costs editing performance and robustness.

| Format | Type | Bit depth | Strength | Cost | House use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ARRIRAW | Uncompressed RAW | 13 bit LogC4 on Alexa 35 (confirm in ARRI's LogC4 specification) | The reference for skin and highlights | Very large files, Codex media | Hero films on Alexa |
| ARRICORE | ARRI's RGB codec, introduced with the Alexa 35 Xtreme | n/a | ARRI's processed image at smaller sizes than ARRIRAW (verified as a new codec; ARRI page) | New; check post support | Alexa 35 Xtreme jobs where data is tight |
| REDCODE RAW (R3D) | Compressed RAW | 16 bit | Flexible compression ratios, mature workflow | RED ecosystem | RED shoots |
| Blackmagic RAW (BRAW) | Partially debayered RAW | 12 bit | Small files, fast in DaVinci Resolve, constant bitrate or constant quality modes (Q0 to Q5, verified on URSA Cine) | Strongest inside Resolve | Pyxis and URSA Cine shoots |
| Apple ProRes RAW | Compressed RAW | 12 bit | Runs well on Mac, recorded by Atomos recorders, Panasonic GH7 and iPhone via compatible apps | Grading support differs by application | Mirrorless and phone shoots with external recorders |

The full ProRes data rate table is in 26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 5.2.

Media planning: GB per hour equals the data rate in Mb/s times 0.45 (3,600 seconds, divided by 8 bits a byte and 1,000 MB a GB). ProRes 422 HQ at UHD 25 fps is about 737 Mb/s (Apple's ProRes white paper, as cited in 16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md), so about 330 GB an hour; two bodies rolling 2 to 3 hours each in a 12 hour day write about 1.3 to 2 TB. RAW formats run higher, so read the maker's data calculator for the chosen mode. Budget primary and backup drives at twice the day's volume plus a shuttle drive, checksum with xxHash64 (a fast hash algorithm) by default and MD5 (an older, slower one) when the client asks, and after delivery archive camera originals and the project to LTO tape (Linear Tape-Open, the archive standard) or a second cloud tier for the usage term.

### 6.6 Bit depth and chroma subsampling

Bit depth is the number of tonal steps per colour channel: 8 bit is 256 levels per channel (about 16.7 million colours), 10 bit is 1,024 (about 1.07 billion), 12 bit is 4,096. Log footage in 8 bit bands in skies and on skin when graded, because the curve spreads few levels across a wide range; 10 bit is the minimum for log, 12 bit for heavy grading. Chroma subsampling records colour at lower resolution than brightness: 4:4:4 keeps full colour resolution, 4:2:2 halves it horizontally, 4:2:0 halves it in both directions. Green screen keys and jewellery edges need 4:2:2 at least, because the edge of a sapphire against a key is a colour edge.

### 6.7 ISO, native ISO, dual base ISO and exposure index

ISO on a digital camera is a rating of how the sensor's signal is mapped to brightness. Native (base) ISO is the setting where the sensor delivers its full dynamic range with no extra gain. Dual base ISO sensors have two analogue gain circuits, giving a second clean base for low light; the Canon C400 and C80 have three. Base ISOs per body are in section 7. Exposure index (EI) is a rating that moves where middle grey sits in the recorded range without changing the analogue gain: rating an Alexa 35 at EI 400 instead of 800 places grey higher, which gives cleaner shadows and one stop less highlight headroom; the Alexa 35's range is EI 160 to 6400 (verified). Practical rules: for log on Sony, expose one to two stops over the base (rate an FX3 at about EI 400 to 500 in S-Log3) for clean shadows; on Alexa, expose at the rated EI and pick the EI by the scene's highlights.

### 6.8 Shutter angle and shutter speed

Shutter angle describes exposure time as a fraction of the frame interval, a convention from rotating film shutters: 180 degrees exposes for half of each frame, which at 24 fps is 1/48 second and at 25 fps is 1/50. The 180 degree shutter rule (shutter speed about double the frame rate) gives the motion blur audiences read as "film".

- Flicker: mains in India and the UAE run at 50 Hz, so lights that flicker do so at 100 pulses a second. At 25 fps and 1/50 there is no flicker. At 24 fps, set 172.8 degrees (which is 1/50 second) to stay flicker-free; at 30 or 60 fps under 50 Hz light, flicker risk rises and must be tested.
- Breaking the rule on purpose: a narrow shutter (45 to 90 degrees) makes every frame crisp and motion staccato, the look of the beach landing in Saving Private Ryan (1998) and of fast product moves where each frame must be a sharp still; a wide shutter (270 to 360 degrees) smears motion into a dream and gains up to one stop in low light. Use a narrow shutter for water droplets and pours at normal speed; avoid it on walking people unless the stutter is the idea.
- Rolling shutter: most CMOS sensors read rows top to bottom, so fast pans skew verticals (the "jello" tell) and camera flashes light only part of a frame. Global shutter sensors read all rows at once: RED V-Raptor [X] and Komodo-X are the cinema examples. For a fashion show with camera flashes or a whip pan past palace columns, global shutter is the answer.

### 6.9 Frame rates, slow motion and time lapse

Base rates: 24 (23.976 in practice) for cinematic delivery; 25 for India and the UAE, which are 50 Hz, PAL-heritage broadcast territories (PAL: the 25 fps analogue colour television standard); 30 (29.97) for platforms and markets where 60 Hz is the norm. Shoot the delivery rate unless slow motion is planned; social platforms accept 24, 25 and 30.

| Capture rate | Played at 24 or 25 | Speed | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 48 or 50 fps | 24 or 25 | 50 percent | Barely-there slowness on a walk, hair, fabric; reads as grace, and the audience does not register an effect |
| 60 fps | 24 | 40 percent | Same, slightly more obvious; the common mirrorless setting |
| 100 or 120 fps | 25 or 24 | 25 or 20 percent | Pours, fabric in wind, a turn of the head; the luxury product standard |
| 200 to 240 fps | 24 or 25 | about 10 percent | Liquids, splashes, powder bursts, jewellery drops |
| 330 to 1,000 fps and above | 24 or 25 | 2.5 to 7 percent | High-speed product: the Alexa 35 Xtreme reaches 660 fps in Sensor Overdrive mode with 11 stops of range at EI 1600, and keeps 17 stops up to 330 fps (verified); Phantom cameras (Vision Research) for the 1,000 fps class |

Rules: light for high speed (each doubling of frame rate costs a stop at a 180 degree shutter), test every LED fixture for flicker at the capture rate, and use slow motion where the subject has motion worth stretching. Slow motion on a static subject is a cheap tell.

Time lapse compresses time by capturing frames at an interval. The arithmetic: frames needed equals clip length times playback rate; interval equals event duration divided by frames. A two-hour sunset over the Thar played as 10 seconds at 25 fps needs 250 frames, so one frame every 28.8 seconds. Starting intervals: clouds 2 to 5 seconds, city traffic 1 to 2 seconds, sun and stars 15 to 30 seconds. For natural motion blur, set the shutter to about half the interval using ND (a 2 second interval with a 1 second exposure). A day-to-night transition (the "holy grail" time lapse) needs exposure ramping, either by the camera's interval ramp or by LRTimelapse in post. A hyperlapse moves the camera a fixed distance between frames; stabilise it in post.

### 6.10 Aperture, f-stops and T-stops

The f-number is the focal length divided by the diameter of the entrance pupil; each full stop (1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16) halves the light. It is geometric and ignores the light lost in the glass. The T-stop is the f-number corrected for measured transmission, so two lenses at T2.8 pass the same light; cinema lenses are marked in T-stops so that a lens change mid-scene does not change exposure. Photo lenses are marked in f-stops and typically lose a fraction of a stop to their glass.

### 6.11 ND filters

A neutral density (ND) filter cuts light evenly across the spectrum so a scene can be shot at a wide aperture and a 180 degree shutter in bright light. Density is logarithmic: each 0.3 of density is one stop.

| Density | Stops | Light transmitted |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 1 | 50 percent |
| 0.6 | 2 | 25 percent |
| 0.9 | 3 | 12.5 percent |
| 1.2 | 4 | 6.25 percent |
| 1.5 | 5 | 3.1 percent |
| 1.8 | 6 | 1.6 percent |
| 2.1 | 7 | 0.8 percent |
| 2.4 | 8 | 0.4 percent |
| 2.7 | 9 | 0.2 percent |

- Worked example, Dubai at noon: the sunny 16 rule (in full sun, correct exposure is f/16 at a shutter of one over the ISO) gives f/16 at 1/800 for ISO 800. At 1/50 and T2.8 the scene is four stops (shutter) plus five stops (aperture) too bright: ND 2.7. The Alexa 35 Xtreme's internal filter offers Clear, 0.6, 1.2 and 1.8 (verified), so add a 0.9 in the matte box (the hood in front of the lens that holds filters and flags stray light).
- Internal ND: motorised filter wheels (ARRI, Sony Venice 2 in 0.3 steps to 2.4 (verify), Blackmagic URSA Cine at 2, 4 and 6 stops, verified) and the Sony FX6's electronic variable ND. Fast, matched to the sensor, the first choice.
- IR contamination: heavy ND (from about 1.2) passes infrared that the sensor records, turning black fabrics brown or magenta; a black suit or an abaya at ND 1.8 can go maroon. Use IRND filters (ND that also attenuates infrared) from Tiffen, Schneider or Formatt Hitech.
- Variable ND: two polarisers rotated against each other. Convenient on run-and-gun events; at the strong end it produces a dark X across the frame and colour shifts, so never on the hero camera.
- Fixed ND in the matte box: the cleanest, slower to change, the cinema standard.

### 6.12 White balance and tint

White balance sets which colour temperature the camera renders as neutral, in kelvin; tint is the second axis, green to magenta, which corrects fluorescent and LED spikes. Set white balance deliberately (5600 K for daylight scenes, 3200 K for tungsten interiors, or a custom balance from a grey card at the key light) and keep it constant through a scene, so shots cut together. In RAW it is metadata and can be changed in post without loss; in log or ProRes it is baked into the colour channels and can be corrected only with some cost. Mixed sources and gels are in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, sections 1.2 and 4.5.

## 8. Lenses

The lens is where the look is bought. Matched glass makes two camera bodies cut as one film; different glass on one body reads as two.

### 8.1 Focal length, perspective and emotion

Perspective (how near and far objects relate in size) is set by camera distance; focal length sets how much of the view is framed. A long lens looks "compressed" because it is used from further away. The emotional read of each length follows from the distance it puts between camera and subject.

| Full-frame focal length | Horizontal angle of view (36 mm wide) | Super 35 lens for the same framing | Read | Mask360 use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14 to 18 mm | 104 to 90 degrees | 10 to 12 mm | Space, scale, distortion at edges; the "real estate wide" when used on small rooms | Palace courtyards and architecture with care; never to make a 40 square metre room look like a lobby, because the distortion misstates the room's size (section 14.7) |
| 24 mm | 74 degrees | 16 to 18 mm | Context: the person within the place | Establishing a guest inside the architecture; walk-throughs |
| 35 mm | 54 degrees | 24 to 25 mm | Intimacy with context; the documentary eye; close to how a person in the room sees | Hotel life, staff at work, handheld fashion |
| 50 mm | 40 degrees | 35 mm | Natural, neutral, honest | Product on a table, two people talking, food |
| 85 mm | 24 degrees | 58 to 60 mm | Flattering compression on faces, background softens, the observer at a respectful distance | Portraits, beauty, the gaze in fashion film, the house default for faces |
| 135 mm and longer | 15 degrees and narrower | 90 to 100 mm | Isolation: the subject cut out from the world; voyeurism; heat haze in desert exteriors | A model across a courtyard, a horse on a dune, a detail across a room |

Rules: faces closer than about 1 m on a lens wider than 35 mm (full frame) swell noses and shrink ears, so beauty and portrait close-ups use 75 to 135 mm; architecture on a wide lens must keep the camera level to hold verticals (8.8); and one film should use a narrow family of focal lengths, because a sequence that jumps from 18 to 135 to 24 reads as indecision.

### 8.2 Primes and zooms

Primes (fixed focal length) are faster (T1.3 to T2), lighter, optically cleaner, and impose a discipline: the director chooses the frame by placing the camera. Zooms (variable focal length) save lens changes; cinema zooms (Angénieux Optimo, Fujinon Premista, ARRI Signature Zoom, Canon CN-E) hold focus through the range, have no exposure change and match prime sets. Use zooms for events, documentary and interviews with two cameras; use primes for brand films. A zoom moving during a shot (the "crash zoom" or a creeping zoom) is a 1970s or documentary signature; it reads cheap on luxury work unless it quotes that era on purpose.

### 8.3 Cinema lenses and photo lenses

Cinema lenses carry T-stops, geared focus, iris and zoom rings for motors, long focus throws (about 270 to 300 degrees of rotation, so a focus puller can hit a mark precisely), a common front diameter (95 or 114 mm) so the matte box and filters stay on through lens changes, minimal focus breathing (the change in framing when focus racks), matched colour across the set, and witness marks (distance engravings read from the side of the lens). Photo lenses are cheaper, smaller and sharper per rupee, and lose on every one of those points: short throws, focus-by-wire on many mirrorless lenses (the ring has no hard stops, so marks cannot be repeated), breathing, and slight colour differences across a set. Rehoused photo lenses (vintage stills glass in new cinema barrels, by TLS, Van Diemen and others) bridge the two. Rule: cinema glass for anything with a focus puller; photo lenses for one-person shoots and macro, where autofocus helps.

#### 8.3.1 Mounts, flange distances and image circles

The flange focal distance is the gap between the lens mount and the sensor. A lens adapts to any body with a shorter flange through a spacer adapter that makes up the difference, which is why short mirrorless flanges take PL and EF glass; going the other way needs corrective optics and is avoided.

| Mount | Flange focal distance | Typical bodies |
|---|---|---|
| PL (positive lock) | 52.00 mm | Cinema cameras; Pyxis and URSA Cine PL versions |
| LPL (ARRI's large-format mount) | 44.00 mm | Alexa Mini LF, Alexa 35, Alexa 265 |
| Canon EF | 44.00 mm | Canon DSLRs; Pyxis EF |
| Sony E | 18.00 mm | FX3, FX6, FX2; Venice 2 and Burano carry E-mount under a removable PL adapter |
| L-mount | 20.00 mm | Panasonic Lumix, Sigma, Leica; Pyxis L-mount version |
| Canon RF | 20.00 mm | C400, C80, C50 |
| Nikon Z | 16.00 mm | Nikon ZR |
| Micro Four Thirds | 19.25 mm | Lumix GH7 |

PL lenses fit LPL bodies through ARRI's PL-to-LPL adapter; LPL lenses cannot go on PL bodies. Coverage is the image circle, the diameter of the sharp, unvignetted image a lens projects: Super 35 lenses are designed for about a 31 to 34 mm circle and vignette on full frame (43.3 mm diagonal) and ARRI LF (44.7 mm), so a Super 35 set on an FX6 or Venice 2 forces a crop mode. When booking, confirm the mount, the image circle against the recording format's diagonal, and the adapter on the rental list.

### 8.4 Anamorphic and spherical

Spherical lenses project the image unchanged. Anamorphic lenses squeeze a wider field horizontally onto the sensor, to be stretched back ("desqueezed") in post. The look: oval bokeh (out-of-focus highlights become vertical ovals), horizontal streak flares (often blue from the coatings), focus falloff and softness at the edges, barrel distortion, breathing, and "mumps" (faces appear wider at close focus on front-anamorphic designs). It is the look of widescreen cinema, and it costs light, weight, focus precision and money.

| Squeeze | Paired with | Result after desqueeze | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.33x | 16:9 sensor area | 2.37, trimmed to 2.39 | Hawk 1.3x anamorphics (Vantage Film), Sirui and Moment 1.33x lenses |
| 1.5x | Full-frame 3:2 | 2.25, or 2.39 with a slight trim of height | Atlas Mercury (1.5x, full frame) |
| 1.8x | 4:3 sensor area | 2.4 | Cooke Anamorphic/i Full Frame Plus |
| 2x | 4:3 or 6:5 sensor area | 2.67 trimmed, or 2.4 from 6:5 | Panavision C, E and G series, ARRI/Zeiss Master Anamorphic, Atlas Orion, Kowa and Lomo vintage |

The 2.39 frame is the widescreen cinema ratio (often written 2.40). The trade for Mask360: a 2.39 frame cropped to 9:16 for Reels keeps about 23.5 percent of the width. A campaign that owes vertical deliverables either shoots spherical open gate (section 10.7), runs a second camera for vertical, or treats the anamorphic film as the hero and shoots verticals separately.

### 8.5 Vintage glass

Older lenses render lower contrast, warmer colour, softer edges, veiling glare (a milky lift of the blacks when light hits the front element), and characterful flare and bokeh. That softness flatters skin and takes the digital edge off a sharp sensor.

- Cooke Speed Panchro (from the 1920s to the 1960s): the "Cooke look", gentle and warm on faces; rehoused sets are rental favourites for period and fashion.
- Bausch and Lomb Super Baltar (1960s to 1970s): soft, warm, glowing highlights; the 1970s American film look.
- Zeiss Super Speed (T1.3, from the 1970s to the 1980s, MkI to MkIII): fast, gently contrasty, the 1980s and 1990s look; still common.
- Canon K35 (1970s): warm, flaring, low contrast; music video and fashion favourite.
- Lomo (Soviet, including the round-front anamorphics) and Helios 44 (the 58 mm f/2 lens of the Zenit cameras, known for swirling bokeh): cheap character; inconsistent copy to copy.

Rules: vintage glass is a look decision made at the camera test, with the grade planned around it; never mix vintage and modern sets in one sequence; budget for the copy variation (test every lens); and know the tell: a whole film of swirl and flare reads as a filter, so one vintage sequence inside a clean film usually lands harder than an entire film of it.

### 8.7 Macro and probe lenses

Macro lenses focus close enough to render small objects at life size or larger (1:1 magnification means a 10 mm diamond covers 10 mm of sensor). Probe lenses are long, thin macro lenses that put the camera's point of view inside a scene: the Laowa 24 mm f/14 2x Macro Probe (2:1 magnification, waterproof front) and the modular Laowa Pro2be (T8, with direct, 35 degree and periscope modules) for food, jewellery and miniature sets, moving through a bowl of saffron or between watch parts. Depth of field at macro distances is under a millimetre (section 8.10), so subjects are shot at f/8 to f/16 with large amounts of light, and stills are focus-stacked (several frames at different focus distances merged in Helicon Focus or Photoshop). Diffraction softens the image as the effective aperture rises with magnification (an f/14 lens at 2:1 behaves like f/42), which is why probe shots need hard light and why their texture is distinctive.

Close-up diopters (+1/2, +1, +2 and +3, from Tiffen or Schneider, in matte-box sizes) are single-element magnifiers that let any cinema prime focus closer for inserts without changing to a macro lens, so the set's look holds. A split diopter covers half the frame and holds a near and a far plane sharp at once; it is a stylised 1970s signature (Brian De Palma's films), so use it once and on purpose.

### 8.8 Tilt and shift

Shift moves the lens parallel to the sensor, so the camera can stay level (verticals parallel) while the frame rises to include the top of a building; tilt angles the plane of focus (the Scheimpflug principle) to hold a receding surface sharp, or to throw most of the frame out of focus. Canon TS-E 17 mm and 24 mm and Nikon PC 19 mm are the stills tools; for motion they are rare because they are slow to operate, so most video architecture is shot level on a wide prime and corrected in post with some crop. The "miniature" tilt effect on a city is a 2008 to 2012 cliché; avoid it.

### 8.9 Filters

- Diffusion: Tiffen Pro-Mist and Black Pro-Mist lift and glow highlights and soften contrast; Black Pro-Mist does so with less lift in the blacks. Tiffen's own page lists the Black Pro-Mist in 1/8, 1/4 and 1/2 densities with 1, 2 and 3 marked unavailable in October 2026 (verified), and notes the halation (a glow bleeding from highlights into surrounding tones) becomes apparent from 1/4 upward. Others: Tiffen Glimmerglass (subtle sparkle), Schneider Hollywood Black Magic, Tiffen Black Satin. Rule: 1/8 for skin and hotel films, 1/4 for a deliberate glow; strength scales with focal length (a 1/8 on an 85 mm reads stronger than on a 24 mm), so test on the longest lens.
- Polariser: cuts reflections from non-metallic surfaces (glass, water, wet leaves, polished marble) and darkens blue sky at 90 degrees to the sun; costs about 1.5 to 2 stops. In hotels it removes window reflections and makes pool water see-through; on jewellery it kills the sparkle the shot needs, so it stays off the lens for diamonds.
- Effect filters: star and streak filters on jewellery are a cheap tell (design-taste, references/worlds.md, jewellery and watches: sparkle filters). Sparkle is made with light (section 14.2).
- Diffusion in post (glow, halation emulation in Resolve or Dehancer) is controllable and reversible; diffusion on the lens interacts with real highlights and looks more organic. The house uses light diffusion on the lens and leaves the decision of strength to the test.

### 8.10 Depth of field maths

Depth of field is the distance between the nearest and farthest points acceptably sharp. It depends on aperture, focal length, subject distance and the circle of confusion (the largest blur spot still read as sharp): 0.03 mm is the full-frame convention, 0.025 mm the traditional 35 mm motion picture value, and tighter values are used for 4K projection and large screens.

- Hyperfocal distance H = f squared divided by (N times c), plus f, where f is focal length, N the f-number and c the circle of confusion. Focus at H and everything from H/2 to infinity is acceptably sharp.
- Approximate total depth of field for subjects well inside the hyperfocal distance: 2 times N times c times s squared, divided by f squared, where s is the subject distance.

| Lens and stop | Subject distance | Circle of confusion | Near to far limit | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 24 mm at f/8 | Hyperfocal | 0.03 mm | 1.21 m to infinity, focused at 2.42 m | n/a |
| 24 mm at f/4 | 3 m | 0.03 mm | 1.85 m to 7.90 m | about 6 m |
| 35 mm at T2.8 | 2 m | 0.025 mm | 1.80 m to 2.25 m | about 45 cm |
| 50 mm at T2 | 2 m | 0.03 mm | 1.91 m to 2.10 m | about 19 cm |
| 85 mm at T2 | 1.5 m | 0.03 mm | 1.48 m to 1.52 m | about 3.5 cm |
| 135 mm at T2.8 | 3 m | 0.03 mm | 2.96 m to 3.04 m | about 8 cm |
| Macro at 1:1, f/8 | n/a | 0.03 mm | n/a | about 1 mm |
| Probe at 2:1, f/14 | n/a | 0.03 mm | n/a | about 0.6 mm |

Read the 85 mm row twice: an 85 mm close-up at T2 holds 3.5 cm: the near eye is sharp and the ear on the same face is already soft. That is why focus pullers ask for rehearsals and marks, and why the house stops down to T2.8 or T4 for moving talent on long lenses: the shallow look is cheap when it costs the take.

### 8.11 Focus pulling

The 1st AC pulls focus from measured distances (tape, laser, LiDAR) and marks on the lens's witness scale or a wireless hand unit, while watching the action itself. Tools: wireless follow focus systems (ARRI Hi-5 with cforce motors, Preston FI+Z, Tilta Nucleus, DJI Focus Pro with LiDAR autofocus assist), rangefinders (Preston Light Ranger 2, Cinema Products' Cinetape sonar), and a monitor with focus peaking (coloured outlines on the sharpest edges) for checking a take after it is pulled. Rules: give the AC a rehearsal with the actor's real pace; put marks on the floor (tape T marks) for actors; for long-lens moving shots, choose a deeper stop or a slower move before blaming the puller; a rack focus (shifting focus from one subject to another within the shot) is a cut inside the frame and should be motivated by a look, a sound or a story beat. The AC's word on whether a take was sharp is final.

## 9. Exposure tools

Exposure is judged on instruments, because the monitor lies: its brightness, the ambient light in video village and the LUT all change what the eye sees. The DP and DIT read the signal; the creative director reads the picture.

### 9.1 Histogram

A graph of how many pixels sit at each brightness, dark on the left, bright on the right (0 to 255 on an 8 bit file). Useful on stills cameras and phones for spotting clipping (a spike against the right wall). Weak for film work, because it does not show where in the frame a tone sits: a clipped window and a clipped face look the same.

### 9.2 Waveform and IRE

The waveform plots brightness (vertical axis, 0 to 100 percent, written IRE after the Institute of Radio Engineers) against horizontal position in the frame, so the face on the left of frame appears on the left of the trace. It is the main exposure tool on set. In Rec.709, 18 percent grey sits at about 41 percent, black at 0, and diffuse white (a white shirt in the key light) at about 90; signal above 100 is clipped or "superwhite" (to 109 in legal-range video).

Skin targets in Rec.709, as practitioner conventions (check against the DP's own practice):

| Skin | Typical level on the lit side of the face |
|---|---|
| Light skin | about 60 to 70 percent |
| Medium skin (much of South Asia and the Gulf) | about 45 to 60 percent |
| Deep skin | about 35 to 50 percent |
| Highlights on skin (forehead sheen) | below about 85 to 90 percent, so they roll off without clipping |

The number to protect is the relationship (the face against the background, the key side against the fill side) and keeping skin above the toe (the bottom of the curve where tones crush into noise). In log, the same skin sits lower and compressed; use the grey reference per curve in section 6.4, or false colour.

### 9.3 RGB parade

The parade shows three waveforms side by side, one per channel (red, green, blue). Point the camera at a grey card or white card under the key: the three traces at the same height mean the white balance is neutral. Saturated colour shows as one channel high: watch the red channel on Indian bridal red, sindoor (the red powder in a married Hindu woman's hair parting), saffron robes and red carpets, which clips in red long before the luma waveform shows trouble, producing flat, detail-less fabric. Pull exposure or saturation until the red trace sits below clip.

### 9.4 Vectorscope

The vectorscope plots colour as hue (angle) and saturation (distance from centre), with targets for the primary and secondary colours. The skin tone line (sometimes the "I line"), at about 123 degrees between red and yellow, is where human skin of every complexion falls: complexion changes mostly brightness and saturation; hue clusters near the line. If a face sits off the line toward green or magenta, the white balance or the lighting (a cheap LED's green spike) is wrong. Use it in the grade too (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 8.5).

### 9.5 False colour

False colour replaces the image with colour bands for brightness ranges, so exposure can be read at a glance on the monitor. Schemes differ by maker; ARRI's, read on the Rec.709 output, is the one most DPs know (verify against the camera manual):

| Colour | Signal range | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Purple | 0 to 2.5 percent | Black clip |
| Blue | 2.5 to 4 percent | Just above black |
| Green | 38 to 42 percent | 18 percent grey |
| Pink | 52 to 56 percent | One stop above grey; typical light skin |
| Yellow | 97 to 99 percent | Just below white clip |
| Red | 99 to 100 percent | Clipped |

Sony, Blackmagic, Canon, SmallHD and Atomos monitors use other bands; learn the scale of the monitor on the cart.

### 9.6 Zebras

Zebras are striped overlays drawn on the viewfinder above a set level. Set them at 95 to 100 percent to show clipping; the old ENG (electronic news gathering) convention of 70 percent zebras for light skin is a rough guide that underexposes medium and deep skin if obeyed. On log, set zebras on the log signal per the maker's guidance or use false colour.

### 9.7 Expose to the right in log

ETTR (expose to the right) means placing exposure as bright as possible without clipping, because sensors record more levels and less noise in the upper stops. In log on mirrorless and Sony cinema bodies, exposing about one to two stops over the base rating gives cleaner shadows and denser skin, corrected down in the grade. Limits: never clip a highlight that matters (a window, a white robe, a diamond's gradient), and on cameras with very wide highlight latitude (Alexa) the gain is small; expose at the chosen EI.

### 9.8 Grey card and colour chart

- 18 percent grey card: a reference for white balance and for mid-grey placement on the waveform.
- Colour chart: the X-Rite ColorChecker Video (the X-Rite photo line now sold under the Calibrite brand) or DSC Labs charts (ChromaDuMonde). Shoot the chart at the head of every setup under the key light, at the actual exposure, filling about a third of the frame. DaVinci Resolve's Color Match reads the ColorChecker and balances the shot automatically, which is a starting point for matching cameras and days.
- Why it matters on Mask360 shoots: a jewellery client's gold must be the same gold across a two-day shoot under different light, and a fabric's colour is a legal question when the film sells it (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 9).

### 9.9 Skin across Indian and Gulf complexions

Lighting craft for complexion is in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 6.1. The camera-side rules:

- Expose for the darkest face that matters, then subtract light from lighter faces with nets or position; recovering an underexposed deep complexion costs noise and a grey cast.
- Read skin on the waveform or false colour; zebras set for light skin mislead on medium and deep skin.
- Watch the vectorscope: South Asian skin carries red and olive undertones that low-quality LEDs push toward muddy brown; the fix is in the light (CRI above 95 and R9 above 90: the colour rendering index and its saturated-red sample; 15-lighting-and-sound.md), the check is on the scope.
- The Gulf wardrobe range: a white kandura in sun and a black abaya in the same frame span about four to five stops of reflectance before light is even considered. Put the kandura near the top of the range without clipping (false colour yellow, below the red clip band), give the abaya a kicker (a light from behind and to the side that edges the form) or a sheen so it has texture, and shade both from direct overhead sun.
- Gold and diamond jewellery on skin: the metal will be the brightest object; expose the skin first, then control the metal with flags and negative fill so its highlights roll off.

### 9.10 Protecting highlights in bright locations

Dubai and Rajasthan mean white marble, pale stone, sand, pool water and a near-vertical summer sun (at Dubai's latitude, about 25 degrees north, the noon sun stands about 88 degrees high in late June and about 41 degrees in late December). Tools, in order: shoot at the right hour (section 3.5), then shade with overhead frames (a 12 by 12 foot frame of silk or grid cloth over the talent), then ND to hold the stop (section 6.11), then negative fill to restore shape, then log or RAW to hold the remaining highlight detail. Windows in interiors: expose the room so the window sits four to five stops over grey on an Alexa or Venice, less on mirrorless, and fill the room up (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 3.5).

## 10. Composition for film

Composition principles (balance, the grid, visual weight, figure and ground) are in 05-layout-grids-and-composition.md. This section covers what changes when the frame moves and cuts.

### 10.1 Shot sizes

| Size | Abbreviation | Frame | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extreme close-up | ECU | An eye, a lip, a clasp, a stitch | Detail as revelation; texture; intensity |
| Close-up | CU | Face from chin to forehead (crop the top of the head) | Emotion, thought; the actor's shot |
| Medium close-up | MCU | Head and shoulders, cut mid-chest | Conversation, interview |
| Medium shot | MS | Waist up | Gesture and face together |
| Medium long shot | MLS | Knees up (the "cowboy" in westerns) | Body language with face still legible |
| Long shot (wide shot) | LS or WS | The whole figure with space around | The person in the place |
| Extreme long shot | ELS or EWS | Figure small in a landscape or architecture | Scale, solitude, geography |
| Two shot | 2S | Two people in one frame | Relationship |
| Over the shoulder | OTS | Over one person's shoulder onto the other | Conversation with spatial anchoring |
| Point of view | POV | What a character sees | Identification |
| Insert | INS | A detail cut into the scene: a hand, a watch face, a key | Information; product focus |

Crop rule at the frame edge: avoid cutting at joints (neck, elbows, wrists, knees, ankles), because a cut at a joint reads as an amputation; cut between them.

### 10.2 Angles

Eye level is neutral, the honest default. Low angle gives power and monumentality (a palace arch from below; talent made dominant). High angle gives vulnerability or overview, and for tabletop food the overhead top shot is its own grammar. Dutch (a tilted horizon) gives disorientation and is a cheap tell on luxury work unless the film is about unease. Bird's eye (directly overhead) and worm's eye (from ground level upward) are graphic and abstract; use each once per film at most.

### 10.3 The line, eyelines and the 30 degree rule

- The 180 degree rule: an imaginary line (the axis of action) runs between two people in a scene; keeping every camera position on one side keeps screen direction consistent (A looks right, B looks left). Cross it and the audience feels the two have swapped places.
- Eyelines: in singles, each person looks just off the lens on the side of the other person; matching the eyeline height and side across shots makes two separately filmed close-ups feel like a conversation.
- The 30 degree rule: between two consecutive shots of the same subject, move the camera at least 30 degrees around the subject (or change size substantially); smaller changes produce a jump cut, which reads as an error unless it is the style.
- Screen direction: a guest who walks left to right in the lobby must keep walking left to right into the suite, or the edit implies she turned back.
- Crossing the line with intent: move the camera across the line on screen (a dolly or Steadicam arc), cut to a neutral shot on the line (head-on or from behind), let a character cross, or cut away to an insert first. Crossing as a deliberate rupture (the moment two people's relationship flips) is a tool.

### 10.4 Blocking and staging in depth

Blocking is the planned movement of actors and camera within a scene; staging in depth places action in foreground, middle ground and background so a single frame carries several layers of information. Deep staging lets a scene play in one shot without cutting, as in Gregg Toland's deep-focus photography for Citizen Kane (1941). For a hotel film: a server crosses the foreground out of focus, the guest reads in the middle ground, the sea moves in the background; three planes make a static frame feel alive. Layering also gives the editor cut points: a foreground wipe hides a cut.

### 10.5 Headroom, lead room and the frame edge

- Headroom: the space above the head. The rule-of-thirds convention puts the eyes near the upper third line (Blain Brown, Cinematography: Theory and Practice, covers headroom and look room); a close-up crops the top of the head, a medium shot leaves a little; too much headroom is the amateur tell.
- Lead room (nose room or look room): leave space in the direction a person faces or moves; on a 16:9 single the face sits about a third in from the edge it turns away from, leaving two thirds of the width as look room. Take it away on purpose to create unease.
- The frame edge: objects that touch the edge pull the eye; a lamp cut in half at the edge reads as a mistake, a lamp clear of the edge or decisively cropped reads as intent. Check edges on every setup, because the eye at video village looks at the centre.

### 10.6 Aspect ratios

| Ratio | Name | Read | Mask360 use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.33 (4:3) | Academy, television | Portrait-like, nostalgic, intimate | Fashion films with a period or archive feel |
| 1.78 (16:9) | HD and UHD | Neutral | YouTube, websites, hotel screens |
| 1.85 | Flat widescreen | Cinematic without the scope commitment | Brand films with a cinema feel |
| 2.0 | Univisium | Between 1.85 and scope; streaming drama | Films that will also be cropped to 16:9 |
| 2.39 | Scope | Landscape, cinema, luxury | Hero brand films; letterboxed on 16:9 screens |
| 9:16 | Vertical | Phone-native, intimate, one subject | Reels, Stories, TikTok, YouTube Shorts |
| 4:5 | Portrait feed | The tallest feed image without crop | Instagram feed (26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, section 2) |
| 1:1 | Square | Graphic, centred | Grid, some paid placements |

### 10.7 Framing for several deliverables

Most Mask360 films owe 16:9 or 2.39 masters plus 9:16 and 4:5 cutdowns. Options, best first:

1. Shoot open gate (the whole sensor, usually 3:2 or close to it) at high resolution: a 6K full-frame 3:2 open gate (6,048 by 4,032 on Pyxis 6K) yields a 9:16 crop of about 2,268 by 4,032 pixels and a 2.39 crop of the full width; both exceed delivery size.
2. Shoot 16:9 UHD with 9:16 centre-protected: a 9:16 crop from 3,840 by 2,160 is about 1,215 by 2,160, enough for a 1080 by 1920 vertical. Compose with the subject inside the central third and keep critical action out of the outer thirds.
3. Run a second camera rigged vertically for the hero vertical deliverables.
4. Shoot verticals as separate setups when the vertical is a primary deliverable; the best verticals are composed as verticals.

On set: load frame lines for every deliverable on the monitors (SmallHD, Atomos and ARRI cameras all draw custom frame lines), mark the platform safe zones (26-platform-and-format-specs-2026.md, sections 5.5 and 5.6), and have the DP compose the master first and protect the rest. Cutdown craft is in 17-short-form-social-and-platform-craft.md.

### 10.8 Bruce Block's visual components

Bruce Block, in The Visual Story, names seven visual components every frame contains: space (deep, flat, limited, ambiguous), line, shape, tone (brightness), colour, movement and rhythm. His principle of contrast and affinity: the more a component contrasts within a shot or between shots, the more visual intensity; the more it shows affinity (similarity), the less. A film plots its story's intensity on a graph and assigns components to rise and fall with it.

| Component | Affinity (calm) | Contrast (intensity) | Hotel film example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space | All flat or all deep | Flat cut against deep | Flat graphic facades early, a deep corridor at the reveal |
| Line | Horizontals | Diagonals against horizontals | Horizon lines at dawn; diagonals of staircases as the day starts |
| Shape | Rounded forms | Angular against round | Domes and arches, then a sharp modern sculpture |
| Tone | Mid-tones | Black against white | Soft overcast morning, hard noon shadow at the climax |
| Colour | A narrow palette | Complementary colours | Sandstone and cream, one blue pool at the turn |
| Movement | Still or same direction | Opposing directions, still against fast | Static frames, then the one camera move |
| Rhythm | Regular cutting | Irregular, accelerating | Long holds, then three quick inserts |

Use: decide in the treatment which components carry the film's arc, so the DP, production designer and editor build the same curve.

## 11. Camera movement

### 11.1 Stillness first

The house preference is the static frame: a locked-off camera on a tripod, composed with care, in which only the subject moves. Reasons: stillness is confidence (design-taste, section 2, on slowness); it lets light, performance and production design carry the shot; it cuts with anything; and it is the opposite of the restless gimbal that marks most content. A film of twenty still frames and one move makes that move an event. Every move must answer "what does this reveal?", and if the answer is "nothing", the camera stays.

### 11.2 The moves

| Move | How | What it says | Tell when wrong | Kit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pan and tilt | The camera rotates horizontally (pan) or vertically (tilt) on a fixed point | Following, scanning, revealing what is beside or above | Pans too fast for the frame rate judder (strobing); a common rule of thumb is that an object should take at least 5 to 7 seconds to cross the frame at 24 fps (the American Cinematographer Manual tabulates safe panning speeds) | Fluid head (Sachtler, OConnor), geared head for precision |
| Dolly in and out | The camera travels toward or away from the subject on track or a dolly | Approach, attention, intimacy (in); release, isolation (out) | A push with no beat at its end | Dolly on track, slider for short moves |
| Zoom | The lens magnifies without the camera moving | Attention without travel; a documentary or 1970s signature | Used where a dolly was meant: no parallax, so it looks flat | Cinema zoom |
| Truck (track) | The camera travels sideways | Accompanying, revealing a space in layers | No foreground: without parallax a truck looks like a pan | Dolly, slider |
| Pedestal | The camera rises or lowers vertically without tilting | Revealing over an object (a table, a balustrade) | | Dolly column, jib |
| Arc | The camera circles the subject | Significance, the pivot of a scene | The gimbal orbit around a product, the most generic move in content | Dolly on round track, Steadicam |
| Crane and jib | The camera rises or descends through space on an arm | Scale, transition between intimate and epic | The crane-up ending on every event film | Jib, Technocrane (telescoping, so the arm extends during the shot) |
| Handheld | Operator holds the camera | Presence, urgency, the body in the room | Shaky without intent | Shoulder rig, Easyrig support vest |
| Steadicam | Body-worn arm and sled isolate the camera from footsteps | Gliding presence through real space; long takes | Floating with no motivation | Steadicam with an experienced operator |
| Gimbal | Motorised stabiliser (DJI Ronin RS series, Ronin 2, Ronin 4D; Freefly MoVI) | Smooth travel at low cost | Horizon locked, weightless, every move the same speed; the "gimbal walk" | Gimbal plus an operator who walks like a Steadicam operator |
| Drone | Aerial camera | Geography, scale, approach | Section 11.6 | Section 4.5 and 4.6 permissions |
| Motion control | Computer-driven robotic arm repeating the same move exactly | Precision, impossible speed, repeatable passes for compositing | Over-elaborate moves on a simple product | MRMC Bolt (high-speed cinebot arm), Bolt X, Milo |
| Vehicle mounts | Camera on or alongside a car | Travel, speed, landscapes | Unsafe rigs; unpermitted road work | Hard mounts, suction mounts, tracking vehicle with a stabilised arm (the Russian Arm) |

### 11.3 Dolly against zoom

A dolly changes the camera's position, so near objects move faster than far ones (parallax) and perspective changes; a zoom only enlarges the image, so planes stay locked together. That is why a dolly-in feels like walking toward someone and a zoom feels like looking harder. The dolly zoom (dolly in while zooming out, holding the subject's size while the background stretches) from Vertigo (1958) and Jaws (1975) is a cliché in brand work.

### 11.4 Handheld, controlled

Fashion film handheld is controlled: an operator with weight in the rig (a heavier camera damps micro-shake), an Easyrig to carry it, a wide-to-normal lens (24 to 50 mm), and movement that breathes with the talent's rhythm. It reads as intimacy and presence. Uncontrolled handheld on a long lens reads as amateur.

### 11.5 Steadicam and gimbals

Garrett Brown's Steadicam (introduced in 1975; early landmark uses in Bound for Glory and Rocky in 1976 and The Shining in 1980) lets a camera glide through real space with an operator's judgement in the motion. Gimbals give similar smoothness for a fraction of the cost, and their tells are the reason to use them with care: a perfectly locked horizon, a floating weightlessness, constant speed, and the orbit. Fixes: add a Z-axis damper or use a Ronin 4D (which stabilises vertical bounce), operate with Steadicam footwork, vary speed, and start and end on composed frames.

### 11.6 Drones

Cinematic drones (DJI Inspire 3 class, with interchangeable lenses and a full-frame gimbal camera) give slow, stable aerials; FPV drones (first-person-view, flown with goggles, often small "cinewhoops" with prop guards) fly through spaces at speed, as in the single-take bowling alley film Right Up Our Alley (2021, flown by Jay Christensen). When a drone is wrong: the establishing shot of a pool at golden hour, the top-down of a resort, any aerial added because the drone was on the truck; design-taste lists the "drone establishing shot without purpose" among the cheap tells. When it is right: scale that only height shows (a palace on its lake, a desert camp against dunes), a continuous approach from landscape into building, and geography the story needs. FPV through a hotel reads as real estate content in 2026; use it only when the speed is the idea.

### 11.7 Motion control

A motion control rig repeats a programmed move to the millimetre, so several passes can be composited: the bottle, then the pour, then the background, then the light pass. The MRMC Bolt moves fast enough to track a falling object at high frame rates, which is how product films catch a ring dropping into water or perfume splashing in a single sharp frame. Book the motion control operator with the rig, programme moves at the tech scout, and budget a pre-light day; the Bolt rewards precision and punishes improvisation.

### 11.8 Motivated movement and speed ramps

Motivated movement is camera movement caused by something in the scene: the camera follows a waiter, rises because the guest stands, drifts with the wind in the curtain. Unmotivated movement draws attention to the camera. The reveal rule: a move should end on more information than it started with (a door opens onto the courtyard; a push in arrives at the eyes). Speed ramps (changing playback speed within a shot, from real time to slow motion) read cheap when they are cut on music beats for energy, as in action-cam and wedding films; they work when the ramp is a single moment in the story (a necklace clasp catching the light) and the rest of the film runs at one speed.

## 12. Coverage and shot design

### 12.1 Master and coverage

The master is a wide shot that plays the whole scene; coverage is the closer shots (singles, overs, inserts) shot afterwards so the editor can shape it. Shoot master first (it sets continuity and light direction), then work in tighter, relighting each direction once. A two-person dialogue scene in the standard pattern is five setups: the master, two over-the-shoulders and two singles (Steven Katz, Film Directing Shot by Shot, works through the variations). Brand films rarely need full dramatic coverage; they need the right inserts and alternative takes of the hero moments.

### 12.2 Inserts, cutaways and B-roll

Inserts show a detail within the scene; cutaways leave the scene (a bird outside the window) to compress time or hide a cut. B-roll is the supporting footage of a brand film: textures, hands, materials, light moving across surfaces, people at a distance, transitions. B-roll strategy for a hotel or jewellery film: list five categories at the treatment (craft, material, light, place, people), shoot each in three sizes and at two speeds, and give every B-roll shot a 10 second hold, so the editor has room. A B-roll list made at the treatment gives the editor a vocabulary.

### 12.3 Shot ratio

The shot ratio is footage shot divided by footage used. Commonly cited ranges: film features historically about 10:1 to 20:1, digital features and commercials higher with no single published figure, documentaries 100:1 and above; the house records its own ratio per job. A brand film worked through: three shoot days rolling about 2 hours of camera time a day for a 90 second film is 21,600 seconds over 90, a ratio of about 240:1. Digital makes rolling cheap and reviewing expensive, so budget logging, sync and editor review time against that volume (section 5.3), bound B-roll rolling with the list (section 12.2), and cut the camera on clear "cut" calls.

### 12.4 Shooting for the edit

Roll at least 3 seconds before action and hold 3 seconds after the action ends (the handles the editor needs for dissolves and trims); overlap action between sizes (if the guest lifts a cup in the wide, she lifts it in the close-up too, so the editor can cut on the action); shoot reaction shots and listening shots; record wild lines (dialogue recorded without picture) and 30 seconds of room tone at every setup (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 10).

### 12.5 Shooting for social cutdowns

Hold the first frame composed and strong for the opening second, because the opening three seconds decide retention (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 3.4); shoot loopable moves whose last frame matches the first; keep text-safe space in the frame for supers; frame for vertical as in section 10.7; and capture a few vertical-native setups of the hero moments.

### 12.6 Continuity

The script supervisor tracks eyelines, prop positions, liquid levels in glasses, cigarette and candle lengths, hair, wardrobe (buttons, sleeves, jewellery), and light direction and time of day; continuity photos are taken at the end of every take that matters. In a hotel film shot over two days, the sun's position is continuity: a breakfast scene shot at 08:00 on day one cannot pick up at 11:00 on day two.

### 12.7 Plates for VFX and AI extension

A plate is footage shot to be composited. For each VFX or AI-extended shot, record: a clean plate (the same frame, locked off, with no actors, 5 to 10 seconds); camera height, tilt, lens, focus distance and stop; an HDRI (a 360 degree high dynamic range photo of the location, bracketed, for lighting CG, computer-generated, objects); a chrome ball and grey ball for light reference; a lens distortion grid; tracking markers on green or blank surfaces; and measurements. For AI extension (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 6.3), shoot wider than the final frame, keep highlights unclipped, and save stills in RAW, because the generated extension inherits the plate's quality.

### 12.8 Stills and motion on one set

Hybrid shoots are the norm for Mask360 clients (light for both is in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 8). Three models:

| Model | How | Right when | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stills window | After motion completes a setup, the photographer gets 10 to 20 minutes on the same light and talent; the 1st AD schedules it as a line | Stills are secondary and share the motion look | 10 to 20 minutes per setup, so fewer setups a day (section 3.5.1) |
| Parallel stills unit | A second set or corner with its own light, talent time and HMU checks | Stills are a primary deliverable (print, OOH: out-of-home billboards and site posters) | A second crew, and a talent-time split agreed in the contract |
| Frame grabs | Stills pulled from the motion files | Feed and web only | A separate pass at a fast shutter |

Frame-grab rules: a grab needs a shutter of about 1/200 or faster (45 degrees at 25 fps) to freeze movement, which strobes the motion take, so grabs are shot as their own pass. Resolution sets the use: UHD gives 8.3 MP (social only); 6K 3:2 open gate (6,048 by 4,032) gives 24.4 MP; 8K (8,192 by 4,320) gives 35.4 MP, enough for the feed and small print and short of OOH. Shoot RAW or 12 bit for grabs and plan the grab frame with the photographer. Talent usage for stills is priced separately from motion (24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7), and both units shoot the same ColorChecker so stills and film grade to one reference (04-colour.md).

## 13. Directing

### 13.1 Talent direction

Direct the thought and the intention; a playable direction gives the actor something to do inside, and the face follows. Asking for the face directly is result direction (asking for a look), and it produces acting. Judith Weston's Directing Actors (1996) is the standard text.

| Result direction (avoid) | Playable direction (use) |
|---|---|
| "Look happy." | "You have just heard the news you were waiting for; keep it to yourself." |
| "Be more mysterious." | "You know something about this room that the camera does not." |
| "Smile at the end." | "On the last line, think of the person you would bring here." |
| "Walk slower." | "You have nowhere to be until tonight." |

Action verbs give tasks with a goal: to welcome, to tease, to reassure, to resist. One note at a time; give it privately and briefly; praise specifically.

### 13.2 Non-actors and hotel staff

Real chefs, butlers, gardeners and craftspeople are the most valuable cast in hospitality films because their hands know the work. Direct them by giving them their real task (polish the brass you polish every morning) and shoot the task; ask for the action again and leave lines and smiles out of it; keep the crew small and the lens long (85 to 135 mm from across the room), so they forget the camera; shoot many takes of short actions. Robert Bresson's Notes on the Cinematographer (1975) is the classic on working with "models" instead of actors, and Jonathan Glazer's Under the Skin (2013) filmed real people with hidden cameras. Every staff member needs a release, arranged through the hotel's HR, and a staff member who declines is kept out of frame without comment.

### 13.3 Models in fashion film

Fashion film asks people trained for the still to move: give a tempo, a path and a purpose (cross to the window as if someone called you), play music on set at the tempo of the edit, and use a movement director for anything choreographed. The gaze carries the meaning: to the lens is address (the viewer is seen), off lens is observation (the viewer watches). Shoot the walk at 50 or 60 fps for a 50 or 40 percent slowness that keeps the body's truth. Three to five takes per setup is enough; after that, models start posing for the camera's rhythm.

### 13.4 Children and animals

Children: the legal hour limits in India (five hours a day, no more than three without rest) and guardian presence (section 4.1); schedule them first in the day, in short windows, with a chaperone and a quiet holding room; play games to get the performance; two cameras save repeats. Animals: in India, the Animal Welfare Board of India requires permission before using animals in films and advertisements and issues a no-objection certificate after (verify the current process); book a licensed wrangler and a vet on set; horses in palace films need their own handlers, water and shade; never use an animal in heat or near pyrotechnics.

### 13.5 Timing, energy and running the set

The director sets the set's energy: calm and quick. Keep the talent's waiting time short (they lose energy while the crew lights), rehearse while the crew finishes, and protect the first take, which is often the freshest. Run the set through the 1st AD: the director decides, the 1st AD executes the schedule. Keep a floating hour in the schedule each day for the shot nobody planned: the light through dust at 17:40, the gardener's dog, the staff laughing at lunch. Those shots end up in the film because they could not be planned; the hour exists so taking them costs nothing.

### 13.6 Working with the DP

The conversation before anything else is light and lens: where the light comes from in each scene, at what hour, how hard; which focal lengths and format; how still or moving; which references. Then test: a camera and lens test with the actual talent in their make-up and wardrobe, graded into a show LUT, is the cheapest day of the production and settles skin, diffusion and highlights before the client sees a frame. House practice: one test day in prep week, at least five working days before the shoot, so the show LUT reaches the DIT and the graded tests reach the PPM. The model is a long partnership: Roger Deakins with Joel and Ethan Coen from Barton Fink (1991) to Hail, Caesar! (2016), or Ari Wegner's long preparation with Jane Campion on The Power of the Dog (section 17). On set, the director chooses what and the DP chooses how; disagreements happen at the monitor quietly and away from talent and client.

### 13.7 Reviewing on set

Monitor discipline: one calibrated monitor (a SmallHD or Flanders Scientific with the show LUT) is the reference; client monitors carry the same LUT and a note that they are not colour-accurate. Keep video village away from the camera and talent. Review playback only at decision points (the end of a setup, a hero take), because each playback costs minutes the light does not have. The creative director checks each setup against the board and the idea sentence and confirms the cover (19-campaigns-and-creative-direction.md).

Remote review, when the approving client is in another city: Frame.io Camera to Cloud uploads proxies from supported cameras and encoders while rolling (support includes RED, Canon C400 and C80, Fujifilm, Panasonic, Teradek and Atomos devices; verify the current list), and live streams run through Teradek (Serv, Wave), QTAKE Cloud or Evercast with a few seconds of latency. House rules: the remote client watches through the same show LUT with a "not colour-accurate" note; comments travel one channel (agency producer to creative director to director); the remote feed is never a second voice to talent. Budget the encoder, a bonded or 5G uplink (several mobile connections combined into one) and an operator, and test the uplink at the recce, because palace and desert locations often have no usable signal.

### 13.8 When to move on

Move on when three things are true: the beat is captured as intended (performance and timing), it is technically clean (focus, exposure, sound, frame), and there is a safety take (a second good take or an alternative). Chasing a better take past that point costs the next setup, and the next setup is usually in the film too.

### 13.9 Celebrity talent

Celebrity calls are contracted in hours, often a short block inside the shift, so the schedule shoots every celebrity setup back to back with stand-ins doing all the lighting beforehand. Riders (the contract's list of on-set requirements) cover vanity vans (a Mumbai budget line), the celebrity's own HMU and stylist, security and entourage meals (section 5.1). The celebrity's team usually approves stills and selects, so add an approval round to the post schedule (section 5.3). Run a look test (talent photographed in make-up and wardrobe under the planned light) or a fitting before the day. Usage, exclusivity and the AI-likeness clause follow section 3.8 and 24-business-of-creative-and-agency-operations.md, section 3.7.

## 14. Specialised cinematography

Lighting for each subject is in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 6; the clichés per category are in design-taste, references/worlds.md. This section is the camera side.

### 14.1 Product

Tabletop on a sweep (a curved backdrop with no visible horizon) or a built surface, camera on a geared head or motion control; turntables (motorised, programmable, from 1 rpm) for 360 degree reveals; macro and probe lenses (section 8.7) for texture; the Bolt for repeatable passes and for following falling or splashing objects. Liquids and pours: shoot at 240 fps and above for splashes (section 6.9), use a hero bottle and duplicates, a wrangler for refills, a narrow shutter for crisp droplets, and backlight through liquids so they glow. A pour is designed on the tech scout: nozzle, angle, glass, backlight, catch tray. Products must be immaculate: clean with lint-free gloves, check for dust at 200 percent on the monitor between takes.

### 14.2 Jewellery

Metal and stones are mirrors, so the image is a picture of the light around them: large diffusion with black and white cards to draw gradients along gold and platinum (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 6.3). Sparkle is made with small hard sources (pinpoint LEDs, fibre-optic heads) and with movement: either the camera or the piece moves a few degrees so facets fire in turn; a slow turntable or a hand turning a ring is the classic. Star filters and added glints in post are tells. Hands and skin: book a hand model, manicure and moisturise, keep hands cool and raised before takes (veins recede), and shoot at 85 to 100 mm macro so skin texture is real but kind. Gold colour is checked against the chart (section 9.8) every setup. Custody, client-goods insurance and launch embargo follow section 3.9.

### 14.3 Beauty

Clamshell light (a key above and a fill or reflector below, see 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 2.6), long lenses (85 to 135 mm) and macro for texture (a cream's surface, a lipstick bullet, a pore that proves the skin is real), slow movement (slider, dolly or motion control at a crawl), 50 to 120 fps for hair and product application. Skin is shot real and retouched lightly (16-editing-colour-grading-and-delivery.md, section 10, covers the law on retouching).

### 14.4 Food

Backlight or side-backlight so steam, sauces and glazes glow; steam fades within seconds of plating, so hold the shot ready before the plate lands and use a steamer as a backup; drizzles and pours at 100 to 240 fps; top shots for plates, 45 degree angles for burgers and stacked food, low angles for drinks; a food stylist is core crew, and every hero dish has duplicates. Speed: food dies on set within minutes; light with a stand-in plate first.

### 14.5 Hospitality and interiors

Plan each room by time of day: east rooms in the morning, west terraces at golden hour, exteriors at twilight. Twilight (blue hour) is the moment the sky's brightness matches the interior lights, about 15 to 30 minutes after sunset; near the tropics it is short (civil twilight lasts roughly 20 to 25 minutes in Mumbai and Dubai against 35 or more in London), so it is scheduled to the minute with every lamp on and the pool lights warmed up. Empty rooms and absent or blurred people follow design-taste, references/worlds.md (hospitality, real estate); the camera side is one sign of life per frame (a book open, steam from a cup, a curtain moving). The clichés (the drone over the pool, the slow-motion smile, the petals on the bed, the champagne clink) are refused by name in the treatment.

Shooting in a live hotel (durations are house planning):
- Rooms: suites used as sets or holding come out of inventory and are charged, often at the best available rate, unless the location agreement trades them for content usage; book a set suite from the night before and dress it then.
- Service windows: restaurants and bars shoot between services (about 15:00 to 18:30) or after close; lobbies, pools and beaches about 05:30 to 08:30, before guests.
- Sound: air conditioning in Gulf summer can be cut only for minutes per take, so plan wild lines and ADR (automated dialogue replacement, re-recorded in post).
- People: engineering and housekeeping stay on call, and one hotel liaison (usually the marketing or PR manager) sits at video village.
- Approvals: for a branded property (IHCL, Marriott, Accor, Jumeirah, Aman), the brand's corporate standards team approves the cut where the property is named; add 3 to 6 weeks to the post schedule for it. Guest privacy follows section 4.1.

### 14.6 Fashion film

Music first: the track or a temp at the right tempo is chosen before the shoot and played on set, because fashion film is cut to music and the movement must be performed to it. The walk and the gaze are the grammar (section 13.3). Light and lens follow the clothes: long lenses to compress and abstract, close handheld for intimacy, slow motion at 50 to 60 fps for fabric. Study the directors in section 17 for how movement, casting and music carry a film without story.

Formats: look films (15 to 30 seconds per look), the campaign film (60 to 90 seconds) and the runway film; plan 2 to 3 looks a day (section 3.5.1). For slow motion performed to music, play the track on set sped up by the capture ratio (double speed when shooting 50 fps for 25 fps playback), so movement lands on the beat when slowed. Wind machines and fans need fabric-specific tests, because silk, organza and wool each move differently at the same airspeed. Runway coverage uses at least three positions: a head-on long zoom at the end of the runway, a side mid shot, and a roaming handheld for details and backstage. A movement director is booked for choreographed looks. The shot-for-shot fashion film list (Thunder Perfect Mind, Kenzo World, Gucci Bloom and others) is in 00-curriculum-and-method.md, section 6.7.

### 14.7 Architecture and real estate

Level camera and tilt-shift or post correction for verticals (section 8.8); wide primes (18 to 24 mm full frame) used sparingly; golden hour for facades with texture, blue hour for buildings with lit interiors; slow lateral moves (truck or slider) that reveal depth with parallax. Drone only with purpose: the building's relation to sea, city or desert. In India, advertised project details and images must match the RERA registration (with the state's Real Estate Regulatory Authority), and in Dubai real estate advertising needs a Trakheesi permit (Dubai's real estate advertising permit; 10-ux-writing-and-content-design.md and 13-motion-graphics-and-3d-production.md cover the rules); renders are labelled as artist's impressions.

### 14.8 Events

Run and gun (shooting reactively with a minimal kit and no setups) with two cameras at least: one on a zoom for the stage and speeches (an FX6 or C400 with a 24 to 105 mm or a cinema zoom), one roaming with a fast prime for details and people. Audio is the event: take a feed from the venue's sound desk into a recorder, add a wireless mic on the speaker, and record ambient with a camera mic; jam timecode across all devices (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 10). White balance to the stage key and accept the colour washes. For live output, a switcher (Blackmagic ATEM) and SRT or RTMP streaming (protocols for sending a live feed over the internet); the URSA Cine and Pyxis can stream directly (verified for URSA Cine). Same-day edits for social are cut on site.

Indian luxury and destination weddings (Udaipur, Jaipur, Goa, Abu Dhabi) are events that cannot be repeated: multi-camera coverage of every ritual, fast primes and dual base ISO for the pheras (the circling of the sacred fire) at night by firelight, local police permission for drones over a baraat (the groom's procession) on top of Digital Sky clearance, venue curfews on the call sheet, and a same-day edit as a deliverable. The genre's tells (speed ramps on beats, drone orbits, slow-motion smiles; section 11.8) are the cliché list for the treatment.

### 14.9 Interviews and talking heads

Two cameras: a medium on a 50 to 85 mm and a close-up on 85 to 135 mm from a different angle (at least 30 degrees apart), so cuts hide edits. Eyeline: the subject talks to the interviewer sitting beside the lens, a little off axis, which reads as conversation; talking straight to the lens (with an Interrotron-style teleprompter device, as Errol Morris made famous) reads as testimony and suits founders. Light with a soft key on the side away from camera (short lighting) and a separated background (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 2.5). When to avoid an interview: when the film's idea is a feeling, a talking head explains it away; use voice-over over picture, or let the craft speak.

### 14.10 Watches

Hands at 10:10 (15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 6.4). Hack the movement (pull the crown to stop the seconds hand) so the second hand and date hold continuity across takes. Keep the lens a few degrees off the crystal's perpendicular so the camera does not reflect in it. A polariser cuts glare on the crystal, which is glass; it has little effect on polished metal, whose reflections are barely polarised. Lume shots need the dial charged under bright or UV light and a separate long-exposure pass in darkness.

### 14.11 Cars

A tracking vehicle with a stabilised arm for moving shots; low-loaders or process trailers (the picture car carried on a low trailer, so talent can act while a driver tows) for dialogue; and police or traffic permission for any road use: Mumbai traffic police through the single window (section 4.3), Dubai's Roads and Transport Authority (RTA) and police alongside the DFTC permit (verify). Avoid wide-angle distortion on bodywork, which brands reject; lighting is in 15-lighting-and-sound.md, section 6.10.

## 15. Phone cinematography

As of October 2026, Apple's iPhone 18 Pro spec page lists 4K Dolby Vision at 24, 25, 30, 60, 100 and 120 fps; ProRes up to 4K at 120 fps with external recording; ProRes RAW (enabled by compatible apps); Apple Log 2; the Academy Color Encoding System; genlock (locking several cameras to one sync signal) with compatible hardware; slow motion to 240 fps at 1080p; and a 24 mm main camera with variable aperture from f/1.48 to f/4.0, a 13 mm ultra wide at f/2.2, and a 100 mm (4x) telephoto at f/2.8 (verified). The iPhone 17 Pro (September 2025) introduced ProRes RAW, Apple Log 2 and genlock to the line, with a fixed-aperture main camera.

- Apps: the native Camera app records Apple Log and ProRes; Blackmagic Camera (free, iOS and Android) gives cinema controls (shutter angle, ISO, white balance, false colour, frame guides) and Blackmagic Cloud sync; Final Cut Camera (Apple, free) offers manual controls and multicam with an iPad; Filmic Pro became subscription-only and, after its owner Bending Spoons laid off its team in 2023 (reported), lost its default status among professionals (verify dates).
- HDR by default: iPhone records HDR video (Dolby Vision, HLG) by default, which uploads too bright and inconsistent beside graded SDR work; for SDR social pipelines turn HDR video off, or shoot Apple Log 2 and grade to Rec.709.
- Android and pocket cameras: Samsung's Galaxy S25 Ultra and later record a Log profile, and Samsung's APV (Advanced Professional Video) codec is the platform's ProRes-class option (verify per model); Blackmagic Camera runs on supported Android phones; the DJI Osmo Pocket 3 (section 7) is the stabilised alternative when a phone's small-sensor tells matter.
- External recording: ProRes 4K at high frame rates needs a USB-C SSD (Samsung T7 or T9 class) clipped to the rig, since internal storage fills in minutes.
- Stabilisation: Action mode for walking; a gimbal (DJI Osmo Mobile series) or a cage on a tripod for anything composed; ND and lens attachments in a cage (Moment, SmallRig) to hold a 180 degree shutter in daylight.
- When the phone is right: social-native content where speed and intimacy beat polish, a crash camera in a car or a pool, behind the scenes, a recce, a live hotel story, and any shoot where a cinema camera would change how people behave.
- The tells: deep focus everywhere (small sensors), oversharpened edges, highlights that clip hard on bright sky, auto exposure pumping, ultra-wide distortion on faces, and the processed "HDR" look. Fixes: Apple Log 2 with a LUT in the grade, locked exposure and white balance, the 4x telephoto for faces, ND for shutter control.

## 16. AI video direction as of October 2026

Models, versions, prices and licences are in 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 2.2; the prompt systems for Higgsfield (Kling, Veo and others) are in the higgsfield-prompting skill. This section is the cinematographer's language translated into prompts.

### 16.1 The vocabulary that works

Video models respond to the same words a DP uses, because their training captions come from film and stock descriptions. Write one camera move, one lens, one light and one composition per shot.

| Layer | Words that steer | Words that fail |
|---|---|---|
| Camera | "static locked-off shot", "slow dolly in", "lateral truck left", "pedestal up", "handheld, subtle breathing", "slow arc around" | "cinematic camera", "dynamic camera", several moves in one shot |
| Lens | "85mm lens, shallow depth of field", "35mm, natural perspective", "anamorphic, oval bokeh, horizontal flare", "macro, extreme close-up" | "8K ultra HD", "sharp", "high quality" |
| Light | "soft window light from frame left", "warm practical lamps", "golden hour backlight", "overcast soft daylight", "blue hour, interior lights on" | "beautiful lighting", "dramatic lighting" |
| Composition | "centred symmetrical frame", "subject in the left third, negative space right", "low angle", "top-down overhead" | "good composition" |
| Motion and time | "slow motion", "real-time", "fabric moving gently in wind", "steam rising" | "epic", "fast-paced" |
| Texture | "35mm film grain", "subtle halation", "Kodak Vision3 500T look" | "photorealistic", "hyperrealistic" |

### 16.2 Image to video from set stills

The house method is image to video from stills shot on the real set (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 6.9, the 70 to 30 rule): the stills photographer or DP shoots RAW frames of each planned AI shot at the real location, in the real light, with the real product, composed for the motion to come (space in the direction of movement, unclipped highlights). The generated motion inherits the real light, material and place, which is what makes it hold up beside live action. Use start and end frames for controlled moves, and reference images for product consistency (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 6.4).

### 16.3 What AI cannot yet do well, and the hybrid pipeline

Weak as of October 2026: continuity across shots (wardrobe, jewellery details and faces drift), hands and fingers in action, readable text and logos, specific real locations (a model cannot produce the actual courtyard of a named palace), exact product geometry, and physical cause and effect (pours, collisions). The hybrid pipeline: shoot the hero assets for real (faces, product, the location), generate only the shots the budget or physics cannot reach (an aerial over a monsoon sea, a set extension, a transition), grade both together, and document what was generated (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 6.11). Disclosure follows 22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 7.6, including India's labelling rules for synthetically generated information and platform AI labels.

The conform, for cutting generated shots into a 4K log or RAW timeline: most models output 720p to 1080p (some upscale to 4K) at about 24 fps as compressed 8 bit Rec.709, in generations of 4 to 15 seconds (22-ai-era-craft-and-generative-tools.md, section 2.2; Luma's EXR export in ACES is the exception). So grade the live action first and generate from graded stills, so the AI shot already matches; an 8 bit generation breaks into banding under a log-style grade, so it is graded lightly. Generate at the timeline's frame rate, or conform 24 to 25 fps as a 4 percent speed change. Upscale once, at the end, with a faithful model (Topaz). Lay one grain and halation pass over the whole timeline so both sources share a texture, and keep an AI shot from cutting directly against a high-detail live-action close-up, where the resolution gap shows.

## 18. Checklists

### 18.1 48 hours before

- Treatment, shot list, boards and schedule signed off; the idea sentence and the "what we are not doing" list printed for the PPM book.
- Risk assessment circulated (section 4.7) and a safety briefing scheduled at call; midday ban hours blocked out for Gulf exteriors from 15 June to 15 September.
- Camera and lens test graded into a show LUT; skin test with the talent's make-up; licences on the camera confirmed.
- Permits in hand (DFTC, ASI, society NOC, police, traffic for road work), drone UIN, RPC and airspace permission (or DCAA permit), insurance certificate sent to rental houses.
- Releases printed (talent, location, minors, staff), with the AI clause.
- Weather, sunrise, sunset and blue hour times on the call sheet; monsoon or heat plan B.
- Media: enough cards and drives for twice the expected footage, sized with the formula in section 6.5; checksummed offload software ready (Silverstack, ShotPut Pro or Hedge).
- Frame lines for every deliverable loaded on the monitors; frame rate and shutter per shot confirmed; flicker test list for LEDs at high frame rates.
- Duplicates of every consumable prop; hero product cleaned and boxed; jewellery security, custody log and client-goods cover confirmed in writing (section 3.9); clearance log started (section 4.1).
- Remote review uplink tested if the client is off site (section 13.7).

### 18.2 On set

Chart and grey card at the head of each setup; slate every take; camera and sound reports kept. Exposure read on waveform or false colour; focus checked on playback for hero takes. Room tone at every location and continuity photos at every hero take. The 9:16 and 4:5 protection checked on the monitor for each setup. The floating hour spent on the shot nobody planned. Video village held to one reference monitor and one voice to talent, with notes through the director.

### 18.3 Wrap

- Media: every card offloaded with checksum verification to two drives before it is formatted; the 3-2-1 rule (three copies, on two different media, one stored off-site; popularised by Peter Krogh) applied before the crew leaves the location; a camera card is never formatted until the DIT confirms two verified copies.
- Custody: the log reconciled and countersigned before the brand representative leaves.
- Paperwork: all releases collected and photographed; location restored and signed off by the owner; continuity photos, camera reports, sound reports and script supervisor notes delivered to the editor with the media.
- Kit: returned, checked against the rental list, damage reported the same day.
- Post: dailies and synced proxies uploaded for review within 24 hours; the post schedule (section 5.3) re-sent with confirmed dates.

## Terms introduced

- 180 degree rule: keep every camera on one side of the axis of action so screen direction stays consistent.
- 180 degree shutter rule: a shutter open for half of each frame interval, so shutter speed is about double the frame rate.
- 3-2-1 rule: three copies of the media, on two different kinds of storage, one kept off-site.
- 30 degree rule: move the camera at least 30 degrees between consecutive shots of one subject to avoid a jump cut.
- Abaya and sheila: the full-length robe and the headscarf worn by many Gulf women.
- Abby Singer and martini shot: the last shot before a company move or the second-last of the day, and the last shot of the day.
- Above the line and below the line: creative principals agreed before production, and the crew, kit, locations and post that scale with days.
- Academy, flat, Univisium and scope: the 1.33, 1.85, 2.0 and 2.39 aspect ratios.
- ACES (Academy Color Encoding System): the Academy's colour management framework for moving images.
- ADR (automated dialogue replacement): re-recording dialogue in post to replace unusable location sound.
- Anamorphic lens: a lens that squeezes a wide field horizontally onto the sensor, desqueezed in post, with oval bokeh and streak flares.
- Animatic: storyboard frames cut to time with temporary sound.
- Arc, pedestal and truck: camera moves that circle the subject, rise or fall without tilting, and travel sideways.
- AV script: a two-column script with picture on the left and sound on the right, timed.
- Axis of action: the imaginary line between two subjects that the camera stays on one side of.
- B-roll: supporting footage of textures, details, places and people that carries a brand film between hero moments.
- Best boy electric: the gaffer's deputy for crew, power distribution and rental orders.
- Bird's eye and worm's eye: a camera directly overhead, and one at ground level looking up.
- Bit depth: the number of tonal steps per colour channel (8 bit is 256, 10 bit 1,024, 12 bit 4,096).
- Blocking: the planned movement of actors and camera in a scene.
- Blue hour: the twilight when the sky's brightness matches lit interiors, about 20 to 25 minutes near the tropics.
- Bokeh: how a lens renders out-of-focus highlights; oval on anamorphics.
- Bonded uplink: several mobile data connections combined into one stream, for remote review from location.
- Call sheet: the daily schedule sent to cast and crew the evening before.
- Camera to Cloud: Frame.io's system for uploading proxies from cameras and encoders while rolling.
- CDL (ASC Color Decision List): slope, offset, power and saturation values that carry a look between systems.
- Checksum verification: a hash comparison proving each copy of the media matches the card.
- Chroma subsampling: recording colour at lower resolution than brightness (4:4:4, 4:2:2, 4:2:0).
- Chrome ball and grey ball: reflective and matte spheres shot on set as light reference for CG.
- Cinewhoop: a small FPV drone with prop guards, built for flying through interiors.
- Circle of confusion: the largest blur spot still read as sharp; 0.03 mm by full-frame convention.
- Clamshell: a beauty setup with a key above the face and a fill or reflector below.
- Clean plate: the same locked-off frame recorded without actors, for compositing.
- Clearance log: the art department's record of every artwork, brand, building and screen in frame and its permission.
- Close-up diopter and split diopter: a single-element magnifier that lets a lens focus closer, and one that covers half the frame to hold two focus planes.
- Company move: relocating the whole unit to a new location during a shoot day.
- Contrast and affinity: Bruce Block's principle that contrast in a visual component raises intensity and affinity lowers it.
- Coverage: the closer shots filmed after the master so the editor can shape a scene.
- Crash zoom: a fast zoom during a shot, a 1970s and documentary signature.
- CRI and R9: the colour rendering index of a light source, and its saturated-red test sample.
- Crop factor: the ratio of the full-frame diagonal to a sensor's diagonal, used to compare framing between formats.
- Custody log: a signed record of each high-value piece leaving and returning to the product table.
- Cutaway: a shot outside the scene's action used to compress time or hide a cut.
- Dailies: the day's footage, synced and with the look applied, for review.
- DCI 4K: the 4,096 pixel wide cinema resolution.
- Deal memo: the short contract confirming a crew member's rate, hours and terms.
- Debayering: converting single-colour photosite data into full RGB pixels.
- Depth of field: the distance between the nearest and farthest points acceptably sharp.
- Desqueeze: stretching an anamorphic image back to its true proportions in post.
- Diffraction: softening as the effective aperture narrows, pronounced on macro and probe lenses.
- Diffusion filter: a filter that lifts and glows highlights and softens contrast, such as the Black Pro-Mist.
- Director of photography (DP, DOP): the head of camera and lighting, responsible for the image.
- DIT (digital imaging technician): the crew member who secures, checks and looks after the footage and the on-set look.
- Dolly grip: the grip who pushes the dolly or crane and hits marks in time with the operator.
- Dolly zoom: a dolly and an opposite zoom together, holding subject size while the background stretches.
- Dual base ISO: a sensor with two analogue gain circuits, each giving a clean native sensitivity.
- Dutch angle: a tilted horizon, used for disorientation.
- Dynamic range: the span in stops between the darkest usable tone and clipping.
- Eighths of a page: the unit schedules use to count screenplay length, one page being about a minute.
- ENG (electronic news gathering): the television news camera tradition and its conventions.
- ETTR (expose to the right): exposing as bright as possible without clipping, for cleaner shadows.
- EVF (electronic viewfinder): a small screen viewfinder on the camera body.
- Exposure index (EI): a rating that moves middle grey within the recorded range without changing analogue gain.
- Exposure ramping: changing exposure gradually through a time lapse, for the day-to-night "holy grail" shot.
- Eyeline: the direction and height of a subject's look relative to the lens.
- f-number: focal length divided by entrance pupil diameter; each full stop halves the light.
- False colour: a monitor mode that paints brightness ranges in colour bands.
- First assistant camera (1st AC): the focus puller, who also owns lens changes and the camera build.
- First assistant director (1st AD): runs the floor, the schedule and safety.
- Flange focal distance: the distance from the lens mount to the sensor, which decides which lenses adapt to which bodies.
- Focus breathing: the change in framing when a lens racks focus.
- Focus peaking: coloured outlines on the sharpest edges of a monitor image.
- Focus stacking: merging frames focused at different distances into one sharp still.
- Focus throw: the rotation of a focus ring from closest focus to infinity, about 270 to 300 degrees on cinema lenses.
- Focus-by-wire: an electronic focus ring with no hard stops, so marks cannot be repeated.
- fps (frames per second): the capture or playback rate.
- FPV drone: a first-person-view drone flown with goggles, agile enough to fly through interiors.
- Frame grab: a still pulled from motion footage.
- Frame lines: monitor overlays marking each deliverable's crop.
- FWICE: the Federation of Western India Cine Employees, the umbrella of Mumbai craft unions.
- Gaffer: the chief lighting technician who executes the DP's lighting plan.
- Gamut: the range of colours an encoding can describe.
- Genlock: locking several cameras to one sync signal.
- Gimbal: a motorised stabiliser that keeps the camera level while it travels.
- Global shutter: a sensor that reads every row at once, avoiding rolling shutter skew.
- Golden hour: the hour after sunrise and before sunset, when the sun is low and warm.
- Greeking: covering or replacing an uncleared brand or logo in frame.
- Grey card: an 18 percent reflectance card used for white balance and mid-grey placement.
- Hacking: pulling a watch's crown to stop the seconds hand, for continuity across takes.
- Halation: a glow bleeding from highlights into surrounding tones.
- Handles: extra seconds rolled before and after the action for the editor.
- HDRI: a 360 degree high dynamic range image of a location, used to light CG elements.
- Headroom: the space between the top of the head and the frame edge.
- Histogram: a graph of pixel counts at each brightness.
- HLG (hybrid log-gamma): a broadcast HDR format.
- HMU: hair and make-up.
- Hyperfocal distance: the focus distance beyond which everything to infinity is acceptably sharp.
- Image circle: the diameter of the sharp, unvignetted image a lens projects.
- Insert: a detail shot cut into a scene.
- Intra-frame: compression that encodes each frame on its own.
- IRE: the unit of the waveform's 0 to 100 percent scale.
- IRND: a neutral density filter that also attenuates infrared, preventing colour casts in blacks.
- Jharokha: an enclosed overhanging balcony window of Rajasthani architecture.
- Jib: an arm that raises or lowers the camera through space.
- Jump cut: a cut between near-identical framings that reads as a skip.
- Kandura, ghutra and agal: the Emirati man's full-length robe, the headcloth, and the black cord that holds it.
- Key grip: the head of the grip department, responsible for rigging, camera support and shaping light without touching the lamp.
- Kicker: a light from behind and to the side that edges the form.
- Lead room: space left in the direction a subject faces or moves.
- Lens mount: the bayonet or lock joining lens to body; PL, LPL, EF, E, L-mount, RF and Z are the common standards.
- Lightmen: Mumbai electricians supplied with a lighting vendor's package.
- Line producer: the producer who runs the budget line by line and the daily cost control.
- Log profile: a logarithmic encoding that fits a sensor's dynamic range into a 10 or 12 bit file.
- Long-GOP: compression that stores a full frame followed by differences, efficient and heavy to edit.
- Look test: talent photographed in make-up and wardrobe under the planned light before the shoot.
- LTO (Linear Tape-Open): the magnetic tape standard used for long-term media archive.
- LUT (lookup table): a table converting one set of colour values to another; a viewing LUT shows log as Rec.709, a show LUT carries the film's look.
- Macro lens: a lens that focuses to 1:1 magnification or closer.
- Master shot: a wide shot that plays a whole scene.
- Matte box: the hood in front of the lens that holds filters and flags stray light.
- Midday break: the Gulf's summer ban on outdoor work in direct sun, 15 June to 15 September.
- Motion control: a computer-driven rig that repeats a camera move exactly.
- Motivated movement: camera movement caused by something in the scene.
- Mumps: faces widening at close focus on front-anamorphic lenses.
- Native ISO: the ISO giving full dynamic range with no added gain.
- ND filter: a neutral density filter that cuts light evenly; each 0.3 of density is one stop.
- NDA (non-disclosure agreement): a contract binding crew to secrecy about unreleased product.
- Negative fill: black surfaces that take light away from one side of the subject.
- NOC (no-objection certificate): written consent from an owner or authority, such as a housing society or the Animal Welfare Board of India.
- Online: conforming the edit to the full-quality originals.
- OOH (out-of-home): billboards and site posters.
- Open gate: recording the whole sensor area, usually close to 3:2.
- Oversampling: capturing above delivery resolution and downscaling.
- PAL: the 25 fps analogue colour television standard of India, the Gulf and Europe.
- Pan and tilt: horizontal and vertical rotation of the camera on a fixed point.
- Parallax: the shift between near and far objects as the camera moves.
- Pencil, hold and challenge: an informal booking interest, a reservation of dates, and another production's request for the same dates that forces a confirm or release.
- Pheras and baraat: the circling of the sacred fire at a Hindu wedding, and the groom's procession.
- Photoboard: a storyboard made from photographs.
- Photosite: one light-sensitive cell of a sensor.
- Plate: footage shot to be composited.
- Playable direction: a direction that gives the actor a thought or task to play.
- Polariser: a filter that cuts reflections from non-metallic surfaces and darkens sky.
- PPM book: the document for the pre-production meeting, where client, agency and production sign off every decision.
- Pre-light: lighting and rigging a set the day before the shoot.
- Previs (previsualisation): a rough moving version of the film made before the shoot.
- Prime lens and zoom lens: a lens of fixed focal length, and one of variable focal length.
- Probe lens: a long, thin macro lens that places the viewpoint inside a scene.
- Process trailer and low-loader: low trailers carrying the picture car, so talent can act while a driver tows.
- Proxies: lightweight copies of the footage for editing.
- QC (quality control): checking exposure, focus and files against a standard.
- Rack focus: shifting focus between subjects within a shot.
- RAW: sensor data recorded before debayering, with white balance and ISO as adjustable metadata.
- Rec.709: the ITU-R BT.709 standard for HD and SDR video, its gamut and transfer, displayed at gamma 2.4 for grading.
- Recce: a reconnaissance visit to a location before the shoot.
- Rehoused lens: vintage stills glass in a cinema barrel.
- Remote pilot certificate (RPC): India's drone pilot qualification issued through DGCA-authorised training organisations.
- RERA and Trakheesi: India's state real estate regulators' project registration, and Dubai's real estate advertising permit.
- Residual current device (RCD): a safety switch that cuts power when current leaks to earth.
- Result direction: asking an actor for an outcome (a look, a smile) in place of a playable thought or action.
- RGB parade: three waveforms side by side, one per colour channel.
- Rider: the contract's list of a talent's on-set requirements.
- Risk assessment: the 1st AD's written list of a shoot's hazards and their controls.
- Rolling shutter: row-by-row sensor readout that skews fast motion.
- Room tone: about 30 seconds of a location's ambient sound recorded for the edit.
- Run and gun: shooting reactively with minimal kit and no lit setups.
- Scheimpflug principle: tilting the lens relative to the sensor angles the plane of focus.
- Script supervisor: the crew member tracking continuity, timings and take notes for the editor.
- SDR (standard dynamic range): the conventional display range that Rec.709 describes.
- Sensor Overdrive: the Alexa 35 Xtreme mode that reaches 660 fps at 11 stops of range.
- Shift, unit and spot boys: the Mumbai 12 hour working day, the whole crew, and the crew who supply tea, water, chairs and umbrellas.
- Short lighting: a key on the side of the face turned away from camera.
- Shot list: the setup-by-setup document of size, movement, lens, duration and frame rate.
- Shot ratio: footage shot divided by footage used.
- Shutter angle: exposure time as a fraction of the frame interval; 180 degrees is half.
- Sindoor: the red powder in a married Hindu woman's hair parting.
- Skin tone line: the vectorscope line, at about 123 degrees, near which skin of every complexion falls.
- Slate: the clapperboard identifying scene, shot and take, and marking sync.
- Slugline: a screenplay scene heading in capitals.
- Speed ramp: a change of playback speed within a shot.
- SRT and RTMP: protocols for sending a live video feed over the internet.
- Staging in depth: placing action in foreground, middle ground and background of one frame.
- Stand-in: a person of similar height and colouring who holds an actor's marks while the set is lit.
- Steadicam: a body-worn arm and sled that isolates the camera from footsteps.
- Stills window: time given to the photographer on a motion setup's light and talent.
- Stop: a doubling or halving of light.
- Storyboard: the film drawn as numbered frames matched to the shot list.
- Stripboard: a schedule of one colour-coded strip per scene.
- Sunny 16 rule: in full sun, correct exposure is f/16 at a shutter of one over the ISO.
- Superwhite: signal above 100 percent on the waveform, up to 109 in legal-range video.
- Sweep: a curved tabletop backdrop with no visible horizon.
- T-stop: an f-number corrected for the lens's measured light transmission.
- Tech scout: heads of department walking the chosen location to plan rigging, power and lenses, after the recce.
- Technocrane: a telescoping camera crane whose arm extends during the shot.
- Tilt-shift: a lens that shifts to correct verticals and tilts to angle the plane of focus.
- Time lapse and hyperlapse: frames captured at an interval to compress time; a hyperlapse also moves the camera between frames.
- Toe: the bottom of the tone curve, where shadows crush into noise.
- Treatment: the director's written and visual proposal for a film.
- TVC (television commercial): an advertisement made for broadcast.
- UIN (Unique Identification Number): the registration number every drone in India carries from Digital Sky.
- Unit base and holding area: where the trucks, catering and crew base sit, and where cast wait.
- Variable ND: two polarisers rotated against each other to give adjustable density.
- Vectorscope: a display of hue and saturation.
- Veiling glare: a milky lift of the blacks when light strikes a lens's front element.
- VFX and CG: visual effects, and computer-generated imagery.
- Video assist: the monitor feed to video village and the recording of takes for playback.
- Video village: the monitor position where the director's team, agency and client watch takes.
- Waveform: a display of brightness by horizontal position in the frame.
- White balance and tint: the colour temperature rendered as neutral, in kelvin, and the green to magenta axis.
- Wild lines: dialogue recorded without picture, for the edit.
- Witness marks: distance engravings read from the side of a lens.
- xxHash64 and MD5: checksum algorithms for verifying copies; xxHash64 is the faster.
- Zebras: stripes in the viewfinder over areas above a set brightness.
