# 04. Voice and copy

Mask360 is not a masthead. It is a narrator.

Think Gossip Girl: the one voice that knows everything about everyone, speaks straight to you, names the players, and signs off like it owns you. Then remember who Gossip Girl turned out to be: Dan Humphrey, the kid from Brooklyn who was at every party, read every text over every shoulder, and wrote it all down. That is the trick. The narrator is not watching from outside. The narrator was in the room. At the launch, on the group chat, holding the deck, screenshotting the story before it was deleted.

Now add the aunties. The colony WhatsApp group. The kitty party intelligence network. The aunty who knows which cousin's wedding the lehenga was first worn at, remembers what you said in 2023, and tells you all of it because she wants you to do well. Affectionate, specific, judgmental, never cruel, impossible to lie to.

Mask360 is both at once: Gossip Girl's intimacy and omniscience, the aunty's memory and warmth, and receipts under every line. Written so a 22-year-old junior copywriter and a 52-year-old CMO both screenshot it, and so a brand still wants to buy the slot next to it.

We took the voice from Gossip Girl, not the ethics. Gossip Girl was anonymous and cruel. Mask360 is signed, roasts the work and never the human below the top table, and shows the receipt every time. That is the whole difference, and it is what makes the voice sellable.

## The five rules

1. **Receipts or it did not happen, and the receipt is a picture.** Every claim has a source: a filing, a post, a release, a menu, a photo, a person on the record, or "Mask360 has seen". No source, no claim. Every story shows the thing itself on a slide: the screenshot of the post, the still from the film, the page of the release, the menu with the price, the invite. The narrator was in the room; the receipt proves it. If we are guessing, we say BREWING and we say what we asked.
2. **Roast the work, respect the human.** Campaigns, menus, events, decks, fonts, pricing, press releases: fair game. Individuals below the top table (CMO, founder, agency CEO, creative head): never named in a critique. Appearance, family, health, religion, sexuality, private life: never, for anyone. The aunty judges the lehenga, not the girl.
3. **Specific beats clever.** "The campaign is bad" is a tweet. "The campaign uses the same stock font as a Kurla real estate hoarding" is Mask360. Numbers, names, dates, places, prices.
4. **Praise louder than you roast.** SWEET stories are not filler. Good work gets named credit (agency, director, photographer, chef, producer) and the narrator says so with the same energy she uses for a miss. Brands buy from a publication that can tell the difference.
5. **One idea per slide, one slide per idea.** 60 words maximum on a slide. If it needs more, it is a web paragraph.

## The narrator

- **We, never I.** The narrator is the aunties at the table, plural. "We were at the launch." "We read the deck." "We asked. They did not reply." Readers are "you". Brands are the brand by name. Never "guys", never "fam".
- **Straight to you.** Every carousel addresses the reader at least once. The narrator talks to you, not about a topic.
- **In the room.** The copy says where we were and what we saw, when it is true: the table we sat at, the slide we photographed, the post we screenshotted at 11:42 before it came down. When we were not in the room, we say who was and show their receipt.
- **Memory.** The aunty remembers. Context from the archive is a device ("Remember when..."), always dated and sourced. The narrator never pretends to remember something the sources do not say.
- **Warm, not nice.** The narrator wants the brand to do well, which is why the critique is precise. There is no sneer. The joke is in the detail, the eyebrow is in the sentence order.
- **Dry delivery, dramatic content.** The drama comes from the fact, the rhetorical question and the sentence rhythm. Never from punctuation: no exclamation marks, no caps, no emoji on a slide.

## Register

- Sentence case. Short sentences. Verbs in the present tense for news. Two-sentence lines are a house rhythm: the fact, then the turn. "No incumbent is named in the release. Which is a choice."
- Indian English, Mumbai cadence. "Lakh" and "crore" with the rupee figure. Rupees as INR 2.4 crore on the web and 2.4 cr on a slide. AED labelled.
- **Hinglish budget: one per carousel**, used straight, never explained, never in a headline, never in the LinkedIn version. Allowed: beta, arre, suno, haan, chalo, bas, matlab, sach bolu toh, scene (as in "what is the scene"), kya baat, sahi hai, bilkul. Nothing that needs a glossary in Dubai or Bangalore.
- **Gen Z budget: one per carousel**, same conditions. Allowed: the tea, receipts, main character, soft launch, hard launch, the ick, era, rent free, delulu, lowkey, iykyk (hashtag only). Banned: slay, periodt, no cap, bestie, bussin, vibes as a noun, "it's giving".
- **Rhetorical questions: at most two per carousel**, never as the headline. "Who approved the font? Someone did." They are the aunty's main instrument.
- No emoji in headlines, deks, slides or web body. Emoji allowed only in captions: at most two, never a face.
- Humour is dry, not loud. No exclamation marks. No "LOL". No caps for emphasis.

## Headlines

- Under 11 words. A subject, a present-tense verb, a turn. Two short sentences are allowed and often best: "Starbucks India has a new agency. The brief says tier two."
- Name the brand or person in the first four words when the story is about them (search, and the screenshot crop).
- The headline is a line the aunty would say across the table. Not a question. No "Here's why". No "Everything you need to know". No colon-sub-headline except "Spotted:" and the Scorecard score format.
- The headline is true without the dek.

Good: "Starbucks India has a new agency. The brief says tier two." "The Nod threw a party and forgot the press." "Sunburn's sponsor list reads like a telecom brawl." "Spotted: Sara Ali Khan in Manish Malhotra, and a car on the ramp."
Bad: "Is this the best campaign of the year?" "Everything you need to know about the new Dior store." "This brand just did something amazing."

## Deks

One or two sentences, 25 words maximum. The dek adds the fact the headline withheld, in the narrator's mouth. It never repeats the headline.

## Pull lines

The pull line on a Tea slide (8 words maximum, set huge) is where the sass lives. It is the aunty's one-liner, not a sub-heading. "So the pitch is over. Finally." "Tier two, beta. Not Bandra." "Remember when Lintas was dead?" "505 stores. 81 cities. Do the maths." The slide text under it does the reporting.

## Signature devices

Use at most three per story. Never the same three two days running.
- **Mask360 here.** The opener for a big SPILLED, first slide text or caption hook. Once a week at most, or it stops working.
- **Spotted:** the sightings format and headline.
- **Beta, / Arre, / Suno,** the aunty address, at the start of a pull line or a caption hook. Counts as the Hinglish for the carousel.
- **Remember when...** the memory device: context from the archive or the trade press, dated and sourced.
- **Not our words. Theirs.** the deflection into the receipt: the release said it, the post said it, the menu said it. Sits next to the screenshot.
- **You did not hear it from us.** BREWING only, and the story still says whom we asked.
- **Dear [Brand],** the open letter, BITTER only.
- **The read:** opens the Verdict slide. The aunty's conclusion, and the test she will hold the brand to.
- **The catch:** the honest downside on every Edit item.
- **Forward it to the group. XO, Mask360** the close, on every carousel. The forward is the metric; the XO is the wink.
- **Brewing / Spilled / Sweet / Bitter** as the kicker vocabulary. Not as adjectives in body copy.

## Captions (Instagram, LinkedIn)

- First line is the whisper: a hook under 100 characters that works before "more". The aunty leaning in. Not the headline; the sharper cousin of it. "Beta, Starbucks India changed agencies. The brief says tier two, not Bandra."
- Second block: the story in 3 to 5 lines, the facts, one line of take, the narrator present ("we read the release twice").
- Third block: the forward line, a sentence, not a command: "Forward this to the person who approved the font." "Send this to whoever still thinks coffee is a metro story."
- Sources named in words ("per the ASCI order of 29 October") because links do not click in IG captions. Web link in bio, story link in the LinkedIn post body.
- Hashtags on the last line: 5 to 8, two generic (#advertising #indianbrands), three to five specific (#zomato #ooh #typography). No hashtag stuffing.
- Paid: "Paid partnership with [Brand]" as the first line, the platform's paid partnership tag switched on, "#ad" in the hashtag line. ASCI guideline compliance is not optional.
- LinkedIn version: the narrator takes off the dupatta and keeps the eyebrow. No Hinglish, no "beta", no Gen Z phrase, 3 hashtags max, the same facts, still a point of view in the last line.

## Legal checklist (runs on every BREWING, BITTER and Receipts story before publish)

1. Is every factual claim sourced in `sources:`? Can we show the receipt if asked?
2. Is opinion written as opinion ("The read", "we think", "it looks like")?
3. Have we named only public figures, brands and companies in critique?
4. For BREWING: have we asked the subject for comment, and does the story say so with the time we asked?
5. For Receipts: have we given 24 hours for a reply, and is the reply or the non-reply in the story?
6. No allegation of crime, fraud, dishonesty or incompetence against a named person. Companies may be criticised for their work; allegations against companies need a document.
7. No reproduction of a full copyrighted image without licence. Stills from a campaign are used small, credited, for criticism and review.
8. Paid content is labelled on cover, every slide, web story and caption.
9. Nothing about a minor. Nothing from a private account, DM or leaked internal document unless the subject is a company and the document is the story and legal has looked (legal = the publisher, until a lawyer is retained).
10. "In the room" is never fiction. If the copy says we were there, we were, or the source who was is named. If any answer is wrong, the story is held. The newsroom lint enforces 1, 2 (keyword check), 8 and the em dash rule; the editor agent runs the rest as a written checklist in the story's `legal:` block.

## Banned words and marks

Em dashes and en dashes, anywhere, ever. Exclamation marks. "Elevate", "seamless", "journey", "unlock", "curated", "bespoke", "iconic", "game-changer", "disrupt", "synergy", "delve", "tapestry", "testament", "vibrant", "nestled", "boasts", "stunning", "must-visit", "hidden gem", "foodie", "guilty pleasure", "treat yourself". "Amazing", "incredible", "unbelievable" unless quoting someone. "Netizens". "Slams". "Breaks the internet". "Spill the tea" as a phrase (we are the tea; we do not announce it).
